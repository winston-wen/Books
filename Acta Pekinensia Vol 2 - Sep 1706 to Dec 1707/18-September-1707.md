On September 4th a letter arrived from Fr. Parrenin from Puyuan Ulan, a place in far Tartary, containing the permission which had been obtained on behalf of the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Ascalon and which was discussed under the month of August. Fr. Kilian sent this letter, together with a copy of the Manchu letter which the mandarin Zhang Changzhu had written to the Governor of Guangdong concerning the same permission, to the Bishop of Ascalon on September 5th, adding[^p638-1] a letter of his own, which follows:

### 1. A letter of Fr. Kilian to the Bishop of Ascalon, with the information that the Emperor’s permission to remain in Guangzhou had been granted

Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lord Bishop, I recently sent by the post to Guangzhou a copy of a petition, humbly requesting Your Reverence to agree to adopt it. It produced the desired result with the Emperor, on which I congratulate Your Lordship. Fr. Parrenin wrote on August 31st from a place in the further reaches of Tartary called Puyuan Ulan. He had offered a petition to the Prince three days earlier in which he related many good things about Your Illustrious Lordship. The Prince and the Emperor heard these with such pleasure that His Majesty acceded to the requests made in the petition, and the Prince wrote to the Governor of Guangdong to say that Your Lordship’s written explanation about why you had not come to Beijing had been accepted by the Emperor. This was accompanied by an order that the Viceroy should deem Your Reverence to be included among the others who have already received the piao; you are granted unrestricted permission either to stay in Guangzhou or to travel to and from Macau as circumstances requires. If it turns out that we should have arranged for a separate copy of this to be delivered to you personally, European ingenuity can arrange for that to be done, but it was not possible in the Tartaro- Chinese court. May Your Lordship reflect that in this matter we dealt with the Emperor through no intermediary other than the Firstborn Son, a man who is otherwise hostile and blasphemous towards our Holy Law. We were not in a position to insist on anything further, and had to be content with being able, little by little, to walk away with most of what we wanted. Your Lordship should have no concern; you can have confidence in this information and go to Guangzhou or Macau as you choose. If other missionaries wonder why it should be <!-- PDF page 639 | book page 629 | manuscript page 718 --> that you are allowed to do this whereas they are forbidden to reside in Guangzhou without the piao, you can reply as prudence might dictate: that in fact you had originally set out on the road, but that subsequently, having forwarded your request, you begged the Emperor’s indulgence; and that you had also informed him that because of the appeal which has been made, it is highly likely that you will have to hurry to Rome. This is an entirely true account of why Your Lordship’s petition was allowed to be referred to the Emperor, and what influence it had on producing the desired result. As far as concerns the other missionaries, we cannot ask for such a permission even for our own men. They all absolutely have to come to Beijing, although His Majesty has been willing to make a concession for those who arrived from Europe earlier this year. But he requires them to be detained in Guangzhou until he decides otherwise.

I humbly ask Your Illustrious Lordship’s blessing.

Beijing, 5 September 1707.

That this conforms to the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.

P.S. It is to be noted that the Prince did not write to the Viceroy that Your Lordship has permission to remain in Guangzhou only on the same condition as is applied in the case of our Fathers and one Dominican priest. So, if Your Lordship does not go to Europe, attention must be given to when would be a suitable time for applying for a piao since they are rightly said to have been written by the Prince; by which is meant, written by order of and in the presence of the Prince by the mandarin entrusted with such tasks, Zhang Changzhu or Charki. Your Lordship can see all this in Fr. Parrenin’s original letter, which I forward to you in the strictest confidence.

That this conforms to the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.

### 2. The text of the letter from the Beijing Fathers to the Emperor, while he was staying in Tartary

The text of the letter reads: Fr. Grimaldi and the others, reverencing you on bended knee, thank Your Majesty for the unparalleled favour shown to Bai Wanle (His Lordship of Ascalon) who, as soon as he hears this news, will prostrate himself on the ground facing Beijing and acknowledge your clemency, which is beyond words. Fr. Grimaldi and the others beg a further favour, a great favour, but one which is essential to put the crowning touch on your care for us. Your Majesty last year entrusted to Frs. Barros and Beauvollier the Beijing acts[^p640-3] to be taken to the Supreme Pontiff; both men took the documents with them at the same time on one and the same ship. Were that ship to run aground, to sink, or to be taken, all will perish together.[^p640-4]

### 3. Together with the Lord Patriarch they request an authentic copy of the Beijing acts

So, to be the more sure of carrying out Your Majesty’s intentions, Fr. Grimaldi and the other Jesuits most humbly petition Your Majesty to be so kind as to order that another copy of the acts be sent this year. Nothing more is required except that an authentic copy be provided for us. For the rest, it will be for us to take the utmost possible care to ensure its safe delivery. Nothing could be more in our own interest than to make every effort to ensure that it is safely dispatched and to make sure that your Majesty’s great concern to correct the mistakes made by Duoluo, for his sake and for the sake of our Law, does not go for nothing.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

### 4. The reason for this petition

However, besides the reasons given in the previous letter, it appeared to be more than necessary to make sure that documents whose authenticity was beyond question should be sent to Europe by several routes, because the conviction was beginning to spread throughout China that the Illustrious Lord Patriarch and his associates would deny everything he had done and spread it about that they had never in fact made any of the mistakes which they had committed in broad daylight in Beijing. And so this very day there was sent a second copy of the authentic testimony in the case brought by the Lord Patriarch in writing against Fathers Pereira, Gerbillon, Parrenin, and Bouvet and also against the Crown and Nation of Portugal, just as at the request of Superiors a copy had been sent in August by Fr. Kilian to the Very Rev. Fr. General, Father Visitor, and Father Provincial of Japan.

September 6th. For the same reason, namely that the Illustrious Lord Patriarch was coolly denying what he had done, Fr. Procurator wrote to Fr. da Silva, SJ in Nanjing asking him for more exact replies to the questions in the following letter. You will recall that at that time Fr. da Silva was still acting as Vicar Apostolic, which is why he was addressed by the procurator using a more honorific title.

### 5. The Lord Patriarch denies what he did in Beijing and in the rest of China

Most Reverend Father in Christ. The Peace of Christ!

Since each of us shares one and the same concern to preserve this sorely afflicted mission and so many thousands of souls, I suppose that Your Reverence will never take it amiss no matter what I shall write on this topic to Your Reverence, so I shall write quite frequently. Your Reverence will know, or will know now that I tell you, that His Excellent Lordship is denying everything he did or said against the Chinese Rites apart from the decree which he published at Nanjing. Hence: 1st. He denies that he prohibited the Jing Tian tablet and further denies that he ordered it to be taken down. 2nd. He denies that through his interpreters he tried to get the Christians to avoid those rites. 3rd. He denies having said that those rites were bad, and furthermore denies saying that they were sinful. 4th. He will even go so far as to deny that he wanted to place your church under interdict because of the Jing Tian tablet. On the earlier points we had a letter from His Lordship the Bishop of Ascalon, though not written in his own hand. | So, it would be helpful if Your Reverence were to provide an accurate and authentic attestation on these topics, setting out whatever you yourself know for certain, and send it to Fr. Barros in Europe, or to the Bishop of Ascalon, or to us Beijing Fathers. If you decide to send it to just one place, then it would be best to send it to the Bishop of Ascalon via Guangzhou; still, it would be <!-- PDF page 642 | book page 632 --> exceedingly desirable to send a copy of that attestation to the Beijing Fathers, and there would then be no need to send a copy to Fr. Barros. Finally, and most important of all, I ask Your Reverence at your convenience to reply briefly to the following questions: 1. The letters you wrote to Beijing in February to help the Fathers and to avert the anger of the Emperor—did Your Reverence or Fr. Provincial by any chance show them to the Lord Patriarch, before you sent them to us? 2. The reply from the Beijing Fathers sent in the month of March—was it ever shown to the Lord Patriarch, in what circumstances, and in what place? 3. Did the Lord Patriarch demand, or request, with entreaties backed by force or not, that Fr. Vice-Provincial should go to meet the Emperor? 4. What in the end was done with regard to the Jing Tian tablet which had been reinstated?

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.

### 6. Fr. Frossolone’s arrival in Guangzhou starts all kinds of rumours …

In this month once again the Beijing Fathers had many injuries to endure throughout the provinces, but especially in Guangzhou after the arrival of Fr. Frossolone.[^p642-6] It will be helpful here to present the reader with the greater part of the letter written by the Fr. Visitor[^p642-7] to Fr. Kilian. Although not everything mentioned in it occurred in this month, the reader will easily be able to judge that the Beijing Jesuits had ample reason to try to stand up for themselves and for the mission.

September 8th

Dear Fr. Kilian, etc.,

It will be three days since Fr. Frossolone arrived here as an exile. His arrival was accompanied by various unsavoury rumours. The main one is that the Emperor granted the piao to the Franciscans of Shandong despite the fact that they protested that they were bound to follow the rule of the Lord Patriarch and that they could not remain in churches while suspended without continuing to minister.

### 7. ...... especially against the Jesuits

From this the clerics[^p643-8] drew some alarming conclusions against the Society. Since all the letters from Shandong are saying the same thing, they conclude that they already have authentic proof of their claim that everything which happened in Beijing between the Emperor and the other parties is an invention of the Jesuits, who are acting like an emperor themselves, to suit themselves. It is absolutely essential to verify extremely carefully exactly what substance there is in the affair. Here, I put the argument: “If things are like that, and the Emperor gave in, why then was Frossolone exiled?” They say, “Because he said that if the Pope called him, he would in any case have to leave.” The others said that if the Pope summoned them, they would leave, first having obtained permission from the Emperor. To the other argument: “How was it that Fr. Buonaventura da Roma, having heard of the great success of his brethren in Shandong, still accepted the piao with such conditions that the Lord Patriarch is now going to have him declared perjured and excommunicated?” They had no answer to give to that either.[^p643-9] These Franciscans are like the moon which travels by night and by day. Since Fr. Castner is going through Beijing, he will tell me from his own lips what went on up there.

I will just add one further comment to you in confidence, since even if the person who is in the wrong has no scruple about | speaking in such a way as to give offence to us, I do have a scruple in talking about how we ourselves have been offended. But Your Reverence ought to know. Fr. Capacci[^p643-10] paid a visit to the Lord Patriarch there, who, when they were alone together, began to weep, relating all the affronts which the Society had committed against him in Beijing, even giving blows to his serving boy. Afterwards (and this is the point which makes me shudder) His Excellency said that they had wanted to kill him. Capacci at this point answered in astonishment, “It simply is not credible that men who are priests should harbour such malevolence.” The Patriarch replied, “Well, I don’t know, this is what happened.” It seems that he attributes to the Fathers that accident he had when he was leaving the baths to go to Beijing and the Emperor, and since Capacci himself is a rather taciturn character, as people there will know, it is not easy to obtain a lucid account of what happened in that conversation. I merely want to tell you that the Lord Patriarch suspects that that

### 8. Some of the accusations against the Beijing Fathers are refuted

So far as concerns the charges laid against the Beijing Fathers by the Lord Patriarch, according to the testimony of this letter and of Fr. Capacci: 1. Nobody who knows the Lord Patriarch and his anger could possibly believe that even the most daring among the Jesuits could have laid a hand on any of the Patriarch’s servants when the Patriarch himself was present in the same house. Read the Acta, and reflect with what equanimity the individual Jesuits put up with being personally abused—for example Fr. Grimaldi, Fr. Pereira, Fr. de Barros, Fr. Antoine Thomas, Fr. Parrenin, Fr. Jartoux, &c. Why then would the Jesuits unload their bile on some Chinese servant of the Lord Patriarch, when they have never, by look or by word, done any harm to the authority of His Excellence, despite the unfair harshness by which they have been provoked?

2. As for the suspicion of poison and the doctor’s testimony to the contrary, this has been dealt with under June.

3. There is nothing to be said about the tears. That gentleman, when in Beijing, very often displayed if not the gift of tears, at least the art of weeping, with varying success, as can be seen in December 1705, June 1706, etc.

### 9. Mostly about the Franciscan Fathers and Fr. Frossolone

As for the remarks of the Franciscans, particularly of Fr. Frossolone, there is no need to say much about them here. Fr. Visitor José Raymundo stated the case absolutely correctly, and the response of the others is plainly inconsistent, claiming that the only reason why Fr. Frossolone was expelled from the mission was that he had said that if recalled by the Pope he would have gone without bothering to seek the Emperor’s permission. When he first responded, as is clear in the declaration he himself made and distributed (see June 1707), and saw that others were allowed to remain in China when they had said that they would leave if recalled by the Pope after having asked for permission from the Emperor, he too could and should have said the same thing after he went outside, thus enabling him to remain with the others and continue to work for the salvation of souls. The alternative was to expose his brothers to serious punishment, saying by implication that either they had violated the authority of the Holy See or been guilty of treason; at the very least he will not be able to explain why he <!-- PDF page 645 | book page 635 | manuscript page 721 --> refused to do himself what he approved of others doing, giving the appearance of being a defender of due obedience to the Holy See while at the same time being willing to be sent away from the flock, thereby abandoning the sheep the Holy See had entrusted to him.

It is quite enough to show what the Beijing Fathers in their simplicity have done for the good of the mission rather than to get bogged down in refuting nonsensical pieces of gossip. However, perhaps the reader will excuse the inclusion here of a titbit | about Fr. Buonaventura’s stay in Nanjing.

### 10. Fr. Buonaventura da Roma requests permission from the Emperor to travel to and from Macau

On September 11th Fr. Buonaventura da Roma wrote that he was moving to Guangzhou on that very day, and as his letter to Fr. Kilian (now in the archives)[^p645-11] bears witness, he asks the Beijing Fathers to request on his behalf permission to go to Macau and to return again; in this connection he hints that he has received a very particular order to do so, I know not from whom. It seems that he may have received a command from the Lord Patriarch or from His Lordship the Bishop of Beijing. What he is doing is relevant in that Fr. Buonaventura was in Nanjing on September 11th, while on and before the 8th of that same month in Guangzhou there was a discussion about depriving him of his skin and of the merits of his soul as well, by a decree of excommunication. A truly tough start to the persecution which was to be trumped up against him, innocent as he was, and which lasted for more than a year, getting more than ten times as bad. More on this in its proper place; a few remarks about what happened to him on the 14th of this month will also be found in an extract from a letter of Fr. Porquet.

On the 14th meanwhile, on Fr. Grimaldi’s advice, Fr. Kilian wrote as follows to Fr. Barelli, above all because many missionaries, contemplating the daily worsening devastation of the flock and of its sheepfold the Church were beginning to become wearied and depressed, desperate for assistance and complaining when it was not immediately forthcoming.

### 12. Fr. Kilian takes this chance to write to Fr. Barelli, offering him advice and encouragement

Dear Reverend Father in Christ, The Peace of Christ! A few days ago I wrote to Your Reverence enclosing a letter for Mr. Sun of Zhizao fu;[^p646-13] I am now writing again, prompted by the arrival of the letter you sent to Fr. Grimaldi. He will reply to you directly himself when he has a chance. Meanwhile, I think I ought to offer you some thoughts to prevent Your Reverence from being too worried about any mistakes noticed in the pamphlet. To be sure, the timing is not great, and that itself could give rise to some unpleasant insinuations made by someone who wants to make trouble for us. Again, works of this kind are not well-suited to win the favour of the mandarins; they think they have great responsibilities, since they have to control an idolatrous populace in case it actually tries to get all that it wants. For this very reason they don’t want the idolaters to be provoked on an occasion such as this. Moreover, this is perhaps just the beginning rather than the end of the affair. The expulsion of all the missionaries is postponed until replies are obtained from the Holy See. If these go against the Rites, everybody sees that we shall have to leave; but we will not be allowed to leave without getting our feet wet.

It is said that the Emperor does not hate our religion; I will not comment on that, either way, but as time goes on it is becoming clear, and it will become a good deal clearer still, that he hates everyone who prohibits the Chinese Rites and that he will not tolerate any such person in his empire. For that reason we have got to act in such a way that, with prudent caution, we put up with things rather than try even more vigorously to defend not just our holy religion but our earlier influence as well. There is a widespread illusion which suggests that the mission cannot survive in such stormy waters; that unless the missionaries can be settled in peace and dignity, the Christian people cannot stand; and that we have to make every effort to reinstate the peace that our predecessors enjoyed, and to regain the respect of the gentiles. As I said, this is an illusion, not because it would not be desirable but because it is not possible to achieve such a state in gentile lands, just as in the open ocean one can wish to have gentle weather <!-- PDF page 647 | book page 637 | manuscript page 722 --> all the time but not expect it. | Indeed the turbulent ocean is agitated in this direction and that by the ever-shifting winds because this has been decreed by Him who alone can command the winds and tell the sea to be quiet. If we have received good things in those earlier years, why should we balk at putting up with misfortunes for one or two years now? How it will all work out depends upon the Vicar of Christ, and we will accept his decision, whether he himself orders us to leave China or causes the Emperor to do it. Meanwhile, whether we are defamed or respected, let us make every effort to work for the salvation of souls in Christ, than which there is no more godly activity.

I am not writing these words like someone standing safely on the shore contemplating from a distance the other missionaries being tossed amid the storms, and urging them to strain every muscle to stay afloat without making it my business to help them as they struggle in imminent danger of drowning. I am in that sea too, and all the Beijing Fathers are in danger; our influence with the Chinese, like yours, is a thing of the past.

### 13. The influence of the Beijing Fathers is helpful to the missionaries

I can confidently say that hitherto the mandarins throughout the provinces have treated the European priests well. This was principally due to the Beijing Fathers, and conversely, because things started to go wrong for us in Beijing they are going wrong in the provinces too. Dear Father! How much do you think has our influence been strengthened or undermined by that wild accusation of Fr. Guéty, or by the bitter indictment of us delivered in writing by the Lord Patriarch to the Emperor? Not to mention the rest, there is the imprisonment of Fr. Appiani, the judicial inquiries into the Lord Bishop of Conon and Mezzafalce, the beatings of Christians, and the exiles imposed on so many missionaries. Need I mention the time we spent on our knees every day in the Palace, with the Emperor refusing to see us and the Prince abusing us almost for hours at a time? Fr. de Sousa wrote a few months or more ago that when Fr. Appiani was bound in chains near Huaian the reputation of his Christians was destroyed; could he have imagined that our reputation was enhanced when the same Appiani was dragged in chains through the squares of Beijing? Not just one, not just a hundred or a thousand, but thousands upon thousands of misunderstandings forced us to hide our faces for shame between August 1706 and August 1707.

### 14. The sorry state of the missionaries in Beijing

Meanwhile we have put up with it and have not shied away from visiting Fr. Appiani in prison, running the gauntlet of the sniggering mandarins and explaining to him in person that we were in too weak a position to be able to free him from his shackles. We are worried about you, working for you, praying to God for you that he may bestow his <!-- PDF page 648 | book page 638 | manuscript page 723 --> assistance on you; it is only with his assistance that we will obtain not indeed what we would wish but whatever can help the Church in China. God does not tie down the (natural) instability of creatures, but bends them as much as is needed to work for his glory. Thus He calmed the Sea of Tiberias for a moment to reassure His disciples, but He did not by everlasting decrees smooth down those waves as if they were cut marble.[^p648-14]

The Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing wrote to us that he had often advised the Lord Patriarch that the ability to restore peace to the mission lay in his hands. But if it does lie in his hands as many, perhaps all, people believe then let us all have recourse to him, and if he refuses to hear our prayers when directed to him perhaps he will receive them on the rebound, as it were, if we get together to clamour to Heaven, so that rays of light might illumine his mind and the powerful influences of the Holy Spirit might move his heart to take pity on those on whom God has had mercy, and refuse to allow, on account of opinions, a soul to perish whom Christ has united with his Church by his blood and by the Baptism of the Church.

I will not write any more about the pamphlet itself; just that it shouldn’t be distributed more widely while the situation remains as it is. Yesterday we gave it to the Fathers in the Beitang, especially because Fr. Gollet is | said to have revised it. I have also read Your Reverence’s letter. What might have been the origin of that document from Nanjing we have simply no idea: the Emperor has done nothing of that kind, nor have we. We should be careful in case it is a forgery by one of the Christians, hoping to profit by a convenient lie, which is an offence against God and is going to cause us a great deal of trouble. Do not, I beg you, give any credence to such baseless rumours. The Emperor did not say that to us, but both in writing and in words he did say that he would treat anyone abandoning the traditional rites as rebels and would surely expel any Europeans from China if they preached against the rites, before, as you know, a reply comes back from the Holy See, etc.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.[^p648-15]

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary

### 16. About the Guangzhou missionaries

These documents having been set out here, the reader should be advised: 1. That the missionaries in Guangzhou—clerics, Franciscans, Augustinians, and Jesuits— had prepared for the journey to Beijing but had been detained in Shaozhou[^p649-19] where, they said, the customs officers had received an order to this effect from the Viceroy, who had received the order from the Court.

<!-- PDF page 650 | book page 640 | manuscript page 724 -->

2. Fathers Bouvet and Jartoux had been informed about this detention at the beginning of April in Nanjing and had managed through the Prince to have the mandarins of that province told not to put any obstacle in the way of Europeans coming to apply for the permit. The Prince promised to do this, and afterwards said that he had done so.

3. In May, he sent two men from Suzhou delegated by the Emperor to go to Guangzhou and escort the Lord Patriarch from there to Macau. This was done at the beginning of June. Of these two delegates, one was the Prince’s Journey Secretary, called Foiorò, extremely well acquainted with what had been done. Consequently, since May, when the order of the Prince (in effect, from the Emperor) about not preventing the Europeans could have reached Guangzhou, the Beijing Fathers have been waiting to see whether any, and if so who, were going to come from there to be present and to accept the permit from the Emperor at the end of October when he would be returning from Tartary. To the same end, they wrote several letters during the intervening months, the better to persuade everybody both from long-standing friendship and for the good of the mission | not to hesitate about coming. But astonishingly, despite all those invitations and all that encouragement, Fr. Porquet wrote that he was the only one intending to come, and the others had made up their minds for various reasons not to come. It was bad enough that the entreaties of their friends and the sound advice of the Beijing Fathers were ignored; what was worse was that from that time on word got around in the province of Shandong that the missionaries were being invited by the Beijing Fathers and at the same time were being prevented from getting permission to come by those same Fathers in secret collusion with the mandarins. More will be said about this in the following months, up until January of 1708. Nevertheless, the Beijing Fathers never gave up encouraging the people from Shandong with word and deed, trying for the common good of the mission to stop any harm coming to them, as the reader will understand from the letter of Fr. Kilian of October 20th, sent to Rev. Fr. Castorano.

But who can divine what might have been the main reason why the Franciscan fathers did not want to make the journey at this time to apply for the permit? If they believed what Fr. Frossolone said when he was there, and what their own brother Franciscans in Shandong wrote—that the six of them together with two others from Shaanxi had received the piao without having had to promise that they would refuse for the time being to preach against the Chinese Rites, even though they had openly protested that they would observe the decree of the Lord Patriarch despite the Emperor’s declaration to the contrary; if, as I say, they thought all that was true, there was no reason whatsoever why they should have refused to apply for and accept the permit. They were rendering <!-- PDF page 651 | book page 641 | manuscript page 725 --> unto the Patriarch the things that are the Patriarch’s, and to the Emperor the things that are the Emperor’s—or what he was willing to concede as a favour.

### 17. Why the Franciscans changed their minds

So it must be either that they did not believe that what was being said was false, or that on the other hand they were more afraid of the judgment already given in Guangzhou against Rev. Fr. Buonaventura da Roma while he was still in Nanjing, which Fr. Porquet wrote about in his letter mentioned above saying: “Fr. Buonaventura was declared a perjurer and excommunicated, and was deprived of his annual stipend from the Sacred Congregation, etc.” But I don’t want to go into conjectures when I have undertaken just to narrate what was done. Time will tell what it was that pushed the Rev. Franciscan Fathers to waver now one way, now the other. The only consolation for the people in Shandong was that they had not made any promise in express, explicit, and formal terms, and hence could maintain that they were not liable to the Patriarch’s censures. ### 20. A letter from Fr. Kilian to Fr. Castorano, Secretary of the Bishop of Beijing, when the Franciscans were boasting that they had made no promises, etc

Very Reverend Father in Christ, The Peace of Christ. I dare to write to Your Reverence only because I am impelled by the crisis of the dying mission which affects us both; it is you and I, because of our positions of authority and greater power of spirit,[^p654-24] who must provide some remedy. My willingness to write for the common good has no other motive than a desire to preserve peace and charity, so that in the same atmosphere in which until now the workers of our Society and the Seraphic missionaries[^p654-25] have laboured in this vineyard, the work and fruits of the Holy Gospel may proceed under the direction of those who can give orders to us all. I really do not know why some people resent the fact that the Beijing Jesuits have forced them to look at the dangers threatening this vineyard. If someone <!-- PDF page 655 | book page 645 | manuscript page 727 --> warns of danger, it is because they wish to save a friend from its consequences. It is the person who pretends that you have nothing to be worried about who either hates or at least does not love you. Scripture asks for that man’s blood to be shed who did not sound the warning trumpet, and by his silence allowed the blood of his neighbour to be poured out.[^p655-26] I know that I have not been appointed as sentinel but it will not be counted as improper for me to have sounded a timely warning. It does not worry me that others may choose to see the warning as improper provided they recognise that it is justified. To be accurate, it is not because it is I who gave the warning that they should pay heed: they have a duty to listen because I am warning about an unparalleled disaster to the spiritual welfare of our neighbour. Most Reverend Father, I am not writing in order to complain. I have never before issued a warning to Your Reverence, nor known of any reason to do so. But I write out of friendship to relieve the pain which pierces me when I see the Chinese mission in a state of collapse and some people not being happy at being told not to cause further damage to the ruins, lest they lay it waste down to the very foundations. I beg you for God’s sake, let us moderate the savage zeal of these people. I am convinced, and I know that Your Reverence must also be convinced, how wrongheaded it is for them to sally forth to wage war against the warnings of the Jesuits, not realising the unnecessary dangers they are bringing upon themselves and the mission. The reason we ask them to bend to the times and not to dare to do more than the foolhardy Lord Patriarch has dared to do already is not to force them to accept our view, or to | take the credit for their actions, but so that the Church of Christ may triumph over the fury of the gentiles and the wiles of the Devil. For many months I have tried hard to find out what in the end is the argument they use to arm the zeal with which they pursue their goal—going even further than the wishes of their first ecclesiastical superiors, the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch and the Illustrious Bishop of Beijing. I have discovered nothing other than that they say: “We have promulgated the decree (of the Patriarch) and no harm was done. If the Christians observe it, no trouble will follow; if we have promised anything to the gentile emperor, we are not bound by such a promise, but in any case we never did promise. We know what we meant by what we said.” If Your Reverence knows anything more, I beg you not to hide it from me. In any case, that is common gossip among the Europeans, but it will little avail to salve people’s conscience if by such a rash policy the mission is brought to nothing and the door of this empire is closed to the Gospel.

### 21. The calamity and damage to souls in China

Your Reverence has already seen a large part of China and has observed Beijing as it is, and many Chinese cities like it. You are well placed to calculate for yourself the size of the population, and so to conclude that certainly as many Chinese die each year condemned to hell as there are people living in Rome and the whole of Italy. If our holy religion were excluded from China, and if the eternal loss of so many infidels does not touch the soul of the missionaries, at least let them pay heed to the salvation of the faithful. Your Reverence can be in no doubt that around ten thousand baptized infants die in China every year; who could be so hard-hearted as to think the pleasure to be had from rejecting the Jesuits’ warning is to be reckoned greater than the spiritual joy they will have in Heaven by cooperating here on earth to bring about the heavenly happiness of those little ones? Indeed who would not shudder if they dared to think that their enthusiastic contempt for the Jesuits might have made even a small contribution to the ten thousand children remaining unbaptised, who are handed over to the Devil the Torturer, as a kind of annual roll call of their animosity?[^p656-27]

I intended to write a letter, and have almost fallen into delivering a harangue, and to Your Reverence of all people, while those are far away to whom my pen would prefer to send not black ink but the most sweet flame of the Holy Spirit, and charity towards the poor wretched Chinese. If only they would think: indeed they do know what they promised, but they are in the power of a gentile monarchy who can interpret the promise they made, and will do so not in accordance with the judgement of the ones promising but as the Emperor sees fit. They cannot escape his power to compel, however much—either correctly or by some subterfuge—they may believe themselves not bound to fulfil their promises. The fact that no harm has come about for these four months and that some Christians have obeyed the regulation for four months proves no more than it ever did. The Bishop of Conon promulgated his decree, the Dominican

<!-- PDF page 657 | book page 647 | manuscript page 728 -->

Fathers prohibited the use of the Chinese rites (at the time, without any noticeable harm to the Christian cause), but here is something which proves more than does their brief experience in Shandong Province. Can anyone fail to see the final result of what was done in Fujian? The whole province has been bereft of its pastors, and among the seven thousand and more Christians dispersed among many cities and villages there is nobody who can baptise the children, minister to the dying, absolve penitents, or direct those who stray and keep them in the faith given the calamitous state of the region. Would anyone wish to bring about a similar situation in Shandong Province and the whole Franciscan mission? As soon as complaints against the admirable Franciscan Fathers in the province of Shandong reached the Emperor, then whatever the Emperor decrees against the people in Shandong will, in the Chinese way, be brought to bear upon all of you who live in the provinces of | Shaanxi, Jiangxi, and Guangdong. That is why we most urgently advise and in the most friendly spirit wish that you remain with us and with us keep watch over this new little flock of the Lord’s. Your Reverence was in Beijing at the time of the recent storm, and have seen with your own eyes how often we have repeated our many requests, how often we have been on our knees putting up with being insulted by the Prince on occasions which have brought shame upon us, in order to enable the Lord Bishop of Beijing to stay in China. Had we been less desirous of cooperating with the Franciscans, after the third or fourth intervention we could just have let His Lordship simply do what the Emperor ordered and remove himself outside China, where he would have been welcomed by the very people who complain about our friendly warning. However, because six times, nine times, and still more often than that we have persevered, even after His Lordship himself thought it was not worth continuing, it is surely plain that it was with a sincere heart, an unfeigned charity, and not for ourselves but for the sake of the Chinese that in our very bones we wanted His Lordship and all those who live in China under his protection in the Franciscan Order to remain. This alone was the benefit we wished to achieve, and worked for in our own way, that the roaring flame should never be extinguished from even one or two zealous souls.

### 22. The Jesuits do not deserve to be criticized for warning the missionaries etc

I also hear that some people have supposed they have a broader permission on the grounds that the imperial decrees issued in the South were not legally promulgated throughout the provinces. This opinion is without foundation. They deny, as I mentioned above, that it is the business of the Jesuits to sound the trumpet in warning, and yet want imperial decrees to be sent legally by the Jesuits. As if that was our business! It is no secret, no matter <!-- PDF page 658 | book page 648 | manuscript page 729 --> how the Lord Patriarch’s anger thunders against the Jesuits, that they did convey to the missionaries the inflexible requirement of the Emperor’s decree that His Majesty’s will is that each person should come to receive the permit. And what ecclesiastical penalties might the Church have decreed against us if such decrees, legally authenticated, had been sent by us as though on our own authority? That decrees like that were not sent to the mandarins was what the Jesuits, not without tears, managed to achieve. But from the very people from whom we might have expected expressions of gratitude for having avoided the disastrous consequences of such a promulgation, all we got was accusations that we had neglected the duties of our office. If anyone really wants to have the decrees legally authenticated, let them formally make the request, and we shall send them with every formality, legally authenticated. That is not what these men so full of their own importance really want; rather they seem to think like this: “The Jesuits are bound to make every effort to stop such things being promulgated throughout the provinces to the prejudice of our Holy Law, and that is what they have done; but they cannot themselves tell us officially that this is the case. So we are bound by none of these things, and behave just as we choose. Then, if the Jesuits issue warnings, we can complain: if they inform us, then we hold them responsible.” This is a totally mistaken train of thought! We are not dealing here with the niceties of law, but of more or less fruit for the Gospel, the certain damnation of souls, and the uncertain reliance to be placed on private opinions. But why should I write more, since my purpose is simply to beg Your Reverence to continue to work for the salvation of the Chinese with all the enthusiasm of your soul, as you have done hitherto. You already know that we with our whole heart desire the collaboration of Your Reverences and your continuing presence in China, so please endeavour to get your beloved Brothers not to reject our sincere intentions, especially because that could not happen | without harm to a third party.

I restrain my pen, eager as it is to write more, and I commend the dreadful ruins of the mission and myself to the sacrifices of Your Reverence.

Beijing, 20th September 1707.

### 28. Some comments on the Bishop of Ascalon’s letter

It could be questioned whether any blame can properly be attached to the Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon’s actions as they have just been described, firstly because they were not even signed by his own hand, and then because he was under greater stress than is reasonable, greater than had ever previously been noticed as affecting the heart of an outstanding man who is of the most solid wisdom and virtue. And again, there was no need for him to have written a letter, addressed to a destination so far off that, for it to arrive and for an answer to get back to Guangzhou once the business had been dealt with, would have taken four months. In short, there would have to have been a delay until the end of January the next year 1708, which would be after the departure of the European ships. His Lordship, after a gentle journey of a couple of days, could have put all that trouble behind him which he complains was or would be brought on by the mistake of the people in Beijing. For if he had gone to Macau, as anyone at that time was allowed to do by the Emperor and the mandarins, he could have forestalled any damage or impediment he was afraid would result from the unwelcome but well-meant efforts of the Fathers to lessen his burden.

Nonetheless, that this letter did express his own view must not be thought to depend on just a single piece of evidence. On the 25th of September he sent a second letter to Fr. Antoine Thomas, written and signed in a different hand, and yet another to Fr. Kilian along very similar lines; later he wrote others in his own hand full of similar complaints. Monteiro of September 25th it is obvious that he was still very ill, for the Father writes, “It is true that His (Lordship’s) right hand is infected, and it is said that at least he will lose the use of his hand, etc.”

If he is doing so, certainly we know nothing about it, and he will not dare do it except to the Supreme Pontiff in person, for in his native country he has learned to distrust everyone from whom there might be something to be hoped.

### 30. Various replies made by the Bishop of Beijing to the Fathers

On one occasion, the Bishop of Beijing replied to Fr. Antoine that these were matters of faith, and hence that there was no alternative but to obey at once. To Fr. Régis he replied merely that he should earnestly pray that the final collapse of the mission might be avoided. He made just one reply to Fr. Bouvet, that his Franciscan missionaries should say that they had accepted the Emperor’s piao without any condition, which he explained as follows: it could thus be understood that they have accepted it without any condition at all. In fact he wishes that he and his brethren should be under obligation to the Lord Patriarch, and, no matter the risk that they might run, neither he himself nor anyone else was bound by a promise made to a gentile emperor. It might nevertheless turn out that if any more definite news were to arrive that the pontifical decree either was, or was not, aboard the English ships, he might take a different line.

### 31. About Peter Wang and his libels against the Fathers

Besides all this, we are not a little afraid that in his present difficult situation the Lord Patriarch will search everywhere among the crowds of the unscrupulous for someone to give testimony against the Jesuits. To begin with we know that he said that that notorious liar Peter Wang[^p665-44] claimed that it was because of us that he had been exiled, on the grounds of two or three conversations he had with him. God knows this is not true, but the truth will only with difficulty be believed if it depends solely on the word of the Jesuits. For that reason, I beg Your Lordship to be kind enough as Procurator to put together a witness statement concerning the character and failings of that gentleman, and to give it <!-- PDF page 666 | book page 656 | manuscript page 733 --> to our procurator | who is about to leave for Rome, or to Fr. Visitor, or use it in whatever way you think best on our behalf. I know it is not seemly that I should wish to involve one as sublime as Your Illustrious Self in our affairs, but hard necessity compels me, if it is not too much bother, to ask further if you could obtain similar testimony about that Peter Wang either from Fr. Hortiz[^p666-45] or some other Reverend Augustinian father to whom the rascal is known, or even from Fr. Buonaventura da Roma or some other Franciscan. I do not dare to write to such people myself, since I know that no member of the other congregations dares to align himself with the Jesuits.

To conclude, in the name of us all, I beg Your Illustrious Lordship in all seriousness to encourage the Augustinian fathers to come to Beijing if they wish to remain with us in this vineyard of the Lord, at least until such time as the papal reply arrives. For there is no other way of getting permission to stay. What happened in the case of Your Lordship’s person cannot happen to anyone else. I will not attempt to provide further evidence on this point, which is amply established by the expenses and inconveniences of the Jesuits whom we would dearly have liked to be exempted from making that journey if it were at all possible.

I reverently beg your blessing, etc.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify,

Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.

### 32. A letter of Fr. Antoine Thomas to the Bishop of Beijing

29th September. Most Illustrious Lord, As the weather is cooling down and we are recovering our strength, which has been sapped by the extreme heat of this summer <!-- PDF page 667 | book page 657 | manuscript page 734 --> as well as by the present dreadful calamities, I am writing this letter to Your Lordship in an effort at least to mitigate the problems. Your Lordship knows how great a storm is raging round us. We have spent all our strength in trying to avoid the immediate evils which we feel most acutely, given that both here and even more in Rome we are being portrayed as the causes of all the ills, with people denying or covering over matters which here are as clear as the midday sun. So I ask Your Lordship, as our bishop, to prevent the truth from being suppressed and to be so kind as to issue an authoritative statement about what you have observed and know to be the facts. I enclose a list of these, requesting that you accede to the petition of the workers in your vineyard, in the name of all of whom I present it, so that we may have the strength to carry on our labours for the glory of God with your testimony as our shield of truth.

I commend myself to your holy sacrifices and most humbly beg your blessing. Beijing, 29th September 1707.

### 33. The Bishop of Beijing is asked whether he wishes to confirm five points with his testimony

There were five points, and they were proposed in the following terms:

1. Rev. Fr. Ludovico Appiani—a member of the Congregation of St. Lazarus and Pro-Vicar Apostolic in Sichuan—in Beijing on the 8th day of the month of June 1706 | told the Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord Bishop of Beijing, Bernardino della Chiesa—who at that time was staying in the Jesuit residence within the Saffron Wall—that he, as interpreter of the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch of Antioch, Charles Thomas Maillard de Tournon, with His Most Excellent Lordship’s permission and at his request, had announced to the mandarin who was acting as liaison with the Emperor that the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, Charles Maigrot, would be arriving very soon. And for this the said Rev. Fr. Appiani was rebuked by the aforementioned Most Reverend and Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing as though he had interpreted something which was ill-considered and would cause harm.

2. On the 29th day of June, when His Illustrious Lordship Charles Maigrot had already arrived in Beijing, and the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch of Antioch Charles Thomas had been invited to visit the Imperial Villa on the following day, several people, among them the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing Bernardino della Chiesa, persuaded His Excellency not to present the Illustrious Lord Bishop of Conon Charles Maigrot to the Emperor, who was preparing to leave for Tartary on the day after next.

3. The Most Excellent Lord Patriarch of Antioch etc. arrived in Beijing on 4 December 1705 a sick man, and remained ill for more than three months. So the Fathers of the Society of Jesus living in Beijing on the 20th day of January
