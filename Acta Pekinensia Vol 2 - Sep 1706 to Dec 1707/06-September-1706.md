### 1. Journey of the Lord Patriarch when he left Beijing

The Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord Patriarch had departed on the 28th of August, while His Majesty was staying in Tartary not far from the Great Wall, keeping with him there for his service the Rev. Fr. François Guéty, Fr. Tomás Pereira, Fr. Antonio Barros, and Br. Domenico Paramino of our Society.[^p115-1] The Lord Patriarch made the journey slowly by water and at length, on the 4th of October, with his companion, the Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing,[^p115-2] disembarked at Linqing, after spending altogether 37 days on the journey which on other occasions would normally be completed within a week. From the house of the aforementioned Most Illustrious Lord and the said city, the Lord Patriarch departed on the 8th of October according to the letters of the Lord Bishop of Beijing, which have been given to us. Meanwhile, however, from Nanjing, already the Reverend Abbate di San Giorgio[^p115-3] had hurried to meet him at the town of Dezhou,[^p115-4] and thence, with the same most excellent Lord he at once resumed the journey. We have no definite information concerning the attempted excursion of this reverend <!-- PDF page 116 | book page 106 --> gentleman to Beijing, except that on August 17th he wrote from Nanjing to Father Superior François Gerbillon[^p116-5] to say that he was hastening from there to the Court, and that he was carrying letters for His Excellency. On the same day, or certainly by the 18th, he had set out, as was noted in the letter of the Most Reverend Father da Silva, Vicar Apostolic of Nanjing.[^p116-6] Further, the Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing wrote to us saying that he gave as the reason for his arrival that on account of a certain disease troubling him, he had undertaken this journey to seek the advice of Doctor Borghese;[^p116-7] but that now there was no longer any need of him, since the severity of the disease had lessened on the way. This may easily be believed, since no prudent person would suppose that, having already traversed 270 leagues, during the 30 that remained he would disguise his illness, unless the troubling force of the disease had ceased.

### 2. How affairs were badly managed in Beijing with regard to the business of the Lord Patriarch. Blame and reproach is cast upon the Beijing Fathers

As I said, the Lord Patriarch was proceeding by very short stages. Meanwhile suspicion against the fathers in Beijing was greatly increasing. The irksomeness of the slow progress of the ship was causing sickness in the minds of those travelling in it; being sick in their minds through their inactivity, they recalled to memory more intensely not just what had happened, but considered in their hearts where mistakes had been made, what the mistakes were, and who had been responsible, and also by what means the mistakes could and should have been avoided. Since, moreover, an embittered heart, from the weakness inherent in human <!-- PDF page 117 | book page 107 | manuscript page 402 --> nature, tends to project its own errors onto others—just as those who are sick in the stomach project onto the food prepared by the cook the harm that they complain they are suffering—so the unfortunate way matters turned out was now, with full voice, attributed to the Jesuits at Beijing, as if, by a terrible crime, they had made themselves the instigators of all the things that were burdensome to the person of the Lord Patriarch, or prejudicial to the honour of the Pope or harmful to the Holy Christian Religion; or which they were afraid could happen to the Lord Bishop of Conon in the course of time.

And this is the reason why I undertook to write a great deal in this month, when scarcely anything has been transacted directly between the Fathers and the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch. He had written absolutely nothing to the Fathers, and they were totally ignorant as to where on land or water His Excellency might be. And it is necessary that, for the present, I use here a different journal style, so that neglecting the chronological order of days, I deal separately with the issues, assigning what is proper to each, lest a daily account of the succession of events separate too much the parts of the matters being considered, and thus cause obscurity, with long and inconvenient intervals bringing about confusion. And in this month it would be especially troublesome, since it was not now a matter of people conversing together in each other’s presence, but of exchanges of questions and answers between people distant from each other, with long intervals of time intervening.

### 3. Order of things to be narrated in turn

There are three things to be narrated in order. First, there are those matters that concern the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch, and at the same time relate to the Lord Bishop of Conon. Then there are those that refer directly to the person of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, and indirectly touch upon the Reverend Guéty. Lastly there are those proper to the Reverend Guéty, which also refer back | to the already mentioned Most Illustrious Lords and through them to the Beijing Fathers. These things are in fact being written in order to demonstrate, in due course, the innocence of these Fathers.

### 4. Various accusations directed against the Beijing Fathers

In order to speak with due reverence of the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch, one must know that many things were said and written by missionaries who met him on his ship during the journey to Linqing against the Jesuits residing in the Court. For example: firstly, that the Lord Patriarch had summoned to Beijing, from Ji’nan fu,[^p117-8] Father Miguel Fernández of the Order of St. Francis,[^p118-9] <!-- PDF page 118 | book page 108 --> by a letter written to this effect, which he entrusted to the Fathers of the Society to send. Secondly, that some of the Fathers have been detected as perjurers. Thirdly that letters to His Excellency sent from elsewhere were received by the Fathers and opened. Fourthly, that matters were conducted in such a way in Beijing that the Emperor was suspicious that the Lord Patriarch had been poisoned. Fifthly, that the Fathers in Tartary had stirred up His Majesty against the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch, the Lord Bishop of Conon and the Reverend Guéty, the catechist Jin Xiu,[^p118-10] etc. Sixthly, that the fathers had brought it about that the legation was revoked, and the gifts destined for the Holy See recalled from Guangzhou[^p118-11] to the Court etc.

### 5. Responses to each accusation

Response to the first: The Most Excellent Lord Patriarch during the nine months of his stay was accustomed to send his letters through the Reverend Appiani,[^p118-12] his interpreter, and the latter always sent them to the courier by his own servants. Why then would he have entrusted the Jesuits to send this particular one to the Reverend Father Fernández? There were then living in the same house the Illustrious Bishop of Beijing and his secretary the Reverend Father Frossoloni[^p118-13] through whom His Excellency was accustomed ordinarily to send his messages to the Reverend Franciscan Fathers. Why was it only Father Fernández that he wished to invite to Beijing through the Jesuits, consigning his letter to them? There is one argument supporting the view that the letter was entrusted to the Jesuits, namely that the Lord Patriarch is reported to have said this himself to Father Fernández. Some people then continue the syllogism: but the Lord Patriarch should not be suspected of lying; therefore … Let us suppose that the Lord Patriarch did say this; nevertheless the minor premise need not lead to such a definitive conclusion, for although we may not rashly presume that there was a known falsehood, we are not thereby prevented from presuming that there <!-- PDF page 119 | book page 109 | manuscript page 403 --> was an unconscious error in the memory of the Lord Patriarch, when reason and the circumstances of the case convince us that what is alleged to have been done was not done. An altogether similar error occurred in Beijing at the end of January, as has been stated in its proper place. Father Kilian suggested that a message by letter to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, which had just been dictated, if it was to be sent, should be sent quickly, since on the following day the seals would be closed throughout the whole of China and the public couriers would be taking a holiday. The Lord Patriarch replied that on the previous day he had entrusted that same message to the said Father to be sent; this, however, was shown to be clearly false by the Reverend Andrea Candela,[^p119-14] the Chancellor of the Lord Patriarch, by whose pen and hand this message had been prepared. This point does not require any very elaborate refutation, both because it is not of major importance by its very nature, and because everyone readily realises that the Fathers in Beijing could not, for any reason at all, have kept back the above-mentioned letter to Father Fernández, since in such an environment its suppression could easily be established. Moreover the Lord Patriarch could, without any trouble, make up for the loss of the suppressed letter by means of another; nor could the Fathers hope for even a scrap of advantage from this whole affair, whether the letter was delayed and hindered in delivery or expedited. Nevertheless Father Kilian diligently inquired of each and every one of the Fathers under his title of Procurator and Apostolic Notary, whether they had any knowledge of the above-mentioned letter? And all unanimously denied that in the course of nine whole months any letter or message or note, to be sent to Reverend Father Miguel Fernández, had been entrusted to any of them, whether in writing or personally; | and they requested that this testimony of theirs be written in the acts by the said Father Kilian.

In order to respond to the second accusation it is necessary to state more precisely who those Fathers were, what the issue in question was, and the manner in which they may have made themselves perjurers. This accusation against the Fathers is similar to that which Father Navarrete put forward in Rome in the proposition: Whenever a decision is taken in a Sacred Tribunal, the guilty party named should be brought to punishment. The prosecutor wrote with a laugh: “So they should.” If the Fathers had been accused as perjurers, would the Lord Patriarch have lacked authority to proceed against them for so grave a crime, when he proceeded against them with ecclesiastical penalties in a case of alleged calumny? Or did it seem better not to call to account in Beijing those accused of perjury by a report from afar, but rather, if the scandal still persisted, <!-- PDF page 120 | book page 110 --> to pursue the case against them, across so many seas and through so many years, in Rome, where they would never be able to appear? Those are unjust to the Lord Patriarch who frequently and frivolously say that it was enough for the Lord Patriarch to have heard one side of the case, and not to hear the Jesuits, so that he could accuse them in Rome; as if the Apostolic Legate had been sent to hunt for accusations. This is a most disgraceful kind of blather, by which, while they aim to strike at the Jesuits, they attack the Legate himself, asserting that he failed in the exercise of his office and authority, preferring to act as prosecutor rather than as judge.

### 6. The third accusation more fully explained

In the third instance there is some truth, which I will sincerely expound. Father Gerbillon had asked the Lord Patriarch, as he was going away, what should be done about letters, from any place, that might come in the meantime? Where should they be sent? The Most Excellent Lord Patriarch replied simply with these few words: they should be handed to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, who was remaining with us, and to whom he left the necessary instructions. About the beginning of September there arrived in Guangzhou an enormous parcel or sack of letters, torn open in more than one place. The courier handed this to the Father Superior, and the Father Superior to the Bishop of Conon. The Bishop of Conon said that the Lord Patriarch had given him the authority to open the paper sack, i.e. the outer covering, and take out his own letters, if any should be found there, but he was unwilling to avail himself of this authority: the Father should send the whole parcel after the Lord Patriarch to Linqing, or Jining zhou.[^p120-15] This was done. However the Father Superior J.-F. Gerbillon added a letter in his own hand, by which he testified in good faith that the parcel had been delivered to him by the postman in this torn condition, and that he sent it to the Lord Patriarch without damaging it any further. Finally in January of the year 1707, when the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing, accompanied by Reverend Father Castorano,[^p120-16] had come to the court to receive the imperial permit, we found out that the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch, having assembled the members of his household before himself and his chancellor, made a solemn enactment, in legal form, concerning the burst parcel of letters sent to him by direction of the Jesuits. I do not argue as to whether this enactment was in fully legal form, but I say that it was not useful, except to support destructive suspicions in the minds of those who did not know of the letter of Father Superior. That <!-- PDF page 121 | book page 111 | manuscript page 404 --> the sack had burst was obvious to those observing with their eyes, was affirmed by the testimony of the Bishop of Conon, and was acknowledged in the letter of the Father Superior, written in his own hand. What need, then, for this pretext of a further juridical act? For the accusation directed against the Jesuits to be shown to be true, it would be necessary to prove legally that the bag was broken by the work of the Jesuits, or by their advice, or with their consent; not simply to testify that the bag was damaged. But who will prove this? If it seems to be good reasoning to conclude that, because the broken bag was sent by the Jesuits, therefore it was broken by them, it will seem far better reasoning to conclude therefore it was not broken by them. For if they had breached it culpably, they would not, with almost childish or infantile simplicity, have sent it to him, since they had very recently had experience of being | under his eagle eye. Let whoever in this matter is making accusations against the Beijing Jesuits also boldly say whether any letter contained in the bag was opened, whether a seal was broken, and whether any were missing. But if no fault was detected in these matters, why do they hold fast to their view, loudly proclaiming that the wounds to the bag are like a homicide. It is a common and daily occurrence in China that the postal directors will not accept larger parcels in the post-box, but wrap them around with a covering, where another is necessary because of the continual motion of the horses. Experienced people in China and Europe know this, and so they are accustomed to protect their letters by binding many sheets of paper around them. Those versed in Chinese affairs easily understand what I have said in this matter; it is not worth the effort for me to examine the issue more closely, by way of excuse, simply for the sake of those not so expert.

### 7. Response to the accusation that poison was given to the Lord Patriarch

The fourth point suggests something more serious. For who would not be astonished that the daring of the Jesuits should have gone so far that it became quite public, causing the Emperor to suspect that poison had been administered? This accusation, so dreadful, was demolished by its proponents, since after a few months it became clear that what had been originally a badly fashioned calumny has been transformed by them into another which was apparent to everyone as a lie, so that even in its second version it evidently would not stand up. I will briefly relate what took place.

The Lord Patriarch had come to Beijing sick, of that same sickness which had forced him to take to his bed for many months, seriously ill, when he was on the coast of India. He had already been in residence in Beijing for nearly four months, and now it seemed almost irksome to the Emperor to keep on granting honours and favours, which at the court are otherwise bought at a high price, and never last for long. The household of the Lord Patriarch and the Most Illustrious <!-- PDF page 122 | book page 112 --> Bishop of Beijing who was there perceived this, and urged him to depart from Beijing by boat, before the dry season should arrive removing the waters favourable to enabling him to sail away peacefully. However, it was his wish to linger there. So the Emperor sent two Chinese doctors who felt his pulse, tested the disposition of his mind, and attempted to impart the hidden intention of His Majesty, which was to make use of remedies, if there were a need for them, or to be on his way if he did not need medication.

On this occasion, when the origin of the disease was being explored, the Emperor sought to discover through his doctors whether the Lord Patriarch was troubled by the suspicion that he had drunk poison. His Majesty wished to have his own testimony that there was no suspicion of poison. However this very curiosity of his in seeking to find this out was helpful to us, because the Lord Patriarch freely acknowledged that this disease had not begun recently in Beijing, but had broken out again on the journey because of the cold, just as it had originated in India from the same cause. But what is said, namely that the Emperor sought this testimony because he knew that the Fathers were hostile to the Lord Patriarch, or he was hostile to them, is without foundation, as may be read in our diary, and the acts of the Lord Patriarch himself. Nor will anything be found up to that time which might give occasion for so pernicious a suspicion; rather, the Lord Patriarch himself has been accustomed to say to his entourage not only in that month of September, but even today, that everything had proceeded well, in peace and charity, right up to May 17th. But that belongs to another day. Lest I be too long in explaining the material in this article, I refer the reader to July of the year 1707.[^p122-17]

The fifth and sixth points it is convenient to deal with here together, and that it may be evident what light and what darkness they contain, it is necessary that I add matters that I put off mentioning under August, in order to gather <!-- PDF page 123 | book page 113 | manuscript page 405 --> them together in an appendix; except where troublesome accusations brought against us during the Patriarch’s journey, should require that they be mentioned sooner. Since however these matters occurred not only in Beijing but also in Tartary, I faithfully report the letters of those responsible for them, as they were originally sent to Europe. However I do this with appropriate moderation | lest I bore the reader by going through all the letters of those at Beijing, since it is evident from the responses of others with what solicitude they laboured for the person of the Lord Patriarch, and for the dignity of the Holy Father. Further on I return to the matter.

On the 11th and 12th of August two extremely harsh decrees of the Emperor were made known to the Lord Patriarch by the mandarin Henkama,[^p123-18] concerning himself and the person of the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Conon. On the 13th the same mandarin informed His Majesty in what spirit the Most Excellent Lord had received them. The Emperor was by no means pleased on reading the words already cited for that day in the month of August. Indeed, he got even more angry. Concerning this Father Pereira at once urgently gave advice in his letter of the 16th of August to Father Kilian, as follows:

### 8. Anxiety of the Fathers regarding the anger of the Emperor against the Lord Patriarch

My Dear Father Kilian

The Emperor has been much scandalized by the high and mighty responses of the Holy Patriarch and said the following to us: “If all of you who have been in China a long time do not write down everything he has done here you will have wasted much effort for little result. I bude[^p123-19] (will not put up with it, or will not be content). And you bu zhen ren[^p123-20] (will not be men) making poor return for so many benefits and honours.” And, moreover, he (the Emperor) said that the Patriarch was out of his mind or did not understand what he was doing. And the Emperor went on to express surprise that Henkama had said nothing in reply when he claimed to have been misunderstood, that he had never come out with it, that he was just the same and stood accused with him and, he said: “You stand here as his accuser, so how do you observe the rule: ai ren ru ji,[^p123-21] love other men as <!-- PDF page 124 | book page 114 --> yourself?” I believe that (the Emperor) has ordered him to repeat other answers to his questions which have never before been said to or heard by an emperor of China. It seems that that this gentleman has forgotten how I disapproved of his actions since he said to me in the audience hall: “I am better than you at political manoeuvers yet I have received more blame than you.” Rehe, 19 August 1706.[^p124-22]

This same Father gave a more full explanation of his concerns when he wrote to Father Antoine Thomas,[^p124-23] the Vice-Rector, on the 18th of August from the aforementioned place in Tartary:[^p124-24]

I cannot get around since my head, (on account of daily and continuous headaches)[^p124-25] is still in the same condition. Zhao Laoye[^p124-26] is going to you and he will be a living letter for me. The Emperor, whose heart I hope God will direct, was scandalized by the replies given there by the Lord Patriarch. May God watch over us all so that we may follow the way of his Greater Glory, and may we with sincere humility humiliate ourselves beneath His Divine Power … The Emperor seems to imagine that there is something we have been concealing from him by our silence. Therefore we see clearly that the Finger of God is here.[^p124-27] If something happens to defend and bring justice to us send me orders to do whatever Your Reverence and the majority of the Fathers think best. I commend myself to God and his saints, and may we place ourselves under the protection of God. Give me your blessing.

Father Antonio de Barros confirmed what Father Pereira said when he wrote in a letter dated 17 August:

<!-- PDF page 125 | book page 115 | manuscript page 406 -->

On 15 August in the presence of the eldest Prince,[^p125-28] the mandarin Zhao, Father Pereira, and many members of the imperial household, the Emperor gave to the unfortunate remarks of the Lord Patriarch a very pejorative interpretation. He said to Father Pereira: If you do not write and publish to the whole world as clearly as possible the favours that were granted to Duoluo,[^p125-29] the unfortunate words he used in response, the things he did against you, after what you had done on his behalf in petitioning me that His Excellency be called to Beijing,[^p125-30] I will regard myself as badly served, and you as not men (in Chinese, wo bu fu, nimen bu zhen ren[^p125-31]). He said more to the effect that the Lord Patriarch had never understood what he was about, or whom he was attacking; but that Henkama | had replied very badly. Then he went on to say that the Lord Patriarch was the one who had blamed him …

This testimony of Father Barros does not exactly agree with that of Father Pereira; but, if one wishes to regard it as the same, let it be so but confirmed by double authority.

The letters we have recorded here came to Beijing through the same gate by which the mandarin Zhao arrived, sent by the Emperor, and appeared in the evening before the Lord Patriarch, namely 20 August. The Fathers at the Court presumed that it was already known to their companions in Tartary what the commands of His Majesty had been, and so Father Kilian, omitting discussion of the contents of the imperial commands and simply wishing to communicate their concerns, wrote to Father Pereira the following:

On the 20 August Zhao Laoye came in the evening at the fifth hour bringing the dreadful zhiyi[^p125-32] (commands) of the Emperor, and we all trembled at such news. Zhao Laoye now (as I am writing this) is again with the Lord Patriarch, reading to him the zhiyi a second time, and granting him a delay till the morning to deliberate about his response. We are afraid that this time we may be ruined, with our whole mission, since we know the stubborn disposition of the Lord Patriarch; if he does not give way, acknowledging some fault (at least in the Lord Bishop of Conon), what will the Emperor do with him? …

### 9. The Fathers staying in Tartary were ignorant of the Emperor’s commands to the Lord Patriarch

These things, and others similar, were written while the actual encounter was taking place between the mandarin and the Patriarch; since fear and anxiety from all sides was pressing down upon the Fathers, who easily discerned that the adamantine strength of the Emperor would rub against the unyielding disposition of the Lord Patriarch, to the detriment of our religion. Nevertheless it was difficult to find a solution to the problem, especially since the Fathers in Tartary did not know what exactly the imperial commands to the Lord Patriarch were; those in Beijing presumed that these commands were known to them, and hence omitted to explain them clearly, simply urging the Fathers in general to make efforts on behalf of the Illustrious Lord Patriarch. That the Fathers did not know the particulars of the Emperor’s commands can be inferred from their letters, not only those already sent, but also those that followed.[^p126-33] For on 20 August Father Pereira wrote in this way to Father Antoine Thomas, from Rehe in Tartary:[^p126-34]

As for the news that we have here, I have already written several times. Zhao Laoye left here with various demands and decisions that will have been revealed to Your Reverence where you are. Here we fear they may be still worse since the Lord Patriarch turns a deaf ear and pays no attention to the questions the Emperor has proposed to him. Here we are guessing, there Your Reverences will know more than we do. The Emperor makes many playful remarks about our Fathers going to Rome to let the whole world know how little regard the Lord Patriarch has for his explanations of the political[^p126-35] nature of the rites. He is greatly scandalized that the Europeans should ask him to teach them about them then to interpret them differently, being ignorant (as he says) of their institutions and principles.

The same appears even more clearly in a letter of Father Antonio de Barros of August 26th to Father Kilian which reads in part:

So far we can learn nothing of the reply the Emperor finally gave to the mandarin Zhao about the obstinacy of the Lord Patriarch. This causes us much distress since we cannot pay attention to what happens, and cannot take hold of opportunities to moderate by petitions and tears the anger of the Emperor which threatens to be extremely dangerous for the mission and all the Europeans. Today I will look for a courier, and we hope to find out what you can tell us from your end so we can come to some conclusion about what we should do. Then, once we know what the Lord Patriarch does with a mind to destroy us, then we should be able to act to prevent him destroying the mission.

But that the Fathers, even in this darkness, did not let slip any opportunity to speak well of the Lord Patriarch, and earnestly to promote his welfare, is seen in the account by Father | Pereira, from Karahoton,[^p127-37] sent to Father Kilian on 23 August. For the benefit of the reader I am happy to set this out in its entirety, even though only a few of the words are necessary for the present discussion:

In the evening of the twentieth the Emperor sent for me and for about two hours after he had made me sit down (on the ground) we had a very good conversation. He gave me an opening by asking questions and I discussed with him the creation of the world; the certainty of death and the uncertainty of the day, and it being once for all; the resurrection, judgement, and paradise and its details; the end of the world, the Flood and its cause; and reward for the good and punishment for the bad. The Emperor opened the way to all this by inquiring about miracles apparently on the strong assumption that they do happen, insisting (this time) that I tell him as much and more than he asked. I replied to all his questions as God helped me to. I observed that he paid close attention to everything I said, and remembered what I had told him on other occasions. May God make him as great in Heaven (by whatever means he chooses) as he has made him on earth. The reach of the hand of God is not short.[^p127-38] He <!-- PDF page 128 | book page 118 --> inquired about the angels and devils, souls and their immortality; of the punishment which God gives to evildoers even in this life and the reward to those who do good; and he asked if there were examples in our Holy Scriptures. I replied that God had cut off the descendants of many for their faults, and to others had granted large families of grandsons and great-grandsons for their good behaviour etc. His Majesty heard and approved of everything with the greatest amiability without saying anything. He inquired (with great exasperation with the Lord Patriarch) whether he would tell them in Europe what he had done here, or whether he would speak passionately. I replied that he would say what he judged convenient. The Emperor replied (suspecting that he would be incapable of speaking dispassionately): “And how will God treat him?” (In Chinese Tianzhu zenme yang,[^p128-39] or will not God punish him?) I replied, respecting the honour of the Lord Patriarch, as I have done many times in the past, even though the said Lord Patriarch has never been willing to believe me: My Lord, the guilt of men completely depends on the intentions with which they act in accordance with their knowledge[^p128-40] etc. and in the case of the Lord Patriarch who does good, and never gives way to his inclinations but to reason, he will not be held culpable in the sight of God.[^p128-41] The Emperor said nothing, knowing full well that the said Lord had made accusations against us and that he had been obliged to question, as Your Reverence knows, how that is compatible with love of neighbour and with His Lordship praising our modesty and patience. Karahoton, 23 August.

### 10. The Fathers work at making suggestions that might be helpful to the Lord Patriarch

From 20 August to the 25th of the same month, how harsh and how hostile the Lord Patriarch showed himself to the imperial commands is clear from the complaints which the Jesuits of Beijing frequently and more earnestly addressed at that time to their companions living in Tartary, asking that they should take steps to come to their aid since the Lord Patriarch was falling into major difficulties and the mission was heading towards disaster. In particular, Father Antoine Thomas and Father Kilian wrote in the name of all. However, for the sake of brevity I am omitting their letters, since what they wrote is clear enough from the replies, nor is there need to clear them of guilt, <!-- PDF page 129 | book page 119 | manuscript page 408 --> since it was not they who were accused, but those living in Tartary. So Father Pereira replied on 26 August to Father Kilian:

On August 23rd I received a letter from Your Reverence together with others. I am always pleased to get good news (about you being well) and about the other Fathers. Fr. Antoine Thomas recommended that we intercede with the Emperor on behalf of the Lord Patriarch to pardon him for everything. I and all the others have the same intention. But the said Lord continues as always in his replies to the Emperor. That only Our Lord God will judge him (this is what the Emperor thinks), that he will not depart with | any public demonstration of anger, that he has been kept in ignorance of these matters which until now have been kept secret out of respect for the Holy Pontiff, is entirely due to our intercession. It was this very reason that there came to me on the day already mentioned with tears in his eyes the eunuch Zhang Qilin.[^p129-42] He handed me a letter that had come from Your Reverence and requested that I translate it at once, as is usual. When Father Guéty found out, he felt obliged to come to hear what was said and done. When the eunuch saw his insolence he was greatly scandalized believing that he was plotting something harmful and he ordered him to leave. I objected and finally the eunuch made a gesture that he should remain. How can I undo by my tears what these gentlemen are doing their best to destroy with such determination? In the end, let us beg God that if they seek to destroy and ruin us, he will deign to take pity on those souls who bereft of pastors will go or be forced into the wilderness without any means of salvation. I am afraid that although the Emperor is capable of putting up with them to the end, nobody can assure me that the eunuch will not tell the Emperor about the actions I described of the reverend gentleman I referred to, nor that my tears no matter how copious could arouse the compassion of the Emperor as much as the decisions of the Lord Patriarch can annoy him. Who could have imagined such behaviour of a legate sent from the Holy Pontiff to bring about peace? Certainly it was in his hands: but the Enemy of Souls misses no opportunity for everything to be lost. The Emperor’s eldest son with his usual intelligence and seriousness asked me whether the Holy Pontiff was looking to seize China.[^p129-43] I replied, equally seriously, saying everything I could <!-- PDF page 130 | book page 120 | manuscript page 409 --> to dispel his error and suspicions which the Emperor has also touched on with me, but with greater sagacity than his son. All this the Lord Patriarch could have remedied if he tried but each time the suspicions were increased by the cool replies he gave the Emperor and the persistent way he continued in the design which he had brought with him from Europe, without listening to the Emperor, the legislator and living exponent of their political rites. This was something that we could never understand or try to understand, to do such violence to those who are Religious after such a clear exposition that the Emperor himself had personally[^p130-44] given him. Karahoton, 26 August 1706.

While the letter just quoted was on its way to Beijing, at the same time the final decree of the Emperor was communicated; in this it was commanded that the day appointed by the Lord Patriarch for his departure not be put off, as had been reported for August 27th. The Emperor had wished that the Fathers in Tartary remain ignorant of this fact. But it is certain this command was brought to Beijing on the 27th; therefore its dispatch from Tartary was at least on the evening of the 25th, or immediately after midnight on the 26th.

### 11. The Fathers entreat the Emperor on behalf of the Lord Patriarch

However, the Fathers knew nothing of it until 29 August, that is, the day after the Most Excellent Lord had left Beijing; then at last the Emperor wished that the threatening decree become known to them, since he did not doubt that it would already have had its effect. As to why the Emperor kept secret from the Fathers in Tartary that he was doing this, when it could not, of course, be kept secret from the other Fathers who were at the court, the reason is clear: because those who were far away could not so quickly make their entreaties, he was not willing to listen to those who were present, and so, as if making fun of them, after the execution of the decree, he advised them of it as if it were in the future, allowing them to make intercessions which would now be useless. Nevertheless in this matter the Fathers, during the time that they were kept in ignorance of the fact, with sincere hearts worked hard and long, as can be seen in the letter of Father Barros, of the 29th, to Father Kilian, which I quote immediately below. |

### 12. Letter of Father Antonio de Barros. Our men are anxious on behalf of the Lord Patriarch

Letter of Father Antonio de Barros to Father Kilian.

When on the 23rd of August I came to the audience I observed the Emperor acting in a way contrary to his usual caution, and resolved to take strong measures in this matter, although what exactly he would do we could not fathom. When on the 26th I next came to the court, through certain signs we concluded that the Emperor, fed up with the language of the Lord Patriarch, had issued a zhi[^p131-45] that the said Lord should depart at once and had sent a reply with some phrases that were not very pretty. It happened that this resolution was fortuitously delayed and gave us time to sound out everything that the Emperor had laid down in the zhi of the 26th. As it happened, on the 26th of August the Emperor sent the eunuch Zhang Shouling (he is the same as Zhang Qilin which is his name and shouling his office[^p131-46]) to ask Father Guéty what news he had, what he had to say, and what he thought about these matters. Father Guéty excused himself on grounds that he had received no news, that such matters were none of his business etc. The eunuch was indignant: 1. because (Father Guéty) had not knelt before a zhi of the Emperor; 2. because he had replied with hands crossed one over the other; 3. because in his way of speaking he showed little Da yang courtesy.[^p131-47] Your reverences should consider what the eunuch may have reported about this.

It was now night, and I was not present, nor Brother Paramino who was at the house of a sick person. Father Pereira who was actually present knelt before the eunuch, and succeeded through prudent charity in placating the eunuch and obtaining pardon for Father Guéty who was half dead with fright. Finally the eunuch asked Father Guéty if he had anything to say. He replied that he was in ill health, that he had come badly provided, that he wanted to return to Beijing before it got cold, and that there he would serve His Majesty in whatever capacity he wished. The eunuch agreed and referred everything to the Emperor, who flared up and ordered that the last resolution he had sent <!-- PDF page 132 | book page 122 --> to the Lord Patriarch should be held back.[^p132-48] The following day (the 27th) he ordered us to be called to his presence (with the exception of Father Guéty). He gave us a clear demonstration of his lack of patience, and of the indignation he held towards the Patriarch, Maigrot, Guéty, and the Chinese who were associated with them. He gave us a fearful written order that we should write to the Lord Patriarch informing him of what had happened. He said that he the Emperor, the Prince,[^p132-49] the daren men[^p132-50] (the grandees of the empire) and all the Court were angry with them and that he could do nothing less than order that some whose names he knew should have their heads cut off, and he named Jin Xiu (the Lord Patriarch’s catechist), Wang Jiao (or Wang Baiduoluo, the former procurator of the Lord Bishop of Rosalia in the Nianzhou business),[^p132-51] Gu Zhe (or Thomas Gu, Father Appiani’s spy),[^p132-52] and others he had heard of through the mandarins of Fujian, and many more through the men that the Da Wangye[^p132-53] (Prince) had for years employed as investigators, of whom one was a Christian who was assiduous in carrying out his duties for the Prince. Since this order was passed on to us by the eldest son together with Xiao Liang Laoye and Li Yu[^p133-55] (both eunuch chamberlains) we tried very hard to convince them, and finally at night we received a dispatch that put off this business until the audience of the 29th which is today. No written instruction from his own hands has been handed down since he has given no instructions for it to be put into writing. In this way we had an opportunity to see | the distasteful decision that the Emperor decided to send to the Lord Patriarch but has held back. Now I hope to find out what has happened today. The Emperor, seeing that the Lord Patriarch has emended his response and that his exhortations have inspired fear, has acquired some hope to see a change for the better. May God prosper our affairs.

From this letter it is clear that the Emperor wished to conceal from the Fathers in Tartary that final decree about not delaying the journey of the Lord Patriarch etc.; in the meantime he put before them other things, so that the minds of those who were upset might be occupied dealing with them and not attend to or perceive this point. For the stratagems of both kings and royal courts are more or less similar everywhere in the world, and rulers delight in concealing from others their hidden plans by disguising them under another form.

It will occur to someone to ask what relevance the Fathers in Tartary had to what might be done by those in Beijing: what could they do to prevent such serious troubles coming upon the Lord Patriarch? And by what means? If they were to send an express messenger, five days would be required; even <!-- PDF page 134 | book page 124 --> if the Fathers at Beijing had made use of the royal courier, nevertheless a letter could not reach Karahoton in Tartary before late in the evening of the 29th, while in the meantime only 18 hours would have elapsed between the communication of the royal command and the departure of the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch. Earlier, by the courier of the 23rd, many Fathers had written, seizing the opportunity—since the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch, having heard their entreaties and advice, had given a somewhat gentler response—and earnestly declared that they would do whatever was possible in his service. Moreover the Fathers in Tartary were also about to do this, as they previously had done as seen in the letters received in Beijing on 23rd August (about which see above) had not His Majesty, in the manner described, on the 26th and 27th of August, more serious evils having been brought to his attention, held fast to his purpose, rather than suspending the final command until 29th August, by which time some more appropriate reply might be hoped for.

### 13. The sixth accusation considered. (No. 4. above)

Up to this point matters have been related as it were in general; now those which concern the person of the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch are to be reported; they deal with the recall of the gifts.

From August 20th to the 28th inclusive, but especially on the 27th, the mandarin Zhao was saying repeatedly, at various times, sometimes from conjecture, sometimes as if from letters of His Majesty, that the embassy to the Holy See and the gifts already sent to Guangzhou had been recalled, or were to be recalled. This seemed to all to be a serious matter; indeed, it was scarcely believed. Father Angelita, secretary of His Excellency, earnestly, and in his own name, inquired through an interpreter of the mandarin Zhao whether this was truly the case? He replied that this was altogether the mind of the Emperor, but the decree concerning the revocation of the embassy and the gifts had not yet been composed. Hence this rumour seemed doubtful, and since at that time many quite definite misfortunes were weighing down upon us, we were labouring to avert those which were definite and present rather than those which were doubtful. Moreover the Lord Patriarch himself did not seem to be very agitated concerning this threatening situation; although he could not but be deeply grieved if, through the revocation of the gifts, the embassy that had been sent should come to ruin; this embassy being the only thing that was believed to be able to provide some consolation in the midst of calamities.

In the meantime the Lord Patriarch departed, and when His Majesty received the news of his departure he immediately decreed that the further action he had determined should be taken. First, he decided to recall the gifts, <!-- PDF page 135 | book page 125 | manuscript page 411 --> although he reluctantly proceeded to the execution of this resolution, as he would have preferred the Lord Patriarch to aspire to reconciliation rather than to depart from China with the project abandoned. |

Secondly, on September 4th he ordered that there be brought to him from the capital the palace records with all the official acts negotiated between himself and the Lord Patriarch. He had them authenticated and he himself examined them, then he had them transcribed, translated into Latin, fortified by the seal of his eldest son who was commissioned to deal with this matter, the seals of two Mandarins being also added, so that properly confirmed they might be sent to the Holy Father.[^p135-56] All this was carried out both in Tartary and in Beijing on the 7th, 12th, 13th, 20th and 21st of September, except for the impression of the seal, which will be described in due course.

The Most Excellent Lord Patriarch had given him a motive for producing the translation and its authentication; for during the dispute of many days which began on the very evening of August 20th, the mandarins, among other things, had put before the Lord Patriarch, who vehemently resisted, that His Excellency should keep in mind that he was conducting the business of the Holy Father, not his own; it could be that the Emperor was making known to the Pope that this business had come to nothing not through any fault of his own, but because of the dishonesty of his legate. The Lord Patriarch replied, with his head erect, that he knew he would be vindicated before His Holiness both in his own eyes and those of everyone in Rome. They were not pleased to hear this, and, among other complaints, wrote a full account to His Majesty. From this it came about that the Emperor wished to maintain his authority, and, by way of an appeal, decided to send all the Acts in appropriate form to the Holy Father.

Nevertheless His Majesty pressed on with his previously conceived decree of recalling the embassy; but, in accordance with the moderation of mind which he had already maintained for ten months, he waited to see whether the Lord Patriarch would even now, from aboard ship, give some sign of a gentler frame of mind; and as, when asking permission to leave he had sought the opportunity to ask concerning the health of the Emperor, so also he might now, with courteous respectfulness, do this while on his way out of the city, and might <!-- PDF page 136 | book page 126 | manuscript page 412 --> request that the embassy be sent on its way to Europe. But since no word from His Excellency was announced, the Emperor began to grow sick of waiting so long, and, on the one hand considering himself to be scorned, and on the other feeling himself in many ways aroused to anger by Father Guéty, the Most Reverend Bishop of Conon, etc., he gradually moved towards the idea of a sentence of death. I write this not from mere conjecture; reason for so writing was provided by Henkama after 15 September when, more anxiously than usual, he asked of me whether no letter had come even by then? Whether the Lord Patriarch had not yet announced that, with his head bent to the ground, he was inquiring concerning the health of His Majesty? And when I said that nothing of that nature had appeared, he lamented this saying: truly, even in this matter, the earlier and the later actions of this Duoluo were not in agreement: when going away he behaved quite discreetly, and gave hope that he would be mindful of the benefits received; but now, repeating his offensive behaviour, he discourteously remains silent, and the consequence will be that my Lord will harshly carry out what he has delayed for so long.

And thus it has in fact turned out. The Emperor had already moved his summer camp, coming near to the hunting area in the more remote parts of Tartary, and what for some days had been quietly going about the camp as a rumour emerged into the light of day. ### 20. He is commanded to write the letter once again and send it to the Emperor

Moreover there are indeed ways of speaking and writing which are understood in their precise sense only by those to whom they are spoken or written. Such is the case in Canticle ch. 8 verse 14, where the soul says to the Beloved, “Depart, my Beloved,” and the Beloved alone understands this to mean “Come,” understanding it in the light of the preceding words, “Friends are listening.” Since friends were listening, and nevertheless the voice of the soul was seeking to be heard, it was appropriate to say “Depart” instead of “Come,” in order to deceive the friends and yet bring consolation to the Beloved.

### 21. The Bishop of Conon is ordered to remain in Beijing and wait there with the maker of clocks

Let us return to those matters which, according to chronological order, refer to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon. He had been ordered by command of the Emperor to remain in Beijing, on the pretext that he should wait for his companion Father Guéty, whose skill the Emperor wished first to put to the proof, since he had been represented to him by the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch on June 30th as being gifted with talents by which he might serve His Majesty to his great satisfaction. ### 23. They have more difficulty in appeasing the eldest son of the Emperor

Father Barros replied to this and the preceding letters on 6th September, in the following way: The troubles of the world often develop in such a way that only the divine omnipotence can take care of them. When we ascertained by certain signs that the Emperor’s anger was directed against the xianggong Jin and others, we immediately—based on long experience, without any great hopes—attempted, sometimes together and on other occasions separately, as best we could (and sometimes perhaps even beyond the bounds of prudence) to placate the anger of the Emperor and the zeal of his eldest son. I received at that time a letter from Father Kilian Stumpf, in which he persuasively passed on not only the wishes of all the Fathers in Beijing but also the orders of the superiors who directed us to use every possible means to placate the Emperor and obtain pardon for these miserable Chinese, especially for the xianggong Jin, so that he might be allowed to depart in peace for his homeplace. We are doing all that we can and should do, not only in obedience to our superiors, but also in response to the pleas of all the Fathers, and out of charity towards our neighbours. We kneel, make petitions, beat our heads on the floor several times; we try every means that we consider more appropriate. We do not know what we have achieved or what will happen, but we are prevented from saying more on this matter. We are filled with confusion without knowing what decisions we may dare to adopt without imprudence or which do not seem impossible without endangering not only us individually, but all of us and the whole mission. And so on the 4th of September (the 28th day of the 7th month) at night (this day we had travelled to Porohoton) the Emperor summoned us to hand in a letter from Your Reverence which had just arrived in the post which he had ordered to be translated. We regarded this as a heaven-sent opportunity, and when we presented the translation to the eunuch Zhang Qilin we added all the good reasons we had been able to find to move the mind of the Emperor and incline him to compassion for these miserable men. The eunuch returned with a reply accompanied by Zhao Laoye. On bended knees we listened to the Emperor’s zhi which was in the following terms: The Emperor says that since you are Religious you can intervene on behalf of those condemned. Nevertheless, | he has sent me to declare to you that this matter <!-- PDF page 153 | book page 143 --> pertains to his government, and not to you, and that it is not for you to meddle in such matters. The Emperor wants to examine and clear up what is going on in this business since for two hundred years there has never been such a confused and complicated matter as the current one. The Emperor wants to know how these affairs have arisen, and at the same time how they can be brought to a conclusion. He says: “The example that An Duo[^p153-87] (Father Antoine Thomas) touches on in his letter has very little relevance to you because the Eleuths were never vassals of mine but these Chinese are my people which makes the situation very different.” In addition he wishes to tell you that it is not convenient for you to make any more requests or to meddle further in a matter that belongs to the empire and not you.

Despite so definite a reply we twice respectfully, and with further humiliating expressions demanded by the danger to which we were exposing ourselves, renewed our requests to the Emperor, striking our heads on the pavement in, as they say, peng tou,[^p153-88] begging that he might have pity on these miserable men and especially on Jin Xiu, who is a simple and unsophisticated man and who is not blameworthy in these matters which the Emperor has found offensive. Twice we repeated this, twice he returned a reply to us in every way the same as before but in ever stronger language and intent, that this was a matter of state and belonged to his imperial authority; that we as foreigners and Religious ought not get mixed up in it nor intercede for such men.

We tell Your Reverences what has happened sincerely and with the greatest sorrow. If Your Reverences should judge that we have not done our duty, and satisfied the wishes of all Your Reverences, just order and declare again what we should do, for we are ready to obey to the letter the orders of the superiors even where we foresee danger in doing what His Excellency has ordered.

### 24. The Emperor, angry and offended, wants to receive satisfaction from the missionaries

I write to Your Reverences a common letter on a matter common to all. Reading what Father Pereira wrote on the outer cover of Father Barros’s letter, I began to fear greatly that we were perceiving at last actually coming to pass, to the sorrow of all, what for a long time I have, with dread, thought might happen. The Emperor feels pain at having been offended, and justly feels this pain. He seeks satisfaction, and does not find any. What, then, is more likely to happen than that he act after the manner of kings, and, as the superior, take to himself the justice which his inferiors deny him? So, woe to all missionaries! My Fathers! No kind of satisfaction is within our power, and so in this matter one can only turn to Him Who can do everything. I do not doubt that the Emperor, since he sees that the Lord Patriarch can and ought to give satisfaction, also thinks that we can and ought to elicit such satisfaction from the same Lord Patriarch. But we can do nothing; for whether we urge him with prayers or reasons, His Excellency will admit neither prayers nor reasons, and he will be the more savage towards us, the more earnestly we wish to bring him to a gentler frame of mind.

What shall I say about these wretched Chinese?[^p154-89] What Father Antoine thinks of them he has written again. | The Fathers of this house and College have told me that it does not seem useful to make any further petitions to the Emperor, but rather to make petitions to God alone, that He may guide the heart of the Emperor. The first reason is that, clearly, this would provoke more anger, until the time comes when God may perhaps suggest some more pleasant remedy. Secondly, since His Majesty no longer considers us as compassionate but rather as troublesome, and if he is thus angry with us also, all hope is taken away that we may be able at some time to return and intercede with him, which it seems necessary for us to be able to do in the event of a penalty being imposed, and when it comes nearer to the time of passing sentence. Thirdly, since there is some foundation for our being under suspicion, the Emperor, or certainly the Prince, has written to Fujian to gain information from there concerning the number of Christians, their views, their practice concerning the Rites, and the quality and rank of the persons. If the Emperor is waiting for this information, certainly he will not allow us to intercede until he is in possession of it, nor will he permit himself to be distracted by us to move in some other direction; we know the character of His Majesty. Certainly Henkama, ten days ago, questioned two servants of Father Beauvollier[^p154-90] concerning the name, rank, etc. of the xianggong of the Lord Bishop of Conon, and <!-- PDF page 155 | book page 145 --> similar matters; this was not done for nothing. It is established, moreover, that he sent this information to Tartary.

### 25. The difficulty of changing for the better the plans of Lord Bishop of Conon

Concerning the Bishop of Conon, we cannot imagine what is likely to happen. It is now certain that he will remain here until the return of the Emperor. There will not be sufficient clothes (appropriate to the weather), nor perhaps money etc. The doctor on August 28th had accompanied the Lord Patriarch as far as Zhangjiawan (this is the place where the boat was standing ready). We see clearly that neither his nor the Emperor’s attitude is good, and so it is to be feared that the Lord Bishop is caught up in an unavoidable fate. But it is not possible to help the man, unless a hand reaches out from heaven and touches his heart, so that it may be softened to gentler notions. There is nothing for us to do but commend him to God in our daily prayers, so that with God’s protection, he may escape the danger that threatens from the Emperor, and with God’s guidance he may have compassion for the mission, and finally that he may permit it to happen that his opinions perish rather than souls.

### 29. Suspicion is wrongly cast on Father Parrenin

But in truth the error is in the apprehension, which is confirmed by false reasoning along these lines: Father Parrenin was watching when I handed over the letters; at that moment we went to the palace of the Emperor; Father Parrenin went in while I remained outside; when he came out from there he led me away to the sick man, where both of us were to remain by order of the Emperor; and in the meantime they knew at court that the letters had been handed over; they questioned Father Guéty about them etc.; thus this information was given, and perhaps a desire to seek them out was inspired by Father Parrenin.

Nothing in this line of argument is true. Already on the previous day Father Guéty had been forbidden by the Emperor to communicate with the doctor before he had seen him, and there were witnesses there at the time; and Father Guéty himself had already been transferred elsewhere; and the servants of the doctor himself had been strictly charged by the mandarins that same night, as an imperial command, that no European be allowed to speak with the doctor. Hence when Fathers Pereira and Barros learned of the coming of the doctor late on the previous evening, and, on the following day, not knowing of the royal command, in the morning went to greet him on his arrival, his servants, kneeling on the ground, first begged the Fathers not to enter, and when they did finally come in, turned them away, saying that it was not possible, under pain of grave punishment, to allow them to speak with their master. That Father Guéty in the morning saw Doctor Borghese was either by chance, or deliberate; that he spoke with him was by his own decision to do so. That Father Parrenin saw the letters being exchanged was without fault on his part but he was not the only one who saw this. The servants of Father Guéty, the servants of Doctor Borghese, and the servants of Father Parrenin also saw it, and at the same time it was known to others who had been appointed to observe him. So why is Father Parrenin considered to be at fault? If the Father alone entered the palace, and Doctor Borghese remained outside the first door, how did he hear, from outside the door, what the other was saying inside? And even if he had been in the presence of the speaker and listened to him, he could not have understood, since in order to tell the court that letters had been handed over, he would have had to speak either in Chinese or in the Tartar language, and Doctor Borghese did not yet understand either Chinese or Tartar.

### 30. And [ false] suspicions against other Fathers concerning the holding back, hindering, or making public of the letters of their adversaries

How little the Fathers in Beijing rejoiced at knowing about these gentlemen’s letters is shown by those three in Tartary refusing to translate the letter that had already been made public and asking the Prince not to compel Father Guéty to undertake the further task of restoring another letter. To speak more specifically of the above-mentioned letter of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon to Father Guéty: after I have quoted it below from the original manuscript of Father Guéty, no one in his senses will doubt that from it the Jesuits could ferret out anything but his own confusion, and the disgrace of those reverend gentlemen and subsequent anger of the Emperor and the scorn of the whole camp. The said Father Pereira wrote to Father Kilian: |

### 31. The danger and mischief that arose from the letter of the Bishop of Conon could not be prevented

Everybody noticed that what up until now neither the Lord Patriarch nor Maigrot, the Lord Bishop of Conon, had dared to say, this disciple of theirs vomited out in a forceful and accusatory spirit. But the more he said the <!-- PDF page 164 | book page 154 | manuscript page 428 --> more he tightened the knot and put a noose around his own neck, and demonstrated how charitable we had been towards him.

This was all the more so since the Emperor before all this happened, through the eunuch Liang (the supreme Prefect of the Eunuchs) had sent to tell us that we were naive simpletons (dai in Chinese[^p164-112]) for still trying to help such people.[^p164-113] All this had made him more annoyed with them but he always managed to conceal it.

I replied that His Majesty saw things that we did not see; but that even when His Majesty called us childish simpletons I had not repented of what I had done; and that in referring to me as a simpleton His Majesty saw into our hearts as making a request on behalf of someone who sought to do us harm since His Majesty had sensed their ill intentions and we had never perceived this. But through this evil done to us we still hoped to receive much good from His Majesty.

The reverend gentleman said further that the Pope had sent the Lord Patriarch here to find out, and hence the Pope to determine and test, whether the Jesuits were obedient | to the Pope. ### 32. The hatred of our opponents towards the Jesuits does not damage the Jesuits alone but our religion

Your Reverences are waiting for me to say something about Father Guéty; well then. If the Emperor calls us little ones, let us be fools for the sake of Christ.[^p165-116] But what will he call this Father except mad with hatred for the Jesuits? What a mess! These completely stupid remarks are greatly to be lamented. They do not injure us, if we are looking to our reputation, or our particular welfare, since our innocence will shine out all the more; but they do harm above all because they destroy the common good and give cause for the gentiles to utter calumnies. With what shame of countenance will we dare to hear our Holy Law blasphemed, since these pagans may think that they have grounds for speaking ill of it? For they will now be able to make this objection to us: just look at those men who say that they are foremost in your Law, Religious superiors, leaders of the mission, bishops, patriarchs, who come here so that, before our eyes, they may tell lies, make accusations, spread evil reports of others, humiliate them and expel them. What then is good in your Law, if those men do the things that our Confucius long ago already taught were not to be done? Etc.

To this letter Father Antoine Thomas, the Vice-Rector, added his own letter, together with [the decrees of] the Council of Trent and the Bulls of the Holy Pontiffs, and, on the 8th day of the 8th lunar month,[^p165-117] sent them all to Father Antonio de Barros. It would be difficult for me to omit this letter, since it can contribute greatly to clarify for posterity the way in which these matters were understood by the Fathers in Beijing and how they proceeded.

The text of the letter follows, dated the 8th day of the 8th lunar month, 1706:

I received Your Reverence’s letter written from Porohoton, and I rejoice to learn from it that my letter has been communicated to His Majesty, and that it was not displeasing to him that we Religious intercede for those in a wretched situation and recommend mercy for them, since that is a duty appropriate to us, as His Majesty said when explaining (in the presence of the Lord Patriarch) a text of Confucius in support of our opinion; but his duty is to establish justice and to punish offenders. And so we make our pleas, as we ought, practising mercy, and His Majesty does what he ought, punishing offences. But we do not dare to intercede any further, lest we seem not to be aware how great is the innate propensity of His Majesty to be merciful, since he always reproves unwillingly, and to an extent less than is deserved; just as a father deals with his sons, being much more inclined to spare than to punish. Therefore there <!-- PDF page 166 | book page 156 | manuscript page 429 --> is nothing that we would wish to add to the affection of a father towards his subjects |, and, his heart filled with honey, we would wish him to pour out sweetness upon them. One who knows how to treat even enemies well, will always be kind in punishing his erring subjects. The fact that we intercede for those who speak and think ill of us is a consequence of our following the teaching and example of Christ, and because it is pleasing to God even His Majesty will approve of it. Nor will it displease him to observe the way we conduct our contest, since we are accustomed to fight against our adversaries with many acts of kindness and love, and we hope finally to win the contest, helped by divine protection.

### 33. The attitude of the Society regarding its enemies

Now that our task of intercession has been brought to an end, the only thing that remains is that we entreat His Majesty that the Christian religion suffer no detriment from the things that he will rightly judge need to be done; this religion is the pupil of our eye, and injury to it is a wound in the midst of our heart. May we be allowed at least to intercede for it, so that what His Majesty has granted on its behalf up to now, giving it freedom, treating the missionaries generously and with honour, and granting innumerable other favours—God approving of this, and granting as a reward a great peace in the realm—may he now preserve in these evil times, and continue his faithful kindness towards this religion.

Concerning the name of the Society of Jesus, it is established that this was confirmed to our Society by Paul III, Julius III, and all the other Popes since that time, who always call it by this name in their bulls. It is confirmed also by the Council of Trent itself, in session 25, chapter 16, where it says: “the clerical religious order of the Society of Jesus according to its Institute approved by the Holy See” etc. Finally the same thing is evident from the bulls and letters from the Holy Father sent hither, which we have here, with their seal, and in which he calls Father Pereira and myself and the others: of the Society of Jesus. Let the Lord Patriarch be asked about this, and the Lord Bishop of Conon, for further evidence; we have very many of their letters, and also letters of kings, princes, and cardinals, addressed to our Fathers of the Society of Jesus. To deny such a thing is to wish to envelop the sun in darkness.[^p166-118]

### 34. Father Guéty is sorry for the lie he told, and confesses it

I must note here in what circumstances Father Guéty came to say the things which were mentioned in the preceding letters, and which will be recounted in what follows. Father Guéty, on account of the manifest lie concerning the letter of the Bishop of Conon, and other shameful deeds committed on this occasion, was full of confusion within himself, and had aroused the aversion of the Emperor; hence daily he feared even worse. To avoid these consequences the Fathers in Beijing, out of Christian love, wrote to their companions in Tartary where they themselves were earnestly trying to think of a remedy. They were forbidden to speak with Father Guéty, nevertheless, that they might initiate some way of helping the man, one day, early in the morning, they sent, by a narrow path situated between a stream and the tents of the camp, Brother Paramino to speak with Father Guéty. They could more easily excuse him for transgressing the command of silence, by claiming that being still ignorant of the language, he had not sufficiently understood the command, or he had come there to provide medical advice. The brother went and immediately Father Guéty said to him that he repented exceedingly of his deceit and the lie he had told; for the brother pointed out to him how grave it was in the eyes of the Emperor in a European, who said he was in China only in order to preach the Gospel and to serve the Emperor. Father Guéty added that he would never again do anything of the sort, and that he would choose death rather than say what was not true; that in the future he would answer in a completely straightforward fashion, even if this would endanger his life; and that he wished this to be made known to the Fathers by the brother. The Fathers rejoiced at this news, and, in order to meet the requests of those in Beijing and satisfy their charity, they went | to the place where His Majesty was staying, and, on bended knees before the eunuchs and courtiers, deplored the error previously committed by Father Guéty, and asked that his repentance be announced to the Emperor and his forgiveness obtained. The Emperor laughed when he heard this. “Let us see,” he said, “how truthful he is.” At once he commanded that he be questioned by his eldest son, and then those many other things happened, some of which are recorded in the preceding letters.

Father Guéty knew this at the time, his servants knew it, and there was no one who did not know it. But on the grounds of this an accusation was made against the Fathers. For Father Guéty himself, among other inaccuracies, wrote to the Lord Patriarch: firstly that Brother Paramino had come to spy on him, and thus the way was opened to the troubles that followed; secondly that the Fathers were hidden by the Prince so as to participate in his questioning; this was also asserted by a servant of Father Guéty in the presence of the Lord Patriarch, the Reverend Father Buonaventura da Roma[^p168-119] being the interpreter. ### 49. The truth of the questions and answers in this interrogation is shown

Regarding the first point: if the Jesuit Fathers Pereira, Barros, and Parrenin secretly incited the Emperor to appoint commissioners to question Father Guéty concerning everything he might know against the Jesuits, for what purpose would they have done so, I ask? In what way would it have been advantageous to them or to their companions? If it was to elicit the truth from an adversary, the Jesuits must necessarily have been very secure in their innocence, seeing that they had no fear | of being exposed before a gentile emperor, to whom they already knew that they had been delated by the Patriarch in a written complaint. Even though they might have known that they were purer than crystal, nevertheless it would scarcely have been a wise plan, in such a court of inquiry conducted by the Emperor’s eldest son etc., to probe their adversary by questions of this kind and provoke foolish replies. If they say it was a game, not an investigation, and what was sought was laughter, not serious truth, then certainly those who say this are day-dreaming, and deceiving themselves and others.

### 50. The Jesuits were not involved in Father Guéty’s being examined in this way

With which minor mandarin throughout the provinces would those reverend gentlemen play such a game? And what none of them would dare to do with the magistrate of a country district, do they imagine that the Jesuits would dare to do with the Chinese emperor, whose authority among all those who rule under the throne is exceedingly great, to the point of religious awe of his majesty? The evidence they bring forward as proof of their suspicion is ridiculous in itself, and indicates their small-mindedness and ignorance, and lack of experience of the Chinese world. The questions and objections, they say, are redolent of European disputes and quarrels and therefore … So only the Europeans living in China are perceptive? So the Chinese emperor cannot formulate an intelligent question unless he receives it, honed to sharpness, from a Jesuit? Without fear of contradiction I say that the wisdom of the Emperor is a source of astonishment to all monarchs; by means of it he rules, as sole king, more millions of human beings than are encompassed at any one time by all the kingdoms of Europe. Yet will some missionary, fresh out of the seminary or monastery, nevertheless deny that something that has been said was said by the Emperor, simply because it was intelligently said?

Nor is it astute to argue as others do ex post facto, alleging that the Jesuits received no notable harm from the allegations made by Father Guéty, but rather seem to have profited from them in the eyes of the Emperor, since he, whom the Emperor rightly believed to be their adversary, could offer no proof of their guilt; therefore it was preferable for them to be accused by Father Guéty <!-- PDF page 186 | book page 176 | manuscript page 442 --> in this way, rather than not to be accused. Since it proved useful for them, presumably it was instigated by them.

Let me briefly respond to this charge. If they are our friends, for the love of God let them offer to us what Job’s friends offered to him after he suffered misfortunes;[^p186-142] if they are enemies, for the fear of God let them remember the enemies of Daniel, and not ridiculously suspect either that the lions instigated the misfortunes of the latter, or that Satan instigated those of the former so that his later situation might be better than his previous one.[^p186-143]

Regarding the second point: the excuse of the Lord Bishop of Conon for his companion Father Guéty was given when he still did not know exactly what the latter said. If he had known the specific replies the latter provided, the rigour of his theology would never have allowed him to say that the conscience of an honest man had, in that situation, obliged him to lie, and to bring false testimony against priests of God in a foreign tribunal before a pagan emperor, and against the Catholic city of Macau, the very root of this Christian mission.

Regarding the third point of Father Guéty: that he had no intention of lying, nor of injuring the Jesuits. In that case, he had the intention of speaking the truth, when knowingly and willingly he said what was false, and the intention of demonstrating the innocence of the Jesuits when he ascribed to them crimes they had not committed. In the same way, perhaps, the Philistines intended to sharpen Samson’s vision when they put out his eyes![^p186-144] I do not doubt that Father Guéty, being greatly embarrassed before the imperial camp on account of his previous lie about the letter of the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Conon, did, in his own way firmly | propose to avoid all lies in the future; but one cannot doubt that this resolution was not in the least put into practice by him when he denied that our Society is called the Society of Jesus, when he spoke of the danger of his being imprisoned in Macau and of the French painter sent back by the Jesuits, of the complaints of the Holy Father, of the contract entered into by us, etc.

### 51. Father Guéty spoke what was false knowingly and willingly

Since he knew these things to be false, and knowingly declared them to be true, speaking against his own mind, I do not know how he turned his mind away in order to avoid lying. If he should declare as he has written, that he said when in a confused state of mind, what he ought not to have said, this would provide <!-- PDF page 187 | book page 177 --> something of an excuse, if this Father had publicly declared that he was confused in his mind before he replied; but to pour out his whole inner self in lies, without protest or qualification and then, when convicted of lying, to allege confusion of mind—such an excuse, if it were allowed to those being examined, would overturn all the tribunals conducted throughout the whole world.

Those who read this process of examination of Father Guéty attentively, will quickly perceive that he was never in so confused a state of mind that he mentioned by name the Bishop of Rosalia,[^p187-145] Basset,[^p187-146] Hervé,[^p187-147] or any other of his own people, while he did accuse Father Mezzafalce from Italy, Father Astudillo of the Dominicans, and Father Francisco de la Concepción of the Franciscans, etc.[^p187-148] Further, he acted very cautiously, so that, in the very difficult situation when the letter that had been inadequately buried and covered in filth was restored to its original condition, he did not specify the name of Father Le Breton,[^p187-149] but instead said “one of my friends.” But the name itself had been specified in the original, as the reconstituted original letter shows; it was delivered to Father Barros by the Emperor, with other documents, to be taken to Europe.

Further, this process of examination easily proves that while Father Guéty was not the most skilful of our adversaries, it does not prove him to be so stupid that what was done should be considered as not done, and what was written should be withdrawn.

This, above all, they want to affirm: that Father Guéty was forced by threats and blows to speak ill of the Jesuits. If this is true, it must be false that the questions and objections put to this Father by the Prince were fabricated by the Jesuits; for to what end would they wish to extract by blows responses that were against themselves? It is true that in this examination process, which was prolonged for many days, Father Guéty was ordered by the Prince to be struck, and suffered some blows, until Father Parrenin, by putting his body in the way, protected him from the hands of those beating him. But he suffered these blows of the fist, not because he was unwilling to say anything bad about the Jesuits, but because what he said of his own free will was shown to have been said mendaciously, and because he denied the very things that he had affirmed a little <!-- PDF page 188 | book page 178 | manuscript page 443 --> earlier; thus he dishonoured the Prince and the Emperor himself, like a common slave, weaving falsehood into falsehood, to the shame of the Europeans and the just indignation of the Tartar and Chinese listeners.

Without doubt after a few years many people will ask: who, indeed, was this Father Guéty, who made himself so famous in Tartary and in the court of Beijing? Wherefore, lest those who come after be totally ignorant of whence and who he was, of whom so much has been narrated. I will add those details that have come to me as quite certain about the man.

### 52. Who was Father Guéty, what was his background and his life history

He was born in France, and, as far as one can guess, in Paris. His father, according to the testimony of Father Guéty himself, | spent his life selling bridles and saddles in the city of Paris itself. From his boyhood days he studied under a Parisian clockmaker named Martinot,[^p188-150] with whom he made his apprenticeship, and he learnt so well, as his master testified to Brother Brocard, that he could construct a clock, if he had been willing to give the matter due attention and work with diligence. However he did not wish to make his living by means of his craft, but, hoping to improve his fortunes, he set sail for India, where, near Pondicherry, his brother was legitimately married to a Portuguese widow, a respectable woman, and conducted a business selling jewellery and buying precious stones. Father Guéty himself, at the age of about […][^p188-151] came to Guangzhou from the coast of India in an English ship, with Father Lirot,[^p188-152] who previously had been in service in the house in Paris of Father […],[^p188-153] he himself being the son of the gardener. Both were accepted by the reverend clerics of the Paris Seminary in India,[^p188-154] and the former was principally associated with the Lord Bishop of Conon,[^p188-155] the latter with the Lord Bishop of Rosalia,[^p188-156] and they worked with them, in the free European fashion, in the kitchen, in household management, and in the office of buyer (according to what was publicly said among the neophytes), for about […][^p188-157] years. Then each was ordained priest in the year […][^p188-158] by the Most Reverend Lord Bishop of

<!-- PDF page 189 | book page 179 -->

Argolis,[^p189-159] and each was accepted among the missionaries of the Paris Seminary. Having been ordained priest, Father Guéty exercised his ministry in Nianzhou, in the province of Zhejiang; but he had very few Christians, to the extent that when our Brothers Rodes and Frapperie[^p189-160] were passing through, he said that he had no more than eight, and these were blind and lame. Father Lirot carried out his ministry in Shaozhou, where, in order to pass away the time, he was accustomed to occupying himself on horseback with bow and quiver among the military archers and the military mandarins, as he himself confessed to the Reverend Father Buonaventura da Roma, of the Order of Friars Minor, who was passing through that place, and was astonished to see him in that guise; he afterwards described what he had seen to the Most Reverend Lord Bishop of Nanjing[^p189-161] in the presence of Father Giampaolo Gozani,[^p189-162] in the month of December 1701. Whether they had a stipend from the Seminary[^p189-163] I do not know, but it is certain that each year they received what they needed to live on, the former from the Bishop of Conon, the latter from the Bishop of Rosalia; this Lord Bishop also diligently took care that his protégé should not know how much money was in the house, and so handed over a large part of the money to the Bishop of Argolis for safe keeping, lest perhaps his junior protégé should learn to spend too freely. Concerning Father Guéty it is true that he did not lack food and clothing for his immediate needs; nevertheless he began to make and repair clocks, and sell them, so that he might either live more comfortably or to be able to provide for future needs. He was of moderate strength, had a long face and deep eyes, was of dark complexion and melancholy temperament, and so (the Bishop of Conon told me) he often found it necessary to summon him from Nianzhou, the place of his mission, to Fuzhou fu, in order to rid him of his melancholy, which the Bishop also said prevented him from frequently saying Mass. I have discovered nothing certain about his illness, but it was of such a nature that, before dinner and supper, it was necessary for him to eat bread liberally soaked in spirits or brandy. This kind of medication was so necessary for him that, in Tartary in the presence of the Prince, the eldest son, and the <!-- PDF page 190 | book page 180 | manuscript page 444 --> younger sons and little grandsons of the Emperor, who wished to observe him at dinner, he was unable to abstain from it. And when they had seen him either eating or drinking one dish of spirits (about six or more ounces), with a notable lack of esteem for the man, they made fun of him by mimicking him, | at times asking the other Europeans whether this was the mode of living among the respectable men of Europe? It is very true that this kind of alcoholic medication will cause a great deal of prejudice against this man in the court, where no one would dare to allow others know he was tasting wine before nightfall, that is, except in the case of banquets etc. I have written these things about the person of Father Guéty in order to preserve the memory of them.

Now let us return to the main thread of the narrative.

This month practically nothing else happened beyond what has been recorded, and these things themselves did not come into our hands all at once, but gradually, as times and opportunities offered. The sayings and writings of Father Guéty which have been recorded were not received by us before about the 8th of October. We heard the first news about Father Mezzafalce being called to Beijing as follows.

On September 19th a distinguished young Christian, the grandson of the Lady Candida,[^p190-164] that matron renowned in China, son of a mandarin in the bingbu or Bureau of Army and Postal Services,[^p190-165] came to the college to hear Mass, and with complete devotion he told Father Soares that on the 16th and 17th of September an order came from the Emperor that he should go to the said tribunal so as to summon as soon as possible a European from Fujian and two Chinese scholars, of whom one was a juren called Jiang and the other a xiucai called Li.[^p190-166] He could not correctly say the name of the European; he first said it was Messe or Misa,[^p190-167] then hearing from Christians that there was a European in Fujian called Dong, being uncertain what the name was, he called him sometimes Me Shi and sometimes Dong Shi.[^p190-168] He advised Father Soares of <!-- PDF page 191 | book page 181 | manuscript page 445 --> this in writing, so that we might inquire in the Beitang[^p191-169] concerning the names of the Europeans, and it was conjectured that the reference was to Father Le Breton, who was call Dong in Chinese,[^p191-170] and fitted in with his being an associate of Father Guéty, and of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, and that a letter had been sent by him to Father Guéty, and translated by him in Tartary in the presence of the Prince. But this conjecture was false.[^p191-171]

On the 22nd of September Father Soares was called in order to hear a confession, and to administer baptism to the little sister of the said youth, and speaking with the youth’s mandarin father himself he heard the same news, but without sufficient clarity concerning the name, and with diversity of location, namely that the European was not in the province of Fujian, but Zhejiang.

### 53. Father Mezzafalce is summoned by the Emperor

We remained doubtful as to what these things might mean, and were with good reason fearful lest notable harm might thence come upon both the missionaries and the mission. And so, since nothing about these things had been written from Tartary by our companions there, we warned them to be on their guard against the evils that might follow, if that were possible, and to let us know about any that may have happened already. They were astonished at the news we sent, and, inquiring diligently, they discovered that the Most Reverend Father Mezzafalce had been summoned—his European name had been given by Father Guéty—and also that a European was to be called from Jining, by the name of Bian,[^p191-172] with the Reverend Fr. Astudilho, a Dominican. They also heard the reason why Father Mezzafalce was called first: namely that the scribe Pursama, who was writing down the words of Father Guéty, told the Prince that he had seen the man on the way when he had brought Father Sabino, that he had accompanied them for three days, and that he had shown himself to be lihai de,[^p191-173] or vehement and bold. The summoning of this Father could not now be prevented. As far as the others were concerned, the Fathers | asked very earnestly that they should not be compelled to come because of their identification by a man so light in the head as Father Guéty had shown himself to be. And in fact it was the pleasure of the Emperor to suspend their being summoned.

<!-- PDF page 192 | book page 182 -->

Then others laboured to penetrate the mystery of how the European name of Father Mezzafalce was found out; when the answer was given that it had been made known by Father Guéty as the others had been—which could not be denied—they asked further why, therefore, the others were not called? Perhaps these reverend gentlemen wished to imply that the Jesuits, while knowing this, had allowed the others to be called as they could not prevent it, but that the Most Reverend Mezzafalce could not have been summoned without their knowledge.

But that is enough about this so varied month. Let us return to our previous manner of writing.
