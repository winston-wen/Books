These Acts begin in the month of April [1705] when the Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lord Charles Thomas Maillard de Tournon, Patriarch of Antioch and Apostolic Visitor with the powers of a legate a latere etc. began his dealings with the Fathers of the Society of Jesus residing in Beijing and continue until 28 August 1706 inclusive when he left Beijing. The whole short work is divided into sections, the contents of which are displayed in the headings to each section.

### 1. The Most Illustrious and Most Revered Lord Patriarch orders the Beijing Fathers to request from the Emperor permission to come to the Court on official business[^p44-2]

The Most Illustrious and Most Revered Lord Patriarch of Antioch Charles Thomas announced, to our great satisfaction, from the City of the Apostle[^p45-3] in a letter dated 7 February 1702 that arrived in Beijing on 17 January 1704 addressed to Fr. Filippo Grimaldi,[^p45-3] that he was being sent on a legation by Pope Clement XI. In the same, he kindly requested that Fr. Grimaldi should send beforehand, to whatever Chinese port he might land at, certain necessary information so that his entry into this empire with customs so different from

<!-- PDF page 45 | book page 35 -->

Europe might be accomplished more expeditiously. Fr. Grimaldi joyfully carried out these requests and sent several letters in anticipation to the ports of Fuzhou and Guangzhou,[^p45-5] in which he introduced the Most Illustrious Lord in due and sincere observance of the instructions. He left to the judgment of the Most Illustrious Lord what would be the more suitable or the more promising course of action in the circumstances, according to his rank and instructions. That he had received these letters the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch attested in two letters sent from Canton on 8 May [1705] to the aforesaid Father, which have been sent to Europe together with all the other original documents in the care of Fathers Antonio de Barros and Antoine Beauvollier, our procurators.[^p45-6] When the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch had arrived in Canton on 8 April [1705], he determined, having immediately on his arrival consulted the more senior missionaries of the various orders and nations, to conceal from the Court his sacred authority. He would deal with the affairs of the mission but for the time being hide the splendour of the office entrusted to him until the time was ripe to openly reveal it to the Chinese Church, to its joy and profit. And to that end, through Fr. Beauvollier, the assistant procurator of the Fathers in Canton, he advised the Beijing Fathers that he intended to move to Nanjing without any public display and that they should direct their letters there, since His Illustriousness would no longer be found in the city of Guangzhou.

<!-- PDF page 46 | book page 36 -->

Not long afterwards there arrived in Macau from Europe some associates of his together with one or two missionaries who disagreed with the others, and the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch changed his mind and ordered through Fr. Beauvollier that the Fathers without any further discussion should announce to the Emperor that he was the Patriarch of Antioch, the Superior of all the missionaries and Apostolic Visitor, and requested to be admitted to the Court. Subsequently, in a letter of May 8th he left it up to Fr. Grimaldi whether or not to reveal his title of Visitor when speaking to the Emperor, if perchance he had not yet mentioned it. The reason for this second determination, which he was under no obligation to reveal, he did not communicate to the Fathers, except that he implied in the letter of May 8th already referred to that it seemed that if he were to assume a public role he would avoid the unwanted attentions of the customs officials which, as he had experienced in Canton were demanded here. From this time on His Illustriousness made no further request for advice from any of the Beijing Fathers, unless you count a private note that he wrote to Fr. Grimaldi on the same May 8th in which he exhorted him nor to be afraid to speak up if he had any suggestions to make. But, in fact, on two later occasions Grimaldi got no reply to his objections but was obliged to carry out the orders when instructed to nominate a man from Our Society to become Vicar Apostolic of Nanjing, which was not a lawful action for a superior.[^p46-7] In obedience to the memorial of the Most Illustrious Lord, the Beijing Fathers acting together informed His Majesty of the arrival of so important a guest and requested that he should be given permission to come to the Court. The Emperor went away to Tartary[^p46-8] and made no clear reply to our first and second application; our third he refused and only on our fourth did he finally reply favourably. On the eighth day of the sixth Chinese month[^p46-9] he allocated Tartar/Chinese[^p46-10] clothing to the same Most Illustrious Lord and ordered him to be conducted <!-- PDF page 47 | book page 37 --> to Beijing no less comfortably than honourably, at the expense of the Viceroy and Governor of Guangdong.[^p47-11] We sent last year the records of the negotiations between the Emperor and the Beijing Fathers about admitting the Lord Patriarch to the Court and we send them again with our procurators, one of whom, Fr. de Barros, was serving the Emperor in Tartary while this business was being conducted. The Beijing Fathers were certainly able to congratulate themselves on this happy outcome of their efforts, not the least because the result they had been able to offer to the Most Illustrious Lord through their services avoided the abuse they would have suffered on the part of their critics if such a visitor were to have been excluded or admitted without public honours. I don’t want to recall what before this they had spread around to denigrate the Jesuits but as an indication let me cite three words from the pen of the Lord Patriarch himself, writing to Fr. Grimaldi on 4 September 1705, where he said: “My entry that in Rome I thought would be much more difficult.”[^p47-12] The Most Illustrious Lord left Guangzhou for Beijing on September 9th and on his way was pleased by the splendid reception he received from the magistrates of the cities and provinces, as he stated in two letters of the 6th and 10th of October in the series he sent on his journey to Beijing.

The great number of the ships which His Illustriousness notes in the letters referred to prevented a speedy passage, and already November was nearly over without news arriving in Beijing of his approach. Such a delay increased our anxiety and accentuated in the mind of the Emperor his desire, which was already great, whether to see immediately the man of such a status as we had attributed to him, sent by the Supreme Pontiff to these far reaches of the East; or to bestow new favours on this man, in whose person he could recommend himself to the other half of the world. It would be true to say that the Emperor’s benevolence was not only displayed towards him to an unusual extent, but that he also granted a special place of honour to the authority in these lands of the Supreme Pontiff. These matters are clearly worthy of being described in detail by us and being recorded for posterity in the annals of the Romans.

### 2. Many favours are heaped upon the Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lord Patriarch by the Tartaro-Chinese Emperor

Once permission was granted to come to the Court, the Most Illustrious Lord covered some five hundred leagues[^p48-13] by river, not at his own expense but as if travelling through a long open entrance hall until he should arrive at the treasure-house from which this benevolence came. As the middle of November approached the Emperor sent people ahead into the province of Shandong to find out about the arrival of his guest and to hasten his journey. Perhaps he feared that the winter growing colder would inconvenience the Most Illustrious Lord. On the 27th of the same month the Emperor sent out to meet him, his whereabouts still unknown, the son of the zongdu and the son of the Viceroy,[^p48-14] both mandarins, and one Father from each of the three churches to convey the first signs of his benevolence while His Lordship was still on the way. They found the Most Illustrious Lord twenty-four leagues from Beijing, ill and outside his boat which was iced up in the river unable to move, and carried him off by the land route to Beijing where on December 4th the Most Illustrious Lord by order of His Majesty was carried to the house of Our Society within the Saffron Wall, which since it was close to the Palace would be more convenient for the frequent benefits to be bestowed on him.[^p48-15] On the same day, December 4th, as soon as the Emperor had been informed that the Most Illustrious Lord had arrived, he selected two mandarins of the third rank from his villa[^p48-16] to greet the welcome guest and offer the compliments of His Majesty. On December 6th the first dishes were received by the Most Illustrious Lord through the same mandarins, who wished to see if he could eat such food after his illness in a land so far from home. Two days later the Emperor granted his Excellency through the huaiji si[^p48-17] four portions in allowance (the Beijing Fathers were allotted eight).[^p48-18] He refused them both politely and modestly but was compelled to accept them <!-- PDF page 49 | book page 39 --> as long as he should dwell at the Court. On December 12th the Most Illustrious Lord’s chamberlain, the barber surgeon Pietro Sigotti, died of dysentery. The Emperor had earlier sent a doctor and medicine. When the Emperor was notified of his death in the way normal to these people, two mandarins were sent to offer condolences, and when requested he offered a burial place for the dead man, an acre in the olive orchard of a certain prince on December 14th. This favour was unusual in this respect: that His Majesty took special care to order a search for a site as close as possible to the burial ground of the Fathers. The imperial beneficence displayed in granting a burial place greatly moved His Excellency and his retinue so that they exaggerated its significance and began to call the place the Italian Cemetery. They went around saying that if His Majesty did this for a dead man, he would do much more for the living. But it would have been more advantageous to the affairs of the Most Illustrious Lord if he had accepted the offer of the Fathers of a part or the whole of a tomb, the refusal of which also created suspicions in the Emperor’s mind of his slight regard and disdain for the veteran Europeans. I would say that the Lord Patriarch was unaware of this and that it did not greatly influence his decision.

Meanwhile His Majesty developed a great curiosity about the affair and instructed his spies who closely observed everything to report if anything was done differently from what had hitherto been usual regarding the deceased and the funeral rites. Without showing their hand they were to determine whether the newly arrived Europeans carried out the usual rituals. He learned there were some differences.

On December 19th, the very day the funeral was to be carried out in our house, he sent the Lord Patriarch two pheasants prepared for the imperial table without giving any sign of displeasure. After the funeral rites had been performed the Emperor was extremely anxious to see the Lord Patriarch face to face, and since his tenacious illness had prevented it up until now and the Emperor had to depart on a journey very soon, His Majesty, seeing that this would postpone it unduly, found a way of demonstrating his goodwill without precedent in China, even at the expense of his dignity. On December 30th, he granted an audience to the sick man, and on the 31st, the Emperor at the second hour of the afternoon received the Lord Patriarch in his orchard scarcely an arrow’s shot from the North Gate of the Palace. The Lord Patriarch was carried there and admitted in a chair through the West Gate, then carried by his servants to the very threshold of the audience hall accompanied by his retinue and almost all the Fathers. Along the whole route they followed were eunuch guards. He was announced by the chief chamberlain and given a dispensation from all the ceremonies as a dear but ailing guest, and amidst the <!-- PDF page 50 | book page 40 --> courtiers all marveling and rejoicing with the Lord he came to the place where the Emperor was seated waiting for him.

The Lord Patriarch was brought in leaning on the arms of others and, bending his knees as best he could, paid his respects to His Tartaro-Chinese Majesty.[^p50-19] While he was still on his knees the Emperor quietly asked after the health of the Supreme Pontiff and ordered the Legate to be seated in front of him on felt cushions. Smiling gently and feeling happy himself, he showed that he wished the man seated before him to share his happiness. Such benevolence may perhaps seem to some Europeans a small matter but in China it matters a lot, as the Emperor himself pointed out when he informed the Lord Patriarch that no important men sent by foreign kingdoms were received with such honours. And then he increased it manyfold, for he caused a drink of cha[^p50-20] to be brought by an attendant, and offered wine with his own right hand served in a golden cup, and presented a table with 35 golden platters prepared elsewhere but which he had touched with his own hands, and offered the first mouthful to the lips of the Lord Patriarch. He gave the lot to him and ordered it should be carried to our house so that he could eat and drink at his leisure. After the refreshments had been served, he gave him leave if such was his wish to engage in light conversation. Then returning again to serious matters he invited him in a friendly tone to reveal whatever business he had been commissioned to conduct. This was actually not according to court precedent but done because his visitor was not used to it. The Emperor patiently put up with much that was against protocol, gently getting back to the matter at hand guided by one thought only: to instill confidence in the mind of his visitor and to inspire him to act confidently. The Lord Patriarch was no less honourably dismissed from the audience than he had been admitted and I can say for the record about the whole event, as a matter of simple truth, that in the whole history of China nobody has been received with more honour or treated with more goodwill. If it should seem otherwise in the eyes of inexperienced Europeans, certainly this was how it seemed to the Chinese and the Tartars, as was testified by the Crown Prince and with some envy by his elder brother.

With the coming of the European New Year new favours were bestowed. On January 1st His Majesty promised to send gifts to the Supreme Pontiff and on the 2nd he handed them over and ordered that they should be conveyed by Father

<!-- PDF page 51 | book page 41 -->

Bouvet[^p51-21] as his yuxian or one who assists in his presence,[^p51-22] on behalf of the Emperor, and Fr. Sabino on behalf of the Lord Patriarch. When His Majesty had agreed to these arrangements the gifts were received to be carried to Rome. A few of them were put on display but since part of them had already been loaded on the ships, the greater part could not be brought without inconvenience. He promised to demonstrate the extent of his good will by adding others when the Lord Patriarch should return to Europe. On January 4th the Emperor who was going on his winter hunting expedition kindly inquired whether the Lord Patriarch had one of his suite whom he could send—since he himself could not enjoy the delights of hunting—to experience with his eyes and hand how much pleasure lay in this noble exercise. Meanwhile he himself while he was recovering his health would receive on behalf of the Emperor during the intervening days entertainment and feasts at the hands of the mandarins until His Majesty returned.

Now the Chinese New Year was approaching and meanwhile some things were said and done, as happens when people are not used to such situations, which might have annoyed the Emperor; so, the Fathers had their hearts in their mouths fearing that the imperial munificence might be lessened during the distribution of gifts in the solemn celebrations of the New Year. And their fear was increased when the last day for such presents approached. However the Emperor through his Chief Chamberlain and several mandarins ordered in his name a gift such as he did not normally give even to the leading princes or his sons, that there should be brought to the Lord Patriarch the following: a sturgeon weighing 180 Chinese pounds,[^p51-23] a variety of fishes brought from many thousands of furlongs around, a boar, deer, and pheasants, all on a table heavy with silverware as well as the feast. Only those who have witnessed in China exchanges of gifts between noble friends can appreciate the splendour associated with such an act of royal liberality, accompanied by a long procession of porters in red costumes with yellow insignia, and accompanied by, on horseback, the Chief Eunuch, many mandarins, and Europeans, all lending solemnity to the gifts being brought to His Excellency. On February 26th, now that the celebrations for the New Year were ending, the Most Illustrious Lord was invited to the royal gardens to watch the fireworks. Everything, <!-- PDF page 52 | book page 42 --> moreover, the Emperor wanted to be arranged to suit the convenience and infirmity of the Lord Patriarch. He ordered him to be carried within the walls of the gardens. He allotted the place where he should sit. After the fireworks came dancing and a eunuch orchestra usually only heard in the inner quarters of the queens. Finally, since night had already fallen, he gave him hospitality in the house in the quarters allotted to his two mandarins of the third rank.

With the opening of spring, the Emperor went hunting water birds which gather together in huge numbers in a lake in the province of Beizhili[^p52-24] preparatory to forming flocks to make the long journey to Tartary to avoid the heat. This expedition of the court lasted as long as that in the winter and the Lord Patriarch received the favours of the King, imperial feasts every two or three days, which were sent to him through his son on the orders of the father. Since His Excellency had earlier said he would take himself off to the hot springs at a suitable time, as the heat of May was approaching the Emperor advised him not to let slip the opportunity of a chance of recuperating his health and appointed a mandarin to accompany him to see to procuring a house etc. There through the same man he made frequent inquiries about his improving health and finally sometime after June 10th invited him to a final audience.

The Most Illustrious Lord now prepared to come into the Emperor’s presence from the hot springs. However a new crisis in his health occurred rendering His Illustriousness incapable of enduring a public meeting. The Emperor was very annoyed at this sudden change for the worse and fearfully ordered the mandarin of the third rank and his substitutes to be present day and night in our house and to report daily. When he heard confirmation that there was hope of his recovery, he sent presents, as is usually the case in China when someone is convalescing: 15 rolls of silk and a whole pound of the precious root called ginseng, because His Majesty was about to go away to the colder parts of Tartary and the Patriarch meanwhile to the warmer South.

His Illustriousness did not want to let slip the opportunity of quietly seeing the emperor and his sons one last time. He urgently requested an audience. On June 29th it was granted while the courtiers and the Fathers had not yet left the Palace. The Emperor himself came into the city in the morning and conducted proceedings with full honours. The Lord Patriarch was led by the mandarins through the great audience halls to the hall named “Recreating the Heart.”[^p52-25]

<!-- PDF page 53 | book page 43 -->

Here because of his weakness he was given permission to take some nourishments from the food supply he had brought with him, to give him strength while his companions shared another table; then he was led inside, by a special dispensation on account of his poor health, for an extraordinary audience. He was ordered again to speak frankly after making the nine genuflexions and prostrations held up by the arms of Fathers Pereira and Gerbillon. Present were the Crown Prince and the first, ninth, and thirteenth sons with a small number of attendants and eunuchs. His Illustriousness thanked the Emperor for the accumulated benefices and they finished with a familiar conversation. By order of the Emperor the main buildings of the Palace were shown to him and he was invited to come the next day to see the Emperor’s Villa and the garden of the Crown Prince. Early in the morning he was taken there by three mandarins, given a banquet on his arrival, and after the meal taken for an inspection and invited to a discussion. After the discussion the royal barge carried him around to inspect the delights of the Villa and the promise of new gifts for the Pontiff was renewed. The Lord Patriarch had not yet left the walls of the Imperial Villa when it was announced to him that the Crown Prince was personally waiting for him in his garden, and he received his European guest with similar honours. I should add firstly that the Lord Patriarch was allowed to enter the walls in his sedan chair; secondly that the Prince accompanied us in another boat, sometimes preceding us as if to show us the way, sometimes, as it were, accompanying us at our side; and thirdly that he not only shared with the Lord Patriarch his private recreation grounds but also refreshed us with various delicacies hitherto unknown to us, and after an audience the Lord Patriarch was dismissed, applauding and loudly praising the building. One could certainly say that there must have been many who, stung in the pride innate to them, could scarcely bear to see such benevolent familiarity of His Majesty to a visiting European, and who in the records of the Empire would judge it unworthy when they recorded the head of the Empire behaving so amicably towards a foreigner.

Good reader, do not imagine this is mentioned without good reason, nor that God did not have good reasons for disposing that the Fathers should be favourably regarded in the eyes of the Empire so that their humble prayers could be of some service to the Lord Patriarch. There should be no envy, no clouds of thanks from others depositing a rain of honeyed words, since it is the work of God. It was done for this reason: that the Church of China should receive an increase.

<!-- PDF page 54 | book page 44 -->

I have no time for those who reject all human means. God wills that men live like humans and uses humans to attract men to his purposes. Whatever the Emperor bestowed upon the Legate of the Vicar of Christ in the Chinese fashion was nothing but golden chains, drawing non-believers into the flock of Christ, and hence not only desirable but to be actively sought. It could for the most religious of motives be accepted, and provided only it was not otherwise impeded would provide ample hope of an infinite good to the benefit of our holy religion if he favours us to the end as he has up until now. The Emperor could be said to have favoured us to the end since for two whole months, although he was offended, he ordered the supply of food to be continued and the Patriarch himself to be carried back to the city of Guangzhou on the backs of others. For the sake of brevity I omit the many other benefits bestowed during proceedings since they are described more authoritatively in the letters from Beijing of the Most Illustrious Lord himself to His Holiness.

### 3. The Lord Patriarch’s actions regarding the controversies about Chinese Rites

When the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch was about to arrive, in letters sent to await him at the port, the Superiors in Beijing asked him to choose two or three learned missionaries on each side of the controversies and in their presence to interrogate the more learned Christians about Chinese customs, not in one place only but throughout China. To this end they ordered to stay in Canton Frs. Raymundo[^p54-26] and Beauvollier, one Spanish and the other French, so they could be at hand to assist His Illustriousness. When all had been done by our procurators according to their instructions the Most Illustrious Lord in reply ordered them to write to Beijing that a decree had already been made in Rome.[^p54-27] It only remained for the superiors to see to it that the contrary practices were introduced by their men and so the controversies would disappear of themselves and any condemnation would be avoided. However his every action showed that he intended to reserve to himself the whole matter so that everyone would agree with what he laid down. The advice of His Illustriousness was all very well but impracticable. Indeed, if it could have been put into practice why for so many years had the Jesuits allowed their adversaries to <!-- PDF page 55 | book page 45 --> defame them and sent so many of their people to Rome, at such great expense and through so many dangers? It was a matter of the salvation of souls, not of refuting arguments. A thousand opinions can be forgotten if but one soul can profit by the precious blood of Christ. Since indeed the Jesuits are completely persuaded that if the opposite practice prevailed the mission could not survive, they have up until now fought for their view. Until a decree of the Holy See is issued which they will firmly adhere to, it is certain that all would either lay down their lives in obedience to the Holy Church or leave the mission as God or His vicar should determine.

Then in Canton Fr. Beauvollier produced a memorandum requesting that the Most Illustrious Lord should examine the main points for both sides in due legal form and summarize them in writing. The Most Illustrious Lord was not displeased with this request but he thought it enough that a procurator for the Lord Bishop of Conon[^p55-28] should respond and that everything should be sent to the Roman tribunal, and he asked for the documents of his procurators if any parts of them existed in Fujian. His Illustriousness also accepted from Fr. Visdelou[^p55-29] a treatise in juridical form which he wished to present against the practice of the Jesuits, and ordered it to be informally passed on to Fr. Beauvollier by the same Fr. Visdelou, if he cared to do so. About the same time, the Most Illustrious Lord learnt from the Most Reverend Lord Bishop of Ascalon[^p55-30] of a book written some time before but recently enlarged by Father Bouvet which he had sent to the President of the Doctors of Beijing[^p55-31] in order to obtain his opinion about whether he had correctly interpreted the texts, sayings, and dialogues <!-- PDF page 56 | book page 46 --> he had collected without diverging from the genuine understanding of the Literary School. In his work he tried to demonstrate that both the ancient and modern Chinese understood by tian, or Heaven, the true and living Lord of Heaven. And when he had obtained the approbation of the President of this College of Doctors regarding Chinese matters, he sent it to be censored regarding dogma to the Bishop of Ascalon with a view to presenting it for approval to be printed. The Lord Patriarch, when he came across this book condemned it, relying on the judgment of two of our adversaries despite the Lord Bishop of Ascalon contradicting them with all his force. He ordered the Lord Bishop of Beijing[^p56-32] to compel the Fathers to surrender all copies and even the woodblocks and to swear that no other copies or blocks existed. Since however the work had never been published this whole affair depended on an error of our two adversaries, who were too ignorant to distinguish between a manuscript and a printed copy.[^p56-33] They were afraid of a book to which they could not reply. When he learned of this error the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch did nothing to make up for his mistake except to return the book to the Reverend Bishop of Ascalon with instructions that he was to show it to no living person. So, while he was at Guangzhou, only one disputation was allowed by the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch, that between Fr. Visdelou and Fr. Raymundo. In this earlier one he reluctantly conceded only this much: that our other adversaries had openly said that they did not want him to rely on their responses alone, and that they were all agreed that he should call on the Most Reverend Bishop of Conon to defend his case himself. So he did not proceed with a second disputation.

Furthermore, when the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch learned that we had begun to build a new church in Beijing, that the old one had been dismantled, and that on account of the destruction of the old church the Jing Tian tablet had been meanwhile placed elsewhere, absolutely forbade that the said tablet should be hung up in the new church. This was despite the fact that meanwhile a different determination might come from the Supreme Pontiff about the signification and usage of the word tian or Heaven.

After he had arrived in Beijing on the occasion of the decease of his chamberlain, the Lord Patriarch introduced some novelties, albeit ones scarcely related to the controversies even indirectly. However the eyes of the Emperor were watching him closely from that moment on, since he wanted to understand what the Visitor of the Holy Law was about; since His Majesty noticed he was <!-- PDF page 57 | book page 47 --> inclined to make changes and persistent in carrying out what he thought up, before long he began to feel anxious to know what exactly the Lord Patriarch intended to change among the followers of the Holy Law.

So, on the third day after the burial of the chamberlain, on the very day of the birth of Our Saviour, he sent two mandarins who asked the Lord Patriarch whether while he was still living in Europe he had heard about the Emperor’s declaration which they, the mandarins present, had received from His Majesty and taken away to the Europeans. The Lord Patriarch replied that at that time one copy had come, and although this was not authenticated nobody had doubted what it contained.[^p57-34] There had remained some difference of opinion since some doubted whether those Europeans in Beijing had presented such a matter to His Majesty sufficiently well and clearly enough. The mandarins invited the Lord Patriarch to explain this more fully. Finally they urged him to tell them how he now understood the declaration. Sabino Mariani and Ludovico Appiani[^p57-35] were terrified when they heard this, and with choking voices and wild gestures and stamping feet in the presence of eight Jesuits and other companions attempted to prevent the Lord Patriarch from answering. Fr. Mariani was unable to contain himself and burst out with the following: they want to pierce the wall! And much was said to persuade His Excellency to plead that his illness had rendered him incapable of dealing with such matters and that no further urging would persuade him to get involved in this business. The Lord Patriarch prudently responded that his health and life were at the disposal of His Majesty and when finally he let slip the remark that he could reply in writing, the mandarins left to allow him to write what he wanted to write. The above mentioned advisors prevented him from writing. When the mandarins returned they found the door closed against them. Some things were said about the matter through Fr. Appiani but regarding the Emperor’s declaration he said that the Jesuit Fathers had no business raising such matters with the Emperor, since they were of inferior rank, and that Europe marvelled that they had the audacity to do so. The next day after the mandarin reported this to the Emperor he told us—we believe with the Emperor’s knowledge—that [the Lord Patriarch] had informed the Emperor that he was under pain of death not to repeat to others without His Majesty’s permission what he had under secrecy been entrusted to refer to His Majesty. December 26th. The mandarins <!-- PDF page 58 | book page 48 --> who had been sent by the emperor were almost excluded by Fr. Appiani, who claimed that the Lord Patriarch had taken medicine and could not see visitors. When they were finally admitted they insisted absolutely on something being written down about the business upon which the Most Illustrious Lord had come to China. His Illustriousness did write something but mentioned not a word about either the Emperor’s declaration or regarding the matters disputed among the missionaries. More will be said about what he wrote in the next section. When on 31 December, with unprecedented condescension, the Emperor himself admitted the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch to his presence and asked several times the reason for his coming and the business that brought him here, and said explicitly that he should tell him absolutely everything, he gave not the slightest indication that it included the meaning of the books and rites of the Chinese which had been in dispute for almost a century.

However, when on the first day of the year of Christ 1706 the mandarins returned to again ask the Lord Patriarch about the meaning of the imperial declaration, one insisted on being told why the Lord Patriarch thought such matters were unclear in China when he himself had sought an explanation from his Lord, who was more intelligent than other men and by hard study and long inquiry of the most learned men in the Empire both about letters and customs had penetrated the meaning of the matters and given the aforesaid opinion. Hence they said the Emperor himself requested that if there was anything missing in the declaration he should be asked to fill it in. But they got no answer except that the Lord Patriarch replied that when his health allowed and he had leisure to do so he would carefully deal with these matters.

Almost the same response had been given earlier to Fr. Kilian Stumpf, who had been appointed together with Fr. Régis[^p58-36] as Procurator in matters relating to the controversies, for when dealing with the Lord Patriarch through his auditor Fr. Sabino there was only one response: the Fathers must wait until the Most Illustrious Lord was better. Meanwhile they should prepare their application but make it short, since if they produced a lot, it could be that the Fathers would lose everything. But what Fr. Sabino said had no authority in this matter. January 2nd. The Lord Patriarch granted Fr. Kilian a private interview in which he was bold enough to argue that His Illustriousness should not refuse to listen to the Emperor on the meaning of the texts and rites since it would be useless to ask Europeans about the interpretations of Chinese in the past, <!-- PDF page 59 | book page 49 --> since none of them could reach even the bachelor’s degree in China if they were to be examined there by the Literary Tribunal. Our European adversaries too can scarcely make a case unless they produce the testimony of the Chinese themselves. The Most Illustrious Lord serenely replied that he could not ask the Emperor because his authority was so great that he could not be contradicted. As for the testimony of other Chinese it should certainly be sought, but of the dead not the living—who, for the sake of their reputation, influenced by this or that, say whatever seems opportune, not what they really think. So it is clear where the principle of Fr. Visdelou, to admit only books, comes from; it would be in the interest of the Lord Patriarch. Our adversaries want to admit books only, and to reserve to themselves alone the right to interpret them.

Meanwhile time was slipping away and nothing was being done about the controversies. On seeing this the Fathers asked the Reverend Lord Bishop of Beijing that His Lordship, using the excuse of the Lord Patriarch’s illness, should get the Lord Patriarch’s permission to take on the specific task which the Most Illustrious Lord himself was unable to perform. We made our request by presenting him with a written petition. The Most Reverend Lord Bishop happily promised he would take on the work and on January 22nd made the request in the terms discussed, but added a further request that during the illness of the Lord Patriarch he should entrust to the Most Reverend Lord Bishop the documents from our archive to recognize them in due legal form. When he received the request the Lord Patriarch denied that he would commit anything to anybody else and wished to do everything himself, instead ordering us to present him with whatever we wanted to show him. Accordingly, on the 29th of the same month the Fathers presented him with a second document, petitioning in particular for juridical recognition of the Emperor’s declaration, and other documents, half of which were described in a catalogue, then containing 45 items, which they offered to him.

When the Most Illustrious Lord had seen the application, he immediately reacted by summoning the Lord Bishop of Conon to deign to be present in Beijing within four months to verify the authenticity of the Emperor’s declaration. The procurators of the Fathers protested that this summons seemed unworkable because of the distance involved etc. His Illustriousness could decide the issue without the presence of the Most Reverend Bishop of Conon, who was neither accused by us nor accused us, but if the Lord Patriarch were to persist with his proposal it was very likely that four or five months would elapse before we could begin to do anything regarding the controversies.

We patiently awaited the decision of the Lord Commissary Apostolic to attend to those matters with which he had come to deal. Although we had no idea when he would begin to deal seriously with the controversies he still <!-- PDF page 60 | book page 50 --> would give no sign to us of what we were to do. Before the second application I have already mentioned was made on January 19th, the mandarin Henkama had, when he was presenting the food offered by the Emperor, two or three times raised with the Lord Patriarch the matter of the meaning of Tian and Shangdi as interpreted by the books and the commentators, as is discussed in the appendix to the diary.[^p60-37] The Most Illustrious Lord in conversation with his people commented as if he had vanquished the mandarin, which was what he wanted to believe. However on the third of January he complained bitterly to Fr. Kilian that the mandarin had been urged on by us and was a bother to him. The mandarin was not prompted by us, nor could he have been: who could have suborned a man who waited daily on the Emperor and had been commissioned specially on each occasion to speak on the side of the Fathers when views were raised which he knew to be contrary to the understanding and practice of the Literary School. I am unable to say if His Illustriousness did triumph or if he had reason to rejoice in his victory. On February 5th the Lord Patriarch requested that we hand to him the Latin translation and the Manchu text of the 1700 declaration of the Emperor with the attestations of the Fathers who knew Manchu, together with a complete catalogue of all the documents; both were respectfully presented with some hope that the question of the controversies would be dealt with. But, not completely unhappily, he complained that without warning a catalogue of ninety documents had been presented to him which contained more and stronger armaments; a lesser amount would have made a stronger case. This was what Fr. Sabino had said earlier, as I have already recounted.

The beginning of the Chinese New Year was the occasion for many Christians to come to meet the Lord Patriarch who now feeling somewhat better received them. At first he addressed them from a distance about their ancestor tablets and the cult of their ancestors. Around February 21st the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch spoke something about this matter according to Fr. Appiani. However, the former could not understand the responses of the Chinese, nor what Fr. Appiani said in Chinese to the Chinese, and had to believe his account of what was said and what the reply was relying on the report he received in Italian from his interpreter.

On February 28th the Christians began to complain at the three churches about the propositions that had been presented to them by Fr. Appiani, but the Fathers begged them not to say a word in reply until the crowd had been dismissed and they could obtain more certain knowledge about the subject of their complaints. When the Christians insisted that they were seriously disturbed, the Fathers had recourse to the Most Reverend Bishop of Beijing as their Ordinary.[^p61-38] On March 1st they consulted him about what was best to say to the malcontents to avoid causing further scandal. The Most Reverend Bishop of Beijing replied that many of the Christians were picaros,[^p61-39] inconstant people who said one thing to the Lord Patriarch and something else to others. The misleading questions of Fr. Appiani and the silver crosses and little Roman presents could elicit any sort of answer from the Christians. For the rest, what has been said up until now in the name of the Most Illustrious Lord has not yet created any obligation; we should only beware lest we precipitate a decree which he, relying on the plenitude of his powers, should try to enforce. March 7th. The Christians presented their difficulties to Fr. Appiani and to Fr. Frossoloni,[^p61-40] who told them that they must obey the Lord Patriarch. There was nothing to fear; everything had arranged with the Emperor and there was a zhi or decree of the Emperor that the Christians could do what they pleased. The Christians requested to see this mandate and they offered to get it from the mandarin Henkama, which Fathers Frossoloni and Appiani duly did. But they returned saying that it was an implicit decree since the Emperor knew about it and had not contradicted it, and this is the sole basis for our adversaries denying that there is any danger. But they would find out the truth in the month of August and after. When the Christians found out there was no real decree and realized they would get nowhere with either of the interpreters, they decided to offer the Lord Patriarch a petition pointing out what the laws obliged them to do, what they prohibited, and how much danger they were in. Their first one was accepted but a second one was torn up contemptuously by Fr. Frossoloni after <!-- PDF page 62 | book page 52 --> the Christians had been ordered more than once to go away for being disobedient and boldly opposing the Great Lord (for such was the title the Christians gave the Lord Patriarch).

May 16th. The Emperor asked Fr. Gerbillon what the Christians were discussing with the Lord Patriarch. When the Father avoided answering, the Emperor asked: did he not realize that all the followers of the True Law would be condemned if he were to try to abolish those rites? The Father passed this on to the Most Reverend Bishop of Beijing and then to the Lord Patriarch, who replied that he did not order but recommended that such rites should be abandoned. If any of the Christians were upset, that was the fault of the Fathers since when he arrived in China he had advised them to get rid of them, and up until now they had not wanted to do so.

March 21st. Fr. Gerbillon introduced a few Christian pilgrims to the Lord Patriarch to receive his blessing. When His Illustriousness came out to meet them suddenly there arrived some one hundred Christians who fell on their knees and held up high five petitions addressed to him containing propositions about the abolition of their native rites. The Lord Patriarch asked the Christians through Fathers Appiani and Frossoloni whether they had first shown these documents to the Jesuit Fathers. They replied that this was their affair, not the Fathers’, and so the petitions had not been shown by them to the Fathers. The Lord Patriarch refused to accept them unless they were subscribed by the Fathers and the Christians denied to him that in this kind of affair they were acting in common with the Fathers. Finally he asked what their petition was about. One old man replied that Fr. Appiani had ordered them to destroy all the tablets of their ancestors etc. Fr. Appiani on hearing his name mentioned cried out in a loud voice: “This old fellow is lying.” Then the Lord Patriarch ordered the petitions to be collected by a servant, and when they were collected he tried to tear them up; however, since his hands were still crippled by his illness his attempt failed, so he ordered Fr. Gerbillon, who was standing by, to finish what he had begun. The Father refused, pleading that he should not perform such an inappropriate act in the presence of the Christians; the Lord Patriarch, throwing down the petition, trampled on it before the Christians and ordered that each and every one of these petitioners should be excluded from the house as long as he himself should remain there. This act filled the hearts of the Christians with an incredible bitterness.

Hence Fr. Kilian followed Fr. Frossoloni, informing him that it was certain that the Emperor would get to hear of it and would ask Fr. Gerbillon, Fr. Pereira,[^p62-41] and the courtiers about it. One of the courtiers named Wu, who had <!-- PDF page 63 | book page 53 --> been baptized as a child, wanted all of his family to swear to abandon the Christian religion on no other grounds than that the Emperor knew the Lord Patriarch wanted to exterminate the rites of the country for the dead etc., and that the Emperor was angry and would certainly take action against those who abandoned the rites. Fr. Frossoloni said the Lord Patriarch had earlier been more flexible but since the middle of the month had made up his mind because he had received letters via Manila from a certain abbate[^p63-42] who was his procurator in Rome looking after his affairs, announcing that a decree had been put together, although he did not know what the decree contained. The Father objected that if a decree had been agreed on, why did they not wait until it arrived? If the decree prohibited the rites the Jesuits were not afraid to die amongst the gentiles in obedience to the Holy See. If the decree does not prohibit the rites it is very inadvisable, on mere suspicion and relying on a chance remark by some abbate or other, to want to push the mission to its ruination. March 23rd. The Lord Patriarch, as if casually, asked Fr. Gerbillon for news circulating in Beijing and the Father replied that a rumour had spread from the city to the Emperor that the rites for the dead etc. had been prohibited by His Illustriousness, and that therefore the Emperor had said that His Illustriousness was a disturber of the peace and had come to overthrow the Christian Law. The Lord Patriarch replied that he had not prohibited such rites and that he wanted the Fathers to take upon themselves the task of keeping the Christians quiet. To which the Father said they could perhaps silence Tom, Dick, and Harry but it was not in their power to silence several thousand. The first consideration should be to avoid unnecessarily exciting dangerous moves.

March 24th. Fr. Soares[^p63-43] was warned that certain Christians had agreed amongst themselves that since the Lord Patriarch had behaved so harshly by trampling on their written petition, which contained the laws of China and the names of God and of the Emperor, they would refer the matter to the Ministry of Rites. The Father was horrified and hurried to the church—it was not a feast day—and forbade as many as he found there to leave but immediately exhorted them not to carry out what they planned since it was the suggestion of a demon. The fright he had received and the vehemence of his speech made the Father ill. We all had to spend half a month working to prevent such a risky undertaking, which the Christians referred to had projected first against the Lord Patriarch and then against Fr. Appiani alone.

However the Christians revealed their minds in great detail to the Most Reverend Bishop of Beijing and his interpreter Fr. Frossoloni on March 27th, arguing that if they could show them that the rites were evil they were prepared to obey even to death. But since the rites were not yet proven to be such the Christians wished to be heard. They could not prudently consent, without a just decision, to expose those already Christian to the penalties imposed by law, and be responsible for all Chinese to be excluded access to eternal happiness, on account of rites which cannot be proved to be evil and furthermore are duly prescribed by law.

During the month of April nothing was done openly, but secretly much was attempted in ways that were extremely dangerous. Amongst other things Fathers Appiani and Frossoloni tried everything to persuade as many as possible of the Christians that they had perjured themselves when they sent their sworn statement. This was based on the assertion that you have sworn that in China the opinion concerning the cult of Confucius and ancestors is not the erroneous opinion which some missionaries claim and yet there are men who hold such errors regarding the said cults. The major premise of the argument was false since the Christians had sworn that this erroneous opinion was not that of the Empire and explicitly cautioned that they did not deny that there may be some uneducated and ignorant people who did hold it. Nevertheless this was used as a kind of instrument of torture, to induce by exaggeration a feeling that they had committed a crime and create scruples in some simple-minded people with the result that two or three confessed to them that they had committed perjury and retracted it in writing. The distinguished confessors then were overjoyed and boasted of having obtained these written confessions. The ultimate case of such a confession is dealt with in detail under the month of May.

The Fathers wanted to return to the controversies in May but the fierce indignation of the Lord Patriarch against Fr. Bouvet etc. suspended action until the 15th, when a third petition was offered to the Most Illustrious Lord; it might be summed up as saying that more than one hundred days had passed since the Most Reverend Lord Bishop of Conon had been summonsed and no news had been had of his coming. Time was being uselessly wasted. Up until now the Fathers had not been allowed to go through the submissions which the Most Illustrious Lord had begun to collect through Fr. Appiani. The Fathers did not refuse to deal with the said Fr. Appiani but requested that His Excellency should also suffer that one of our men be present at the examination and the recording of proceedings because Appiani was an open opponent of ours, was strongly suspect to the Christians, and also, as the Lord Patriarch was fully aware, was not perfectly master of the language. If, indeed, he wanted to place confidence <!-- PDF page 65 | book page 55 --> in the person and information provided by Fr. Appiani alone, he would be seen to be asserting that the huge and most holy efforts of Pope Clement XI were useless. The Lord Patriarch accepted the petition but was not happy with it. He ordered it to be kept in his files but that Fr. Kilian should offer another.

On May 18th our catechist John Lu, a man with a licentiate degree, attempted, but was not admitted, to present to the Lord Patriarch a certain petition about the case of his confession, which was as follows. When the catechist was approaching the communion rail in the church a certain Christian whispered in his ear inquiring whether he had confessed to the perjury which both of them had committed. He remembered that the two spiritual fathers Appiani and Frossoloni had shown them how serious this was. This simple and upright man, disturbed by such a denunciation, turned back and examined himself but could not remember the exact words of the oath. He went away to make his confession to Fr. Appiani. The latter requested him to put his confession into writing without telling him why he wanted this done. The penitent thought his confessor wanted it in order to better understand what it was about. So he wrote it down, handed it over in good faith, was absolved, and went off. Fr. Appiani took away the writing in the hand of the penitent to the Lord Patriarch, who showed it to the Christians to inspire them with fear and as an example of repentance.

The Christians, knowing the falsity of the argument presented by Fr. Appiani and at the same time indignant at his way of acting during the sacrament of confession, determined to repeat their oath. They informed John Lu what had happened to his writing. He thought again about the matter, and when he read again with his own eyes the form of the oath they had sworn he recognized his error and regretted what he had done. He had confessed to nothing in what he wrote except what he had actually sworn, hence his retraction was purely imaginary, as he was pleased to discover. Fearing that his fictitious retraction should in future be used to damage his reputation he tried to get it back from Fr. Appiani, who refused to return it to him on the grounds that it had already been entered into the archives. He attempted to offer another document in its place. When Fr. Appiani refused to accept it the Lord Patriarch ordered him to present it, but he was shut out that day and on the 19th he was ordered to wait until June. When the month of July was a quarter way through he offered it again but His Excellency refused it. Finally on bended knees and touching his head on the ground he placed the said document at the foot of the crucifix. The Lord Patriarch was indignant, violently threw him out of the room and ordered his servants to beat him, which he only avoided by fleeing.

On June 3rd Fr. Kilian paid his compliments to the Lord Patriarch, who was staying at the hot springs, and brought with him a fourth petition in which he <!-- PDF page 66 | book page 56 --> pointed out that the date set in the summons to the Lord Bishop of Conon had already passed. Immediately the Lord Patriarch refused to receive Fr. Kilian as the procurator, so that the petition could not be presented. Meanwhile a rumour of the coming of the Lord Bishop of Conon was spreading. When asked several times in a public audience on June 29th if he had any further business, the Lord Patriarch denied it each time. However he asked His Majesty for any requests he might have for the Supreme Pontiff.

On June 3rd in his Villa, in the presence of all the companions of the Lord Patriarch and all the Jesuits, the Emperor said face to face to the Most Illustrious Lord: “Yesterday you asked if I had any orders for you. I, the Emperor, can think of nothing more to be added. You should simply tell the Supreme Pontiff that he should take note that in China for two thousand years we have reverently followed the teaching of Confucius, and that from the time when Fr. Ricci and others arrived here and during the more than forty years I have reigned— that is, for some two hundred years—the Europeans have lived without blame and without deviance, in great peace and tranquility. If in future the men of the true religion should hold even a single point which departs from the teaching of Confucius, Europeans will with difficulty remain in China.” That evening His Majesty ordered the same message to be given to His Illustriousness. On this occasion, the Lord Patriarch ordered that the Emperor should be told through Fr. Gerbillon as interpreter, in the hearing of everyone present, that there was a man of mature age and good morals, of episcopal status and notable virtue, with an outstanding knowledge of Chinese letters and affairs, whom he had on that account called to the throne to satisfy His Majesty on those matters in dispute among European missionaries, and who had arrived here yesterday. The Lord Patriarch had already notified the Emperor of this coming arrival through his intermediary, the mandarin Henkama, at the hot springs on June 1st. Fr. Appiani on June 16th did the same in our house, where Henkama was then living night and day by order of the Emperor on account of the Lord Patriarch’s illness. Hence the Emperor said he would be happy to talk to this man and promised that since he was about to depart for Tartary he would call the newcomer there for a discussion.

July 1st. The Lord Bishop of Conon was ordered by the Emperor to write down everything in Confucius which did not agree with the Christian Law, adding the reasons. The Lord Bishop at first demurred and then, avoiding the points in dispute, wrote a few things; in the second of these he said that the sacrifice which the Emperor was accustomed to offer to Heaven was evil. The mandarins were deeply offended by this statement and burst out in threats. His Majesty had said directly to the Lord Patriarch that he himself would declare what the Chinese Empire thought and did, and that he did not care whether or not it agreed with the Christian Law, which was a matter he left to the Supreme Pontiff.

### 4. The Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lord Patriarch deals in Beijing with certain business other than the controversies

The Emperor demanded, on 25 December 1705, that the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch should reveal to him formally the reason for his coming. However, those are mistaken who think that the fact that the missionaries were disputing about Chinese Rites was hidden from the Emperor. The people who stubbornly persuaded themselves of this are the very ones who for so many years have been stirring up public discussion of these matters. For good reasons they now tried to convince themselves, and half believed, that His Majesty was up to this point ignorant of the controversies since, over the long period the Jesuits had been warning strongly of the dangers to come, they had heatedly denied it. It was impossible that the Emperor should not know about such matters, but even if he did know since he said nothing, they argued, there can be no danger. However they didn’t distinguish cause and effect or acknowledge that time was moving on.[^p71-53] At the very least one can say that the Emperor had a distant knowledge, since he knew that five years before we had petitioned for that declaration about the Rites, but now he knew the Lord Patriarch had come to make a visitation and rightly concluded that he had come especially to establish peace and concord in doctrine.

On the 25th, as I have said, the mandarins sent by the Emperor announced that His Majesty had noted that the Lord Patriarch had travelled nine thousand leagues amid so many inconveniences and dangers, from which he had concluded that the reason must have been serious for such serious labours. He wanted him to open up to him about this. The Lord Patriarch replied that his business was in the name of the Supreme Pontiff who could not come himself: to offer well deserved thanks for the royal favours towards the mission and missionaries and to humbly beg his Majesty to continue such favours by watching over the Europeans who came from so far away and by protecting the Gospel with his continued goodwill. Fathers Sabino and Appiani wanted the Lord Patriarch to say little in reply and, especially, to say nothing about the imperial declaration to the mandarins who were inquiring about it. Nevertheless, the Lord Patriarch himself, although he declined to speak at length in public about anything else, finally agreed to tell them what he thought in private that very same day.

On the 26th the mandarins insisted that the business entrusted to him should be written down to be shown to the Emperor and that after he had finished this report in Italian there should be a further discussion in private.

<!-- PDF page 72 | book page 62 -->

After the document had been written and its contents revealed, the Lord Patriarch let slip a few details regarding the matters, not including the controversies, which had been covered in the secret conversation. That evening, speaking to Fr. Gerbillon, he said he had been obliged to reveal to the Emperor the complete program of his visitation, and that the Emperor had vouched for the innocence of the Beijing Fathers but had given him permission to make a visitation of other missionaries throughout the provinces. This was before the Italian report was translated.

On the morning of the 27th the mandarins announced in general terms that in their opinion the business was finished, and asked whether there was anything else the Lord Patriarch might wish to add before the report was translated into Manchu. By this one word “finished,” which the interpreter misunderstood, he gave false expectations to the Lord Patriarch, since he interpreted it as if it referred to everything in the report. That he believed this to be the case he later said to others including Fr. Kilian. He extended the meaning of this word used by the mandarins as if it meant everything had been agreed. This he reported to the Fathers in the Palace, and these in turn to the mandarins so that they could spread the news as certain. He denied and so did his associate that he had said anything other than what the Chinese had said, or something like it—i.e. that it was almost finished, as good as conceded or something of the sort. The mandarins disagreed: “We deny that we said the business was finished since the report is in your language and has not yet begun to be translated, and the Emperor knows no more than is contained in our summary account.”[^p72-54] In the report, however, there was nothing other than expressions of thanks and courtesies—except the following, which I quote here from the report itself: “Since I have a duty of obedience to your imperial orders, I add that such is the solicitude of His Holiness for the health of Your Majesty that he is desirous to have correspondence with this Court and have someone who will continuously report on the prosperous state of the Royal Person, and let him know anything in which he can be of assistance or which does not meet with Your Majesty’s approval. To which end it would help if there were here also established a person of prudence, integrity, and learning who would also be superior for all the Europeans, in order to satisfy the desire of His Beatitude, the service <!-- PDF page 73 | book page 63 --> of Your Majesty, and the perfect regulation of this mission, to which the patronage, example, and loving teachings of charity from Your Majesty give such encouragement.”[^p73-55] These words, together with the rest of the same document, was translated into Manchu but not yet written out in a clean copy when the Emperor, very desirous to see it, cast his eyes over it and said: “This is a lot of nonsense. Isn’t there anything else to be presented?” The mandarins were astonished at the Emperor’s perspicacity and bowing down before him apologized for having nothing more serious to offer.

December 29th. The mandarins returned late in the morning on instructions from His Majesty to say that for such a position of general superior, it would be necessary to choose a man who had lived in China for at least ten years and who was not inexperienced in the customs of this country; they added further arguments very pertinent to the matter and hard to refute. The Lord Patriarch was badly affected by such news and, whether because of his illness or his natural disposition, began to say very irritably that yesterday everything had been granted but now was denied. This could only be because the Emperor had been listening to others. Fr. Pereira, who foresaw how badly this would sound in the Kingdom of China, intervened to warn His Excellency not to touch on the person of His Majesty, who had in fact granted nothing whatsoever nor taken anything back but was rather proposing conditions to be further discussed. The Lord Patriarch, badly misestimating the extent of his authority, said that he would not put up with anyone interrupting him when he was speaking and ordered that his remarks should be interpreted to the mandarins.

Fathers Gerbillon and Pereira, who were present on the orders of His Majesty, remained silent since they were certain from long experience how badly such a comment would be received, and for that reason held back and suggested that nothing further should be said. Finally, at the wish of the Lord Patriarch, Fr. Appiani conveyed to the mandarins what the Lord Patriarch had replied. Hardly had Fr. Appiani uttered a word when the mandarins began to rage, filled with sorrow and anger, shouting out that their Great Lord had been insulted. They said the whole world would be amazed that their Lord should be exposed to such ridicule and ignorant abuse from one who was so inconsistent and changeable, now saying one thing and now another, and so on. So that they could vent their anger they removed to another place, filling heaven and earth with their justifiable complaints against a barbarian newcomer. Fathers Pereira and Gerbillon remained with the Lord Patriarch to suggest to him <!-- PDF page 74 | book page 64 --> humbly but in a friendly way that in this Court it was needful to act differently. But His Illustriousness angrily and in tears cast aspersions on Fr. Pereira, pronouncing him guilty and ordering that he should be examined under oath by his auditor. He contemptuously accused him of having laboured for thirty years serving the Emperor with his hands. The Father, remaining as cold as marble, briefly, calmly, and reverently began to excuse himself but was dragged out of the room by the arms of the auditor.

The Emperor first heard what had happened through the eunuch who brought the report of the mandarins to the place where he was hunting. That evening he severely rebuked Fr. Appiani through a chamberlain so that the Lord Patriarch would learn how much he had offended against the reverence due to His Chinese Majesty. But, alas, the whole business failed through these few words of the Lord Patriarch.

On the 29th the Emperor, conversing with his people about this case, said: “Our new guest suspects the old Europeans want to grab this position of superior but this is false. Quite apart from the fact that such an office has no standing with us Chinese and Tartars, it would just cause them enormous problems, for when the others wanted anything this person would have to take care of the affair in Beijing. I know these old people of ours; you can be certain that none of them want to take on such a burden.” Then he advised us through his gobetweens that we should protest to the Lord Patriarch that none of us would accept such a post of superior, and he added that he would regard anyone who accepted such an office as a nobody. At the positive urging of His Majesty we submitted a protestation in writing this same day to the Father Auditor, but the following day he returned it.

December 30th. This protestation to the Most Illustrious Lord said that we had no designs of the sort that His Illustriousness attributed to us, nor would any of us accept such an office of superior even if the Emperor should urge us to accept under pain of death. The Lord Patriarch accepted this “formally,” as he said, acting as a papal legate with all of us on our knees before him, kissing his hand, and then hearing it read out aloud. Then he gave judgment. He knew he said that some or one of us of us had subverted his negotiations with the Emperor; we should reflect on what we had done in resisting the intentions of the Supreme Pontiff and the Holy Church. He wanted to establish a man to conduct correspondence between the Roman court and the court of Beijing for the benefit of the mission etc. We listened in silence then all departed equally in silence, not uttering a single word.

A second affair arose out of the first. The only happy aspect of this business was that it was all finished within twenty-four hours. After Fathers Gerbillon and Pereira heard His Illustriousness say, when he had heard our protestation, <!-- PDF page 75 | book page 65 --> that the Supreme Pontiff, for the good of the mission, wanted a man to manage the interchange of letters between Rome and Beijing, they decided to refer this matter to the Emperor, who they thought would more easily grant it since there did not seem to be as many reasons against it as had been in the imperial mind on the question of a general superior. They raised the matter at the court with the Chief Chamberlain, who passed it on to the Emperor. At this point the Emperor decided to receive the Lord Patriarch himself in audience the following day.

December 31st. The Lord Patriarch despite his illness was received very kindly before the Emperor, as has been described at considerable length earlier. The Emperor first inquired if he had any requests to make. His Illustriousness proposed the appointment of someone to handle the exchange of letters. This is easy, said His Majesty; it could be entrusted to one of the veteran Europeans in the Court. The Lord Patriarch objected that this person would have to be a man of confidence—known to the Pontiff and well-informed about Roman affairs, expert in the curial style—because the legates of all the courts came to Rome and learnt there how to handle the business of princes. This speech displeased the Emperor and his disagreement was interpreted as follows: “I know nothing about what you do but we don’t speak of a person of confidence; my people are expected to do their duty to the Empire whoever they may be. Some are closer to me, some in the middle distance, and some far away, but all are equally obliged to serve with confidentiality. Whether far off, near, or in the middle, I only have to say, do this, and they do it; none of them would dare to fall short in any service I entrust to them! Leave this business to me, but if you insist on choosing a new man who does not know how to address me or listen to me, who needs an interpreter, there will be suspicions and distrust.” His Illustriousness replied: “If I chose a man he would spend day and night learning the language.” The matter was allowed to lie there. The Emperor clearly forbade this and many other matters to be followed up, and so this piece of business was put to rest and concluded immediately.

In chronological order the third item of business to be narrated should be the legation of Frs. Bouvet and Sabino but this is tied up with the fifth item and is the most serious of all, so it may best be dealt with under one heading.

The fourth matter began early in February and was all over within a few days without any result. The Emperor was about to return from hunting and his goodwill towards the Lord Patriarch, despite the two recent cases, had been felt for 26 days. So Fr. Appiani, full of optimism and hope, sent to the home of the mandarin Henkama inviting him to come, since His Illustriousness had secrets to impart to His Majesty. The mandarin came and heard the proposal: the Lord Patriarch wished to buy a house in Beijing and requested that he be allowed <!-- PDF page 76 | book page 66 --> to buy one with his own money. Since the mandarin in the previous days had heard the Emperor say more than once that the Lord Patriarch distrusted the old Europeans and held grave suspicions about them, this astute minister, wishing to catch out His Illustriousness, claimed great interest in a business which was so difficult and which could only be successful through Tartar inner knowledge, only querying why he did not make such a request through the veteran Europeans. The Roman official at this time had no suspicions that the reasons for his hesitation, whether they were real or imaginary, were designed to get him to name those he distrusted.

The mandarin reported every last item to the Emperor as soon as he entered the audience hall. Then that very hour, with a serene countenance, he gave the news to Fr. Appiani, who was with us, that today was not the right time to announce something to the Emperor, who was tired; on some occasion in the near future which was more opportune, he would present the business on behalf of the Lord Patriarch. Although there was no hope, around about February 4th, the mandarin—feigning to speak on his own behalf and as a friend but actually on instructions of the Emperor—said to the Lord Patriarch: “Are you asking to buy a house? It would be just as easy to get permission to buy a house as to ask to be given a house by the Emperor. You see what he did when he gave a house to the old Europeans. He wants to do the same and even more for you. If you ask this favour of him be careful not to show any spirit of divisiveness, join with them in friendship and make the application through them. The Emperor will listen to them more willingly, for the Emperor hears only good things about you from them. You are a foreigner and ignorant of our ways. What European magnate would you recognize until you became confident of them by speaking to them? If you knew [the Jesuits] you would admit them to your counsels and accept that their advice about gaining the Emperor’s confidence has some efficacy.”

This piece of friendly advice was most acceptable to the Lord Patriarch, who expressed his gratitude to the mandarin and immediately ordered Fathers Grimaldi, Gerbillon, Antoine Thomas, and Pereira, through the mandarin and without the knowledge of the Emperor, to immediately seek audience at the palace on the following day, after they had come to hear what the Patriarch proposed. On February 5th three of the Fathers—Fr. Grimaldi was forced to stay home because of his chronic illness—duly came. His Illustriousness said he wanted the Fathers to go to seek a favour from the Emperor. The Fathers considered on the basis of precedents that there was no hope of such a task succeeding given the dispositions of the court, and replied respectfully that in this Court of China one cannot easily get a result in any business at the first attempt. Some time is required and all avenues explored; if they got a favourable <!-- PDF page 77 | book page 67 --> reception they would come back to faithfully report to the Lord Patriarch what had happened. He ordered the Fathers to go away to consult Fr. Grimaldi about the matter.

To satisfy the orders of the Emperor and the desires of the Patriarch the Fathers first went to the palace, since this was the shortest route to the College.[^p77-56] When the Emperor heard that the Fathers had nothing else to propose to him than a house he made clear that, by the warning he had given to Henken,[^p77-57] he had not intended that the Lord Patriarch should make requests through the Fathers but that he should consult the Fathers and be instructed by them about the reasons why, here and now, he should abstain from making a request like this. He spoke at length on this subject in the presence of the three Fathers and a number of the courtiers, and summed up by saying that the only reason for granting a house might be to please the Supreme Pontiff and the applause that would follow in Europe; but the most powerful argument to the contrary remained that it could not be certain of what nationality, what Order, or what kind of man would live in such a house as was being requested. If it was inferred from such a grant that the views of the Lord Patriarch now prevailed, this would be to provide a house where the lifestyle was not the same as that of those old Europeans. The same was valid for a single person if that person’s way of life was different: each would have to be given their own house, which would be inconvenient and disedifying to us who seeks nothing else than uniformity. Henkama, who was present, managed to get a word in: “What if you were to grant a house on condition that it would be common to all?” “That is absolutely out of the question,” replied the Emperor, and dismissing the Fathers he said to his people: “Don’t you see: first he asked for a general superior; when that was knocked back he asked for a man to conduct the exchange of letters; and when this was lost he asked for a house. Now he will go back from a house to the former, and then back to the first!”

Then he ordered that we should be told not to presume to make such a request. When he heard that we had gone away sad he ordered us to be told that we could still ask, but that it would not be granted. Then at last he sent a <!-- PDF page 78 | book page 68 --> third message to soften the blow, saying we were to be warned that we could speak about this matter after a month had gone by.

In this way the fourth item of business concluded and His Illustriousness privately complained with extreme bitterness about the three Fathers and with deep suspicion against the Jesuits. Finally he accepted one day a written testimony about the prohibition issued by the Emperor, as we have related above in the previous paragraph about the controversies.

The third business matter in order of time was about sending gifts to the Supreme Pontiff. This had a sorry outcome, as we will now see under the fifth item of business.

On January 1st, as we said under number 1, the Emperor made a definite decision that some gifts should be sent to the Supreme Pontiff and gave permission that the Lord Patriarch might choose one of his own people as the bearer; he named Fr. Sabino Mariani, his auditor. The mandarin who had charge of accompanying gifts and was very experienced in such matters represented to His Majesty that he was afraid some serious accident might occur in this affair, since Fr. Sabino neither could understand him nor be understood by him. The Emperor approved of providing some suitable assistance and said he would not only assign some European who knew the language as he had requested to accompany him as far as the port: it would also be altogether appropriate for His Majesty to send to the Supreme Pontiff, as a mark of increased honour, someone who was also a legate—one of those who assisted him in his court of Beijing.

On February 2nd he designated Fr. Joachim Bouvet as such a man and, having made the choice, the said gifts were taken by the Eunuch Chief Chamberlain and several mandarins to the Lord Patriarch the same morning. Fr. Bouvet was called there and the gifts were placed in the care of him and Fr. Sabino to be carried to the Supreme Pontiff. The Eunuch, knowing the will of his Lord, spoke to Fr. Bouvet alone and committed the gifts to him; nobody in the room doubted that the Father was the Emperor’s legate by mandate of the Emperor, was accompanied by Fr. Sabino by indulgence of the Emperor, and was not and could not be a legate except by an order of the Emperor.

On February 3rd both were admitted to the Emperor’s presence but to Fr. Bouvet alone did the Emperor give his instructions: that he was to inquire about the health of the Supreme Pontiff etc. When Fr. Sabino requested that the Emperor give him written credentials he explicitly denied them to him, although the custom is to bestow in writing the mandate by which a legate is appointed. Frs. Gerbillon, Parrenin, Bouvet, etc. pointed this out to the Lord Patriarch, who pretended not to hear them. We do not know how much he understood about this legation of the Father. He wrote about this to the provinces, <!-- PDF page 79 | book page 69 --> saying that [Bouvet] had been added to the legation through the influence of others; he certainly seems to have believed that the Father was not a true legate, and he stated this dismissively in his letters to the Holy Father. Either he imagined this had been done by deception or he was persuaded that such an act was null and void since him being present, with such eminent authority and being his superior, meant that [Bouvet] could not legitimately accept such a legation without his knowledge and consent. As he said on May 12th: “It is marvellous that so many believed in the Emperor’s written mandate but nobody questioned the facts of the matter as they really were.” Meanwhile, in such a far-off country, he continued acting secure in this one belief in the facts as he wished them to be without reflecting on the rocky course of events which, by the rules of diplomacy, can ensue in courts of princes.

The bingbu tribunal,[^p79-58] which had the responsibility of packing the imperial presents in cases, handed the keys to Fr. Bouvet. His Illustriousness demanded that the Father hand them over, which he did out of respect, but, in the presence of witnesses, protested seven times, which His Illustriousness ignored. Finally, after the Father had been dismissed, he handed the keys over to Fr. Sabino with instructions that only in the case of death should he pass on the gifts to Fr. Bouvet. While they had arrived in Canton and were both waiting to board ship, and when their escorting mandarin was about to return to the court, Fr. Sabino asked for and got a copy of the imperial orders for Fr. Bouvet’s legation. When Fr. Bouvet learnt of this, he gently asked for the document so that—now that Fr. Sabino could no longer doubt his role as legate, having received from the mandarin certification of his status—he could act as his colleague. This was to prevent the mandarin from reporting behaviour at which the Emperor might take offense on returning to the court. The Lord Patriarch was informed of this proposal of the Father through letters he received from Fr. Sabino. He raged furiously, uttering shameful threats and saying direly that he would order Fr. Sabino rather to throw away the gifts than to hand them over to Fr. Bouvet. The upshot was that the Fr. Superior, J.F. Gerbillon, ordered Fr. Bouvet to completely give up the business of the gifts.[^p79-59] The Father Superior, taught by twenty years of experience at the Court, was not ignorant of how great was the tragedy that threatened, and how little hope there was of a happy ending to the business; but he chose the lesser of two evils and put benefit to another before his own loss. He was mindful also of the order given in writing by the Lord Patriarch to Fr. Bouvet, that whatever happened he was to give the <!-- PDF page 80 | book page 70 --> gifts to Fr. Sabino. He and the Beijing Fathers needed to take care not to offend the Emperor, so there was nothing they could do about it except to freely accept such a humiliation, to suffer whatever resulted, and to give way ignominiously. We chose to give way for the glory of the Holy See and to procure it insofar as we could. The Father Superior even notified the Lord Patriarch of his letter.

So far we have been discussing the third item in chronological order, the fourth in our order. Certain things were public, and privately we were engaged in discussions about the situation—especially one held exactly a month later that was interrupted by the sudden dangerous illness of the Lord Patriarch, which compelled us to call it off. This will shortly be referred to.

The fifth item of business came about this way. The Lord Patriarch showed the mandarins a great desire to come before the Emperor when they came to the hot springs on the morning of June 1st. It happened that Fr. Kilian and Henkama were ordered to go away by an order issued to Fr. Kilian. [The Patriarch] explained what he had in mind to the mandarin; it was just one thing, to open his whole heart to the Emperor. This was precisely what His Majesty had desired for a long time, and he had been anxiously awaiting the day when what he wanted hopefully for so long might be fulfilled. The Lord Patriarch would be in a fit state to be admitted into the presence of His Majesty and to undergo the required ceremonies in ten days’ time. On the tenth day the Emperor sent to invite him to an audience. It was to be held on June 12th but in the evening two days before, after supper, the Most Illustrious Lord suffered a violent attack of vomiting and dysentery, as did all those who had been preparing to accompany him to the Imperial Villa. There was no way His Illustriousness could proceed to the audience, much to the sorrow of the Emperor, who had found a new motive for wishing to see him in the excellent disposition which he had shown to fulfil His Majesty’s desires. His Illustriousness was detained sick at home until the severity of his disease allowed private access. When he was sufficiently recovered, on July 20th, the Emperor sent gifts and requested that he inform him through the mandarins what he judged necessary for him to know. Time and time again he reiterated that he was unable to communicate such things unless the Emperor should give a strict order about the person through whom he should communicate. The reason was that what he had to say did not concern either his own business or person, or his Lord the Supreme Pontiff, but the Emperor himself and the imperial family.[^p80-60] At his third refusal to speak <!-- PDF page 81 | book page 71 --> the Emperor became extremely angry, most unhappy that a newly arrived foreigner should say that he had come to China to conduct negotiations in matters belonging to the Emperor or his august family. So, in a fit of anger, he took up his brush and wrote a condemnation of the whole proceedings that the Lord Patriarch had hitherto engaged in, declaring his communications unworthy and finally giving him strict orders to speak out. When this order arrived at the house of the Lord Patriarch on June 21st, he could hardly contain his anxiety and pleaded both with us and the mandarins who were present. His business, he said, or rather that of the Emperor, was that Fr. Bouvet had made himself a legate of the Emperor etc. and that the Portuguese were prohibiting others to come to serve His Majesty. He vehemently proclaimed himself the most zealous of the zealous in everything pertaining to His Majesty. There was nobody present who did not realize that what he was saying would do harm to His Illustriousness and when all the other interpreters refused to act, Fr. Appiani signified the words to the mandarins. They absolutely refused to accept the words as they had been spoken but wanted something to be given to them in writing, no matter how late it was. It was not until around the eleventh hour that the mandarins finally left our house.

The Fathers made many representations to the Lord Bishop of Beijing to bring the Lord Patriarch to his senses. His entourage, however, by their blandishments and urgency prevailed over the Lord Patriarch and succeeded to such an extent that they brought about the succession of evils which were to follow. The Lord Patriarch firmly replied that his first obligation was to the Holy See and forced his somewhat reluctant chancellor to place his seal on the document.

This response, which revealed his secret advice, was written in Italian and was handed over to the mandarins by Fr. Appiani on June 22nd. He said to them in sorrow that it contained two very serious accusations, namely: 1. That Fr. Bouvet had been added to Fr. Sabino, who had been appointed with the agreement of the Emperor as the legate who was to bring the gifts to the Supreme Pontiff, simply as an interpreter—not to perform an imperial legation, and certainly not in the first place—and there was written testimony from other Fathers that it was done out of ambition. 2. That the Emperor had prudently resolved the disputes and forbidden any to arise in future, especially about the arrival of foreigners whom His Majesty asked to be sent, and that the root of <!-- PDF page 82 | book page 72 --> the problem should be removed—namely that the Portuguese wanted none to come except by way of their kingdom, first becoming subjects of their king and their laws, etc.

The whole of this Italian document was translated into Manchu in our house and awaited with great impatience in the Emperor’s Villa where, as soon as he had cast his eyes over it, the Firstborn Prince exclaimed: “He deserves death for this! What is going on! What is happening! Isn’t Bouvet our legate? Yet he puts up against him his servant, as if his servant could be an imperial legate!”

The Prince, a man 34 years old, presented the Manchu translation to his father. His father, keeping his anger under control, asked: “What is it? What does it say? Not another complaint about the legation of Fr. Bouvet!” When he was shown the list of statements and read them, His Majesty exclaimed: “So very true! This is just how things are!” Then half in jest, through the eunuchs, he put several questions to Fr. Appiani. From these Fr. Appiani realised how foolish he had been in translating this complaint of the Lord Patriarch. How much this displeased His Majesty became obvious from the fact that the Emperor inquired of the veteran Europeans whether the Lord Patriarch would be thought in Europe to have incurred the death penalty! And, whether Fr. Sabino would avoid punishment there? He was judging the case according to Chinese law, by which Fr. Bouvet or others would be condemned for their testimony if the least falsehood were detected in their testimony.

The Emperor personally drafted an edict for the Lord Patriarch in which firstly he dealt with the case of Fr. Bouvet. Secondly, he said that since he was sent by the Supreme Pontiff he should pay attention to nothing but matters relating to our holy religion. Thirdly, he said how dare he talk about removing the root cause of discord when he so often caused disagreements. Fourthly, that the Europeans in China have up until now behaved well but bad behaviour had begun only with his arrival. Fifthly, he threatened that henceforth no missionary would be admitted except after an examination by the mandarins in the port etc.

He asked the Fathers to go and forewarn Fr. Appiani of the severity of this decree and prepare the Most Illustrious Lord to receive it quietly and so, by giving a good example, win over the mandarins and the whole court. When the mandarins came the Most Illustrious Lord carefully suppressed his emotion and quietly thanked His Majesty for his most wise advice. When he heard this, the Emperor strongly advised him to take more care and told him he should be sure that all his decrees were sent in writing to the Supreme Pontiff. Anxiously he inquired whether the Lord Patriarch now sufficiently acknowledged that his servant Sabino was granted permission to go but that he was not an imperial legate.

<!-- PDF page 83 | book page 73 -->

[The Emperor] drafted a new condemnatory decree which was not to be revealed unless the Lord Patriarch himself should display any animosity in carrying out his orders. He casually made some of the Fathers aware of this decree and they immediately notified Fr. Appiani so that when he was leaving the Villa he should warn the Lord Patriarch against showing even the slightest curiosity in front of the mandarins or placing trust in those mandarins who were around and complaining about the attitude of the Emperor. And if, God forbid, there should be a further edict, he should receive it respectfully; but the last thing he should do would be to inquire about this. And if the mandarins were to inquire whether Fr. Bouvet deserved anything other than respect? This was a sword hanging over his head, and the legation of Fr. Sabino would be recalled.

When the Lord Patriarch thought of this he burst into tears which might be called a shower of gold if you think of the price he had paid for them. They were, however, the only way of avoiding whatever there was to fear from an irate Emperor. The mandarins were very pleased to see the Lord Patriarch’s tears and gently began to provoke him to tell them their cause. He said he was weeping because the Supreme Pontiff would put the entire blame on him if Fr. Bouvet should be recalled and the gifts held up to the dishonour of the Holy See, for the news of the gifts had already gone before them by way of Manila. He then added his fervent prayers that both should be ordered to go by His Majesty.

The mandarins reported the Lord Patriarch’s tears to the Emperor sympathetically. He was moved by their account and immediately ordered Fr. Appiani to be sent to announce a reconciliation. Soon after he instructed the mandarins to tell Fr. Bouvet and Fr. Sabino that they should both go and that the harsher decrees already issued need not be sent to Europe. [The Patriarch] should now write to his man Sabino telling him how to behave while the Emperor would write to his man Bouvet etc. While this was going on the Lord Patriarch again had a weeping fit, but since he was willing to make public the reason for his tears he lost everything he had previously gained. Nobody wanted to interpret what he said but finally his reply was given in writing by Fr. Appiani to the mandarins who were standing by. His Illustriousness was weeping at seeing the holy religion suffer from what had happened.

Fr. Superior Jean-François Gerbillon and all the rest congratulated the Lord Patriarch on the passing of the storm-cloud in this way. But it was too soon to say that it had passed since black clouds were still massing in the heart of His Illustriousness, to be released if an occasion arose to fulminate against the Jesuits and the Portuguese nation. That he had not become more amenable was seen round about July 20th, and again in the month of August when he once more went on the attack with many complaints—especially, he began to enumerate this or that person who had been prevented from coming in such and <!-- PDF page 84 | book page 74 --> such a year, and to claim that they had wanted to detain Fr. Appiani, who was present, in Macau. All these stories, however, became a black mark against him and were turned against their author, to our great sorrow.

The sixth and last issue occurred when the Most Reverend Bishop of Conon exasperated the Emperor in many ways and the latter attempted to get some satisfaction from the Lord Patriarch. This should have consisted in a certain self-abnegation, on the advice of the mandarins, using general words signifying nothing in particular but admitting that he had erred in producing the Bishop of Conon. But he was less respectful than he should have been and refused to comply as the circumstances demanded. So it came about that he aggravated the Great Emperor. However the Lord Patriarch constantly denied this was so, which was the reason for the accumulation of decrees as black as they were dishonourable. When this happened His Illustriousness appealed to his standing as Apostolic Legate, which did not count for much with the gentiles at the far ends of the world, whatever the kingdom or secular ruler. Whatever weight his status might have in itself, an appeal to it could not create the desired effect. The Emperor insisted: “If he is a legate why does he not produce his credentials? If he now wants to be treated as such, he should produce them now.” The Most Illustrious Lord wanted the two Most Reverend Bishops of Beijing and Conon to produce the apostolic letters about his powers as Legate which they had received from Rome. But they did not think it opportune, believing that such documents in Roman curial style would have little effect on anyone who did not observe them. When His Illustriousness realised they had good reasons on their side for not producing their letters he gave up, and the Emperor wanted him to leave Beijing. [The Emperor] did not issue another decree except that [the Patriarch] should not change the day of his departure, which he had already freely chosen. However [the Emperor] recalled the gifts, together with Fathers Bouvet and Sabino, until he should receive notice from His Holiness testifying to the legitimacy of his legation. [The Emperor] decreed that he would do this but first informed the Lord Patriarch, as it were to threaten him, and finally suspended action until the beginning of October. His Illustriousness made no attempt— in words, by letters, or through intermediaries—to placate the Emperor in any way whatsoever. The decree was then carried out by sending a mandarin to ride on horseback in stages to Canton—which took 25 days—to order the aforesaid men and the gifts to return from there. As it said in the decree, this was because even though the European veterans swear to their belief that Duoluo (the Lord Patriarch) is truly a legate of the Supreme Pontiff, this cannot be trusted in imperial affairs until the matter can be tested by legal credentials.

There were business affairs other than those recorded in the acts, but they were either not relevant to the substance of the legation or they solely concerned <!-- PDF page 85 | book page 75 --> the Jesuits. An example is the investigation into the way of life of the Beijing Fathers by the most disreputable of men who had been thrown out of all of the churches; the Lord Bishop of Beijing objected to this on more than one occasion. Another was the condemnation of all the contracts which were said to have been made by the Fathers before they were questioned about the details, let alone proven to be guilty of any crime. However, we will not dwell on them since the common good urges us to consider more important matters.

### 5. Certain memorable events which occurred during the visit of the Most Reverend and Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch

What other events should I list? Not those more successful than those already mentioned; nor is it useful to record all those that turned out badly. There are however some that should be narrated to avoid even more serious consequences by revealing the truth, or to correct what has happened if it can be done.

I have previously said that the gifts were recalled by His Majesty and Fathers Bouvet and Sabino ordered to return to the Court. We are not so concerned about Fr. Bouvet since he accepted his task legitimately and the Emperor’s anger was not very seriously directed against him. What we were especially upset about was that the respect owed to the Holy Father Pope Clement XI and his expectations raised about the gifts, in news sent ahead by way of Moscow and Manila, would not be satisfied. We omitted absolutely nothing that might bend His Majesty’s mind lest the punishment of the Lord Patriarch might detract from the honour which he had decreed lovingly and in royal fashion to the Supreme Pontiff. But we got nowhere: we got nothing to report to Europe from our efforts other than a renewal of the threats originally made to us in Tartary, that if we kept on insisting we would be absolutely prohibited from making any requests whatsoever.

Secondly, our great hopes for Christianity were overturned. What a harvest, God Immortal, had we hoped for, so many floods of favours had the Emperor bestowed on the field of China together with those who cultivated it! He himself had said that he wanted Chinese to become Christians, and yet the Lord Patriarch acted as he did against the Chinese. The duty of writing to the Supreme Pontiff was not the responsibility of His Majesty, nor the spreading of our religion, nor should he be blamed if it is overturned. We cannot remember without tears how many good things have been stopped. Even the Emperor himself recommended moderation while the Most Illustrious Lord, despite his setbacks, knocked down with his lightning bolts what [the Emperor] had previously promoted.

The Emperor also deserves praise for continuing to display the same esteem and reverence towards His Holiness amidst these tricks and disturbances, more <!-- PDF page 86 | book page 76 --> than once speaking of him no less honourably and with the same warm tone. On one occasion he rebuked the courtiers and eunuchs for blaming the one who sent for the blunders of the one sent. He replied laughing: “Everywhere in the world you find those who prefer to follow their own opinion rather than the instructions of their master; they think they are the masters while claiming to be helping them.” As yet the authority of His Holiness remains untouched on the part of the Emperor, although it cannot be denied that there has been some damage done amongst those who judge by the outcome of events.

Thirdly, since the Lord Patriarch left, the Most Reverend Bishop of Conon has remained in Beijing, and to this day he is living with the Fathers of the Society inside the saffron wall. Up until now the reason for this has not been explained, although it does not seem to be some sort of punishment; we must wait until the Emperor reveals his intentions. This detention becomes more significant if one considers that in the same place there is detained the Lord Patriarch’s catechist, who lent his hand and brush to writing down the words which, we have noted above, offended the Emperor. We have humbly interceded for both of them on repeated occasions through our four men in Tartary but each time we have been repulsed, and now we are strictly ordered not to return to the subject.[^p86-61] This would seem, Most Reverend Father, the point at which we would want to abruptly break off, but it would seem more natural to imply, if not explain, the reasons for this unexpected delay that the Most Reverend Lord Bishop of Conon threw into proceedings. And so, besides what I have already written, I will put together some remarks, in a page or two inserted here, on the reason we think may have led to it.

It began with the discussion the Emperor held in Tartary with the Most Reverend Bishop of Conon on 2 August 1706.[^p86-62] In this can be detected the motive for his detention. In the account of this discussion we will use the letter E for the Emperor, and R for the replies of the Bishop of Conon. The Lord Bishop of Conon was admitted to the presence of the Emperor together with Fathers Appiani, Guéty, and Angelita, and our Fathers Antoine Thomas, Pereira, Parrenin, Barros, and Beauvollier. The Emperor began the interrogation as follows: E: “Do you understand Chinese characters?” R: “Liao, liao,[^p86-63] (that is, a very little).” E: “Duoluo introduced you as someone extremely versed in our books and that is the reason I have brought you here. Have you read the

<!-- PDF page 87 | book page 77 -->

Sishu?”[^p87-64] R: “Yes.” E: “Have you memorized what you have read?” R: “No.” E: “You have read them but not committed them to memory?” R: “It is not the custom in Europe to learn something by heart.” Up to this point the Emperor and the Most Reverend Lord Bishop conversed without an interpreter but not without difficulty on both sides in understanding the other. His Majesty spoke slowly, distinctly, and clearly, yet was not sufficiently understood by the Most Reverend Lord; so, he ordered Fr. Parrenin to come closer and to act as his interpreter. The Father acted as interpreter on the orders of the Emperor except in certain places where [Fr. Parrenin] asked His Lordship to explain himself as best he wished and could, lest he be thought to be biased.

So they proceeded through the interpreter. The Emperor said: “Can you not recite a few words of the Sishu?” And His Lordship spoke two or three words which the Emperor took up immediately, dwelling on the Chinese words and pronouncing slowly what followed in the same book. But it was to no avail. The Most Reverend Lord remained silent through the rest of the passage. The Emperor recited from memory some three pages and, turning to Fr. Antoine Thomas, inquired whether in Europe none of the sciences were committed to memory. The Father fell to his knees and replied: “Very many are memorized but there are also many that are not committed to memory.” The Emperor asked the Bishop of Conon: “Do you read our books?” R: “I have known them for a short while.” E: “Can you read for me the large characters that are opposite you suspended above my head?” R: “The first character is hua; the second yin: the third I don’t recognize; the fourth is wo.” E: “Aren’t you mistaken? The first is hua, that you got right; the second is yan not yin; the third is yun; and the fourth is ge not wo. So it reads: Hua yan yun ge.[^p87-65] Now tell us what the words mean.” R: “I don’t understand them.” E: “If you don’t understand the meaning of four characters, how on earth can you understand our books? I know this task is too difficult for you. Even Chinese who have spent 50 years studying our books often need to turn to dictionaries to penetrate the more difficult passages.” R: “Since Chinese after 50 years of study still have difficulties in many places, it is no wonder that foreigners like me know almost nothing and cannot explain those characters. I beg Your Majesty not to be displeased at my ignorance of them.” E: “I am not surprised that you are ignorant of them. But how did it come about that Duoluo (the Lord Patriarch) said that you were especially well acquainted with our books and the customs of our Empire? And that <!-- PDF page 88 | book page 78 --> you were able to give an account of all of those matters in the teachings of Confucius which you say are incompatible with your religion?” R: “This came about because the Lord Patriarch did not know me and had never set eyes on me.” E: “I see that I have a problem today. If you were able to write down your interpretations, I would give you an immediate answer. But what way can I begin to deal with you now, since you neither read nor write [Chinese]? What am I to write? I can’t even speak to you since you don’t understand a word. Ba Duoming (Father Parrenin),[^p88-66] tell him this: the writings which you recently submitted to me are full of errors. Here is just one which I bring to your attention. You cite there the Yueling in the book called Liji.[^p88-67] But you are mistaken. Confucius is not the author of that treatise but another man whose name is Lü Buwei,[^p88-68] who is very far from being one of our sages; moreover, we admit there is much that is reprehensible in his teachings. It is for the elegance of his style that we honour him, attaching no value to what he taught. We preserve his works only on account of their style.” R: “I am aware that that treatise was not written by Confucius but by Lü Buwei; but, it was included among the sections of the books of the Liji, which is part of the teachings of the Literary Sect.”

E: “How did you come to know this?” R: “I read it in a large work the title of which is Xingli daquan.”[^p88-69] E: “In what part of that book? Quote the text.” R: “I do not recall the place or the text.” E: “I remind you again that whatever remains of the writings of this man is not valued by us except for the polished elegance of his brush. But I have already told you all this: I have already said that all that you recently submitted to me is full of errors.” R: “What I wrote there are the exact expositions which I have found in books.” E: “What sort of an answer is that? On your own admission, and from my experience that you do not understand the books, it follows that you are not speaking from your own knowledge but from what others say, others whom you are following.” R: “That <!-- PDF page 89 | book page 79 --> is quite true. For I was not the one who first found the objections that I made against these points; those who taught me the language when I first arrived in China also taught me that all these are worthless.”[^p89-70] E: “If this is so, they are equally as ignorant as you are. Why do you allow yourself to be imposed upon? Let us suppose that I say that everything you have submitted to me in writing is true—what would you conclude from that? And if I say that all those things are false, and that you have been basely deceived—what conclusion would you draw from that?”

The Most Illustrious Lord hesitated before he answered; but, after he had thought about it for some time in silence, he answered: “The things I noticed then do not seem to be in accordance with our religion.” E: “In order to know whether they are in agreement or not, you need first of all to understand them, and it is quite clear that you do not understand those things which you have caused to be written.” R: “I understand them to some extent.” E: With a smile the Emperor replied: “To some extent! That is, you do not even half-understand them, and so with the slight understanding that you have, how can you make a definitive judgment about them? If I had only a slight understanding of your affairs and wanted to take control of them, what would you have to say about me?” R: “With the little knowledge that I have, that is how it appears to me.” E: “What are you saying? Before you understand something thoroughly you are going to make a decision about it? You have not learned to write, and you do not know how to read (Chinese); where did you get this knowledge of our books? While you are deficient in this knowledge, are you in the meantime going to make rash judgments? In our books you cannot discern truth from falsehood, true doctrine from figures of speech, or a recognized author from one who is not recognized; so, what authority have you to make a pronouncement about our beliefs?”

In reply to all this, His Most Illustrious Lordship could not utter a single word. And the Emperor continued: “Among the matters that you claimed were evil, why did you pass over the three which are in contention, namely Jing Tian,[^p89-71] <!-- PDF page 90 | book page 80 --> the cult of the dead ancestors and Confucius, and the tablets? Doubtless, you must have supposed that there was nothing wrong with them?” R: “I was not advised that I might write of them there.” E: “Even if you think you did not write about them, in fact however you did write about them. For these points are implied in what you have written. Therefore I ask you, what evil do you find in these words: Jing Tian, Revere Heaven? Perhaps in this phrase Tian does not signify the Lord of Heaven?” R: “It does not mean that.” E: “You amaze me! Have I not told you before that Tian means the Lord of Heaven himself and that this is a far more beautiful name for God than Tianzhu or Tiandi wanwu zhi zhu,[^p90-72] which is a term you use in China for God? For the first means only the Lord of Heaven, while the second means the Lord of heaven, of the earth and of the ten thousand things; but Tian itself signifies the Lord of heaven, of earth, and of all beings. Tell me why the people call me wansui,[^p90-73] ten thousand years. It cannot be done according to your religion, because I am not ten thousand years old.” R: “This is done to express the desire they have that Your Majesty’s life may last as long as possible.” E: “You have answered correctly, and learn from this that in our language, words and rites are not to be understood merely according to what is said or according to their external appearance. We celebrate rites in honour of Confucius, and of our ancestors who have died, in order to demonstrate our love and reverence for them. Explain where I am mistaken, and whatever according to you there is in our teaching which is not in accord with your religion. When did these errors begin? Did it perhaps begin in the time of Li Madou (Father Ricci)[^p90-74] and continue up to the present day? You must have read the book written by this Li Madou?” Father Parrenin interpreted this, just as he had interpreted what went before, using the Chinese name unchanged, Li Madou. The Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon turned to Father Parrenin and said: “Reverend Father, who is this Li Madou?” The Father said: “Li Madou is the Chinese name of Father Matteo Ricci, and it is printed at the beginning of the book in which he proves that Tian or Shangdi is not the material heaven, nor li nor taiji nor anything created.”[^p90-75] In this book <!-- PDF page 91 | book page 81 --> there is Father Ricci’s system of Chinese doctrine, which the Most Illustrious Lord Maigrot in his exposition wanted rejected. When the Most Illustrious Lord realized from what Father Parrenin had said that Li Madou was Father Matteo Ricci, and that he was being questioned as to whether he had read this man’s book in Chinese regarding the expression Tian, he replied: “I have not read that book.”[^p91-76]

The Emperor continued: “Li Madou and his companions came to these lands at least two hundred years ago.[^p91-77] Before those times China knew nothing of the Incarnation nor of the name Tianzhu which you now give to God, who was not made flesh in these lands of ours. Before the arrival of Ricci, why did we have no right to call God Tian? And what difficulties now prevent God from being called by the name of Heaven in this China of ours?” R: “The reason is that Heaven is not the Lord of Heaven; He who made Heaven is the Lord of Heaven.” With a suggestion of severity in his tone, the Emperor said: “I told you long ago that Heaven and the Lord of Heaven mean the same.”

Then, proceeding to other matters, he said: “We honour Confucius as our teacher only to express the gratitude due to him by reason of the teaching which he bequeathed to us. When we pray before the tablets we do not ask for honour or happiness. Listen! There are three points which you have called into question. If you are not happy with them you should consider leaving this country. And if those who have embraced your religion see that all that goes on among yourselves is arguments, disagreements, and differences in procedure, they will have doubts about your religion and others will think less about embracing it. To tell you the truth, I myself look on you as if you have come not to strengthen but to destroy your religion; if all the efforts for this come to naught, take the blame yourself.”

The Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon had no answer to this, and when the Emperor brought about a halt in the proceedings, two yuxian,[^p91-78] that is assistants, went down on their knees, and spoke to this effect: “Today Your Majesty’s <!-- PDF page 92 | book page 82 --> heart overflows with goodness, when with such zeal and exceeding great kindness he spends himself in promoting the interests of these Europeans, at the very time when they are ruining their own cause. Let Your Majesty cease to squander his wisdom to no good purpose, and let him strive no longer to question this man who is ignorant of everything and pig-headed in everything that does not suit himself.” The Emperor smiled at what they said and again directed his words to the Lord Bishop of Conon: “Come now,” he said, “let us finish some of this business today. Do you wish to have a debate about our books?” R: “I do not wish to debate anything, for I do not have the capacity to debate with Your Majesty.”

After this, the Emperor now for the first time addressed Father Beauvollier: “What do you say,” he asked, “about the word Tian and its meaning?” The Father replied: “Tian, both from the elements of the character and its accepted usage, means yi da, the first great one[^p92-79] or the first being, who existed before there was heaven or earth and anything else, who created heaven and earth and everything which is contained in them.” E: “Do you Yan Dang have anything to say against that?” R: “I cannot debate it, for an ignorant person who is not an expert should not enter into a debate.” E: “Are there any other Europeans in China who think the same as you do, and who are more erudite in Chinese matters?”

R: “There certainly are!” E. “Where do they live, and what are their names?” R: “I do not know.” E: “For Europeans, our books are hard to understand. I myself have examined Liu Ying (Father Visdelou)[^p92-80] who is said to have studied our books thoroughly, and I found that he did not fully understand their meaning.”

After this, His Majesty took a sheet of paper with four lines of writing and holding it in his hands said: “I have examined the two characters yan and dang which make up your name. Do you understand the second one?” R: “Yes.” E: “And what does it mean?” R: “It means to protect.” E: “It does not mean that.

This character is unworthy and should not be used as the name for a religious man.”[^p93-81] R: “When I came to China, I was given this name by others, and I did not dare to change it although I was told that it means eunuch.” (However, this was wrong since dang does not mean eunuch but is a proper name, the name of a particular eunuch, a man regarded as loathsome in China, who destroyed the previous Da Ming dynasty and the Chinese Empire.) So Fr. Parrenin did not translate these last words of His Lordship.

The Emperor then asked Father Guéty by what Chinese name he was called. Father Guéty pronounced his Chinese name, which was not understood by the Emperor until the mandarins said more loudly that he was called Fang Zhou. The Emperor turned back to the Bishop of Conon and asked him in which province he lived. R: “In the province of Fujian.” E: “How many followers of your religion live there?” R: “About five thousand.” E: “Do all these people agree with your opinions or do they oppose them? Do they reject the Jing Tian, the cult of the ancestors, the tablets, and the veneration of Confucius?” E. “I do not know the followers of our religion who live outside the metropolitan city of Fuzhou. Since they are poor and of lowly estate they do not possess those shrines called zutang[^p93-82] in which the tablets are kept. I do not know for certain what anyone in particular does in this regard.” E: “Just suppose that I arrange for an investigation to be made by the mandarins of that province and I find that you have made a false statement to me in such a matter; what do you think will happen?” After he said this, His Majesty summoned the mandarins who introduced us and said to them: “Take this man away now, and after that I shall send my orders down to you.”

Although what I have related may seem long or irrelevant, I have included it out of a desire that the truth should be told, and, where not absolutely certain, to at least provide what prudent conjecture would suggest to be the reasons why the Most Reverend Lord Bishop of Conon together with Fr. Guéty and the catechist have not yet been permitted to leave and await the return of His Majesty from Tartary. I must respectfully request Your Paternity and beg your pardon when I ask you to examine certain propositions to see whether the arguments displease you.[^p94-84] Some examples are where: 1. Guéty. There was no crime in this since it had not been prohibited. But the Emperor in his wisdom ordered inquiries to be made whether he had received any letters and [Fr. The mandarin intermediaries ordered the basket to be brought. The European was accused of deceit and lying amid loud shouts of condemnation but, in fact, nothing was discovered in either letter which deserved making such a great secret of it. He was compelled to put the letter together again from the fragments. Nothing important was discovered except that in the letter reconstructed by his own hands there were two points that were worth chewing over. Repentant, he said that in future he would rather die than say something untrue and would prefer to tell the truth, even if it was against his interests, rather than lie. The Emperor seized on this reply and ordered him to speak up regarding anything he knew about the Lord Patriarch’s affairs, those of the Bishop of Conon, and so on. ### 6. The state of Christianity in Beijing after the visit of the Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lord Patriarch

I will not talk in this section about the number of Christians, the churches, or the outcome of the holy labour of particular missionaries. I will restrict myself to the Beijing Court and how the affairs of our holy religion are getting on there. The more prudent of our readers can judge its signs of life from the synopsis I attach here:

1. The Emperor laments that favours hitherto unheard of in China have been wasted for nothing: this is exactly what the Crown Prince and the Firstborn Prince daily charge us with.

<!-- PDF page 98 | book page 88 -->

2. The Emperor believes he has been insulted, and before the eyes of his court, which contemplates ways of vindicating His Majesty. Hence they propose arguments for recalling the gifts, detaining the Most Reverend Bishop of Conon, annulling the legation, and summoning His Reverence Mezzafalce and the two teachers of the Bishop of Conon.

3. The Emperor suspects that there are serious differences between the preachers of the holy religion because he sees so much dissidence, envy, disagreement, accusation, etc. They feed these suspicions by secret investigations. The most unusual of these was on the part of the Firstborn Prince who, not content with gathering evidence throughout the provinces, even sent one of his slaves to feign seeking baptism so that he could get inside as far as possible. They verify the terrors inspired in them by Fr. Guéty by bringing to light anything they can against the Jesuits etc., and they will continue to do so, unless God should shortly intervene, by starting yet further investigations.

4. In the presence of the Emperor many now openly criticize the Holy Law, which previously they did not dare to do. When they perceive he is annoyed with the heads of our religion they try to stir him up against the Holy Law itself. The foremost amongst them is the Eldest Son, who is more to be feared by us than sword and fire. Some of the nobles try to persuade—in one case a wife, in others sons or domestic slaves—to become renegades from our Holy Faith, for one reason alone; so, they say that the Emperor has been offended by the Supreme Pontiff through his legate.

5. The bonzes and sectarians exult and openly spread diabolical omens of the imminent demise of our Holy Law, which leads to revulsion on the part of gentiles and trepidation on the part of weaker Christians.

6. The Emperor has shown often that he is wary of our holy religion, hence also of his people who have embraced it thinking that it was good, and this because they saw missionaries performing good works, displaying the charity and truth, etc. that they preach in their words. Now, on the contrary, they see the highest authorities accuse some of them of crimes, and furthermore before the Emperor, without good reason for the accusation.

7. The authority of the Supreme Pontiff, for so many years elevated amongst both Christians and pagans, is greatly diminished. Whatever holds for others, everybody here is scandalized that those who have received more than others from the Supreme Pontiff should more than others wish to see his reputation diminished. Before they can speak of Christ they speak of his eminence in Christ, and above all they demand veneration of him on account of Christ of those whom they have not yet taught to know Christ.

8. The authority of the Lord Patriarch has suffered a great blow from the imperial decrees.

<!-- PDF page 99 | book page 89 -->

9. The reputation of the missionaries is destroyed in the eyes of many since several have been convicted of self-contradiction; some have been forced to admit telling lies, and they have made others suspect of similar crimes.

10. The good name of the Jesuits is gratuitously disparaged by those who, for the glory of God and the increase of religion, should pay attention to them. The situation here is not the same as in the courts of Europe where one can freely criticize the Jesuits without their ministry being in any way threatened, to the detriment of souls. Here when someone dishonours missionaries in the eyes of the Emperor, it turns many away from the faith, and it scandalizes many in their faith. And whenever their service to the faith is attacked, I can say with confidence that the more they work for others the more they are attacked by others. While they suffer so much, either the mission will survive such a convulsion or it will collapse—which may Jesus Our Saviour avert!

It has been a sad task gathering so many things in this tragic summary. Our only hope remaining is that Our Most Holy Lord [the Pope] will have contributed to changing things in the decree he laid down on 20 November 1704. This is now said to be in the hands of the Lord Patriarch although the decree itself has not been published up until this day, 30 October 1706. If it agrees with the decrees of the Emperor there is hope that our holy religion will survive, but if it contradicts them we must commend this vineyard to the Lord of the Vineyard. But we cultivators are compelled to endure the storms which we remember occurred in neighbouring kingdoms, and today while we wait to see what the future brings we can only say: may His will be done as it is in Heaven. The Beijing Fathers like our neighbours flourish in times of danger, and up until now have given no reason to doubt that they will be prompt in obedience to the Holy See and will not refuse to offer themselves even to exile and death.

### 7. The complaints of the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch against the Beijing Fathers

Very Reverend Father, Your Paternity need not be fearful from the title of this section that it will be a long list of complaints. This is not really the case even if it is so titled. We are men and live amongst men, capable of wrongly judging others and being wrongly judged by others. By long and daily experience we have learnt that if but a thousandth part of the accusations many have made against us were true, the whole of our Society would have perished long ago. The Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch himself came to know some time ago that many of the things he had believed, either from his own first impressions or by what others told him, were not as he had thought. And I am easily persuaded that this is so because on several occasions in China he has written and said very perceptively that scarcely anything of what had been written in

<!-- PDF page 100 | book page 90 -->

Rome, or even of similar things he had heard in China, were true. However, I have reason to fear that others less prudent and more averse to us have, in relations and delations written in his name, wanted to take up such charges. So, I list here their complaints together with our replies:

1. The Beijing Fathers sent none of their people to the ports to await the Most Illustrious Lord so that they could be of service to him from the moment of his arrival.

2. They did not arrange that the baggage brought by the Most Illustrious Lord and his 12 associates would be immune from duties imposed in the port.

3. They wrote no letters to him during the five months he was in Canton.

4. The Fathers did not arrange for the Emperor to send from the court a delegate to receive His Illustriousness and take him to Beijing.

5. The Visitor Fr. Grimaldi did not reply about the appointment of a Jesuit as Vicar Apostolic of Nanjing.

6. Neither did he respond about subjection to the vicars apostolic.

7. The Governor of Guangdong did not visit or bid farewell to the Lord Patriarch except through his son.

8. The gifts given by the mandarins to the entourage of the Most Illustrious Patriarch were insufficient.

9. The Fathers did not obey the warning of the Most Illustrious Lord to immediately introduce a practice contrary [to the Chinese Rites].

10. The Beijing Fathers did not greet the Most Illustrious Lord on their knees.

11. They did not treat the Most Illustrious Lord as a legate by offering him the courtesies due to his physical presence.

12. The Fathers neglected to promote the Most Illustrious Lord’s business by giving him good advice.

13. The Fathers prejudiced the Emperor against accepting the doctor brought by His Illustriousness.

14. The Fathers prevented the requests of His Illustriousness from being accepted by the Emperor.

15. The Fathers arranged that the Emperor would not allow a visitation by His Illustriousness of their men in Beijing.

16. The Fathers spoke disrespectfully of His Illustriousness.

17. The Fathers did not suppress the agitation of the Christians.

18. The Fathers did not arrange that bishops should be treated with special honour by the Emperor.

19. On the contrary, they were responsible for the humiliation of the Bishop of Conon and his companion Fr. Guéty.

20. They did not arrange for the Most Reverend Bishop of Conon and the catechist to leave Beijing with the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch.

21. The Fathers arranged for Fr. Bouvet to be joined to Fr. Sabino and for a public announcement to this effect to be printed, giving him precedence.

22. The Fathers extorted false oaths from the Christians.

23. They used force against bad debtors contrary to canon law.

24. They made usurious contracts—or at least close to usurious and indecent.

25. They did not prevent the Most Illustrious Lord from being treated ignominiously by certain ministers of the Emperor etc.

Responses to each of the above complaints:

Re 1: We did not hear the Most Illustrious Lord make this complaint personally but it came from others who were zealous for his honour and to promote his interests. There are two principal ports in China, Fuzhou and Guangzhou. It would then have been necessary to send two Fathers from Beijing some 400 leagues in all, going and returning. But at whose expense and for how many years would they be detained in the port? Secondly, the Fathers cannot be absent from Beijing for even two days without previous permission from the Emperor after presenting a legitimate excuse. In this case, however, they would have to be away several months, perhaps even two years. You may say that the arrival of the Most Illustrious Lord was a good excuse for taking a leave of absence. Quite so. But who could reliably predict that the Most Illustrious Lord would arrive at a predetermined time? Who could respond to a query from the Emperor about how he should be received without anticipating the wishes of the Lord Legate while keeping all options open, given that we neither knew nor wanted to know what had been determined regarding his entrance. Third and last, if we had sent Fathers from Beijing, would this not have been a cause of complaint; would not some have said that this was done in order that the Jesuits should take him prisoner, depriving him of the freedom of choosing his companions, interpreters of his choice, etc.?

Re 2: Complaints nos. 2 to 9 inclusive are found in the letters of the Most Illustrious Lord written by his own hand or in his orders and the originals sent to Europe. Regarding exemption from customs duties, it is agreed that Fr. Grimaldi and others worked on his behalf. This is what His Illustriousness wrote on 8 May 1705: “There are many difficulties and problems on the part of the customs office, which has not yet released my baggage despite my producing Your Reverence’s chop,[^p101-93] and the intervention of Fr. Visdelou and Beauvollier.” Yet the attempts of the Fathers were not efficacious enough. But His Illustriousness <!-- PDF page 102 | book page 92 --> cannot reverse the blame onto the Fathers as he wanted to. It falls on him. He might as well apply to the Father Rector of the Roman College or the Father Prior of the Minerva to move the stony face of a customs officer in the old city [Rome] to allow a Chinese mandarin of the first rank to pass without charge together with the mules of his entourage and the valuable goods brought from his homeland.

Re 3: This charge disappears immediately if one considers that the Most Illustrious Lord in the month of April ordered the Fathers to be informed through their procurator Fr. Beauvollier that he was immediately leaving Canton for Nanjing, to which they should address their letters. He changed his mind on May 8th. The Fathers could not have received news of this switch from the earlier advice before the end of June. Consequently, following this instruction, letters for Canton could not have reached Canton before the end of July or beginning of August. The Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch must have seen that this long delay in his letters was truly not the fault of the Fathers but accidental, as I have shown. And he admitted this himself on September 4th when he wrote in his own hand: “I acknowledge receipt of your letters of the 17th, 22nd, 23rd, 24th, and 31st of July together with others, and also another of yours without a date in the same bundle. I have received another from Your Reverence of August 14th.” There is no need to cite other letters from our superiors Fathers Gerbillon and Antoine Thomas that were sent to His Illustriousness.

Re 4: Much earlier some people tried to persuade His Illustriousness that the Fathers were determined that he should not be admitted to the Court. When they heard that permission had been granted for him to come they wanted him to believe that there was a scheme on the part of the same Fathers to be delegated to greet His Illustriousness in the port and conduct him to Beijing. The desires of men are without limit but reason sets bounds. Hence the Most Illustrious Lord, rejecting their advice, wrote to Fr. Grimaldi: “I am pleased that envoys from the Court have not come since it leaves me free from being put under pressure, and I am happy that the Emperor knows that I must visit the Christian communities; this will enable me to come to the Court more at ease, and to carry out my duties. Canton, September 4th.”

Re 5: It is true that His Illustriousness wrote the following in his own hand to Fr. Grimaldi on May 8th: “I want you to propose one of your subjects, the most suitable among the Fathers of the Society of Jesus, to be the Vicar Apostolic of Nanjing. I have the duty to appoint someone as long as the position is vacant.” There were many considerations prohibiting a response. For our superiors cannot put anyone forward any of our men for any kind of ecclesiastical dignity simply at the words I want you to propose. Then there are the rights of the

<!-- PDF page 103 | book page 93 -->

Primate of the Indies to make such an appointment.[^p103-94] And thirdly, we who are appointed to missions have taken an oath before the highest authority which is competent in this matter; hence we cannot get involved and must decline with all due respect to get involved in such difficult matters.[^p103-95]

Re 6: The Most Illustrious Lord had written to Fr. Grimaldi on May 8th: “It would be good if Your Reverence should advise your subjects that in dealing with the vicars apostolic they should be very attentive to demonstrating their obedience to the decrees of the Holy Apostolic See.” Nothing is demanded here about replying, only action is mentioned. So, Fr. Grimaldi could wait until he had received a positive order to offer such an act of subjection before responding to this letter. Yet the Father did advise his subjects to do what is implied in these words, although they hardly constituted an order. Further, the Father Visitor did not make any reply whatsoever since he indicated that he would discuss such matters personally with the Most Illustrious Lord when he came to Beijing.

Re 7: The argument that the Fathers are responsible for the omissions of the Governor goes like this: according to the testimony of His Illustriousness on September 4th one of the Fathers, namely Fr. Tomás Pereira, wrote to the zongdu or Supreme Prefect, asking him to treat the Lord Patriarch kindly (which he generously did according to a letter from His Lordship of October 6th). But, so the argument goes, the Governor omitted to return the visit of His Illustriousness etc. because none of the Fathers wrote to him. But no conclusion can follow if the premises are false. Nobody wrote because none of the Fathers knew the man. From an early age he had been a mandarin in the provinces outside the capital, and had just recently been shifted from Yunnan to Canton.

Re 8: This is a matter of little importance. When the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch wrote on the subject he was criticizing [his entourage]: joking, not complaining.

Re 9: This is dealt with above in the third section when discussing the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch’s actions regarding the controversies. There is nothing to add here except that the Fathers only want to find out which rites those <!-- PDF page 104 | book page 94 --> are, and of these, only those from which they should, on the basis of the facts, withdraw approval. Meanwhile His Lordship should believe that, whether or not the missionaries had sufficient reasons to protest against the declaration of the Most Reverend Bishop Maigrot, the decrees of the Holy Inquisition and Innocent X were equally good reasons to defer obeying the advice of the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch, since he knew that His Holiness Pope Clement XI had similarly subjected the matter to a new examination.[^p104-96]

Re 10. This would require a longer treatment. I refer Your Paternity to the Beijing Diary which our procurators are bringing you. Here a few words must suffice to clarify the issue. When the Emperor heard that a high-ranking European was coming as Visitor General etc. he wanted to know everything about his clothing etc. However, in order to excuse him from the usual ceremonies for foreigners visiting his Empire, he determined that the Lord Patriarch should dress in Sino-Manchu clothing and that he would be received and perform the ceremonies appropriate to the way he dressed. The newcomers from Rome did not understand this, thinking that there was nothing good or dignified in their reception as secular men—that this was not commensurate with Roman practice. Although he was dressed in Chinese style they paid their respects to him in the same way they would have in Europe, and urged the missionaries to do the same in what they said and did: kneeling before His Lordship and embracing his feet, addressing him on their knees while the Lord Patriarch himself placed his hands on their heads. We in Beijing knew nothing of such practices. The Emperor however heard about it through his spies and scolded us because the Lord Patriarch received Chinese Christians, his subjects—which included Europeans—on their knees. He absolutely forbade us to perform such genuflections. When the Lord Patriarch asked us about this and was informed what had happened, he vehemently refused to excuse any suspicion of guilt, insisting that it was our duty to instruct the Emperor that he was the kind of dignitary to whom such a kind of veneration was owed. In no way did His Illustriousness understand that such actions would be taken by His Majesty as a claim to jurisdiction over his Empire and that it would be inappropriate for him to enter into unhealthy disputes with him. Furthermore on more than one occasion we bent our knees to him and never murmured a word, nor did His Illustriousness experience the slightest repugnance on the part of any of our men to doing so.

<!-- PDF page 105 | book page 95 -->

Re 11: The Most Illustrious Lord complained that the Fathers did not give him the honour of attending on him to demonstrate our due subservience. In fact he went further and said that once a legate or quasi-legate of the Viceroy of Goa came to Beijing, and the Fathers attended him day and night. He knew this for a fact, and everything that happened then had been recorded in the records. If he called for the Fathers, and he would do so, they would have to wait all day in his apartments until evening came without seeing them. His Illustriousness said all this at a time when for lack of other business a certain priest suggested, out of ill will, that he should do something of the sort. What really happened or didn’t happen during the Portuguese legation is not worth discussing now. At that time there were four, or five at the most, men of the Society in Beijing, all living in a house close to where the Legate was staying. Now, however, His Illustriousness was living for nine months in a house of our Society. There were always six Jesuits staying with him; others from other houses came in turn as their health and tasks assigned by the Emperor allowed. Fr. Grimaldi, on account of a chronic illness, and another who had been ill for three years were excused. Four, for the greater part of the time, lived in the Imperial Villa. Fr. Pereira had to go there at least every second day and then had to take to his bed sick for more than a month; he slowly recovered, then was obliged to serve the Emperor for two months in Tartary, after which the Lord Patriarch himself went away [to the hot springs] and another two months went by. All this did not satisfy His Illustriousness, and credible as it is, even if he still felt himself offended, we were entitled to dismiss his claims.

Re 12: If the results the Most Illustrious Lord achieved in Beijing are considered, the wiser men in Europe might consider this complaint to be true, and we for our part confess that it is true to the extent that it asserts that the advice the Jesuits did give made no impression on His Illustriousness. This, as God is our witness, happened against our wishes and to our acute displeasure. This was because he did not seek advice, distrusted everything presented to him, and despised and laughed at it, as those of his entourage can bear witness. Without exception the most trustworthy of these is our Ordinary, the Most Reverend Bishop of Beijing, who for almost eight months lived with His Illustriousness in our house. I will not repeat what I said in earlier sections about the secret and open attempts of His Illustriousness to get the Jesuits to petition for a superior general, for a confidential representative, and for a house to be presented to him by His Majesty. Nor will I repeat how we acted repeatedly and without reply to procure his public admission to the court.

And as for our extraordinary efforts regarding the funeral procession of his valet through the streets of Beijing, from which he withdrew at the last minute, I would prefer you in Europe to look for examples in the Acta Pekinensia, where <!-- PDF page 106 | book page 96 --> you will see the following: How bitterly he rebuked the Father Visitor about the time and place when they discussed the matter on December 19th. How contemptuously he spoke about the funeral arrangements for the valet, as Fr. Kilian heard on 26 December, and then had to candidly advise him against parading his grievances against the Fathers before the mandarins and the Emperor. And how despotically he imposed silence on Fr. Pereira on December 28th. How necessary it was to act discreetly in so serious a matter was shown when, after reluctantly admitting Fr. Gerbillon and the Lord Bishop of Beijing, who warned him about a rumour reaching the Emperor, he displayed nothing but indignation.

To present just one witness, let me cite Fr. Appiani, who sometime around June 22nd said openly to the Lord Bishop of Beijing that he had been seriously rebuked by His Illustriousness on the June 3rd, again on the 8th in writing, and again when established at the hot springs for suggesting that it would be prudent, speaking as a friend, that he should humiliate himself and consult the Jesuits. But if [the Jesuits] were such in the eyes of the Most Illustrious Lord, how could he bring himself to submit to their advice, when he would not listen to that offered to him with episcopal respect and the authority of the Ordinary of the place? His Illustriousness confirmed his attitude on more than one occasion, saying that the Jesuits ought be satisfied to do what he wished or ordered and that he was not used to giving anybody the reasons for his actions. He answered only to God and the Supreme Pontiff.

Re 13. How far the Jesuits were from being prejudiced against His Illustriousness’s doctor is clear from their first memorial, in which they praised his reputation when advising his Majesty of his arrival. Furthermore how could they have been prejudiced since they knew he was certainly going to come to Beijing in the company of His Illustriousness, which they could not prevent, to demonstrate his medical skills. Yet what happened will hardly be believed in Rome: the Emperor who was so keen to have a European doctor did not make much of it when he was unexpectedly offered one. Before I reply to this I wish to call to mind the case of Fr. Nicolas Cima of the Order of St. Augustine, who came to the Court of Beijing to practice medicine and did not last a year there.[^p106-97] This can hardly have been known to those who thought that the Court was ready for a doctor. I do not say that this was unimportant, but what really worked against Dr. Borghese were: 1. He was young, which made the Emperor think he was inexperienced. <!-- PDF page 107 | book page 97 --> 2. He lacked medical books, which led the Emperor to conclude that he was negligent of his science. 3. When ordered to take the Emperor’s pulse, he gave a diagnosis of the Emperor’s state of health after a very brief effort, which was contrary to the art of medicine as practiced in China. 4. When he was asked on one occasion to prescribe some medicine, he did not do so from memory but made up the prescription from a standard work, which in Europe would be praiseworthy but in China detracts from the reputation of a doctor. 5. The Emperor learnt from his people that during the journey the doctor had not succeeded in curing the valet of His Illustriousness, who died in Beijing on the eight day after his arrival with the doctor continuing to offer hopes of recovery. 6. The doctor, as far as we can see, acted throughout the journey as His Illustriousness’s attendant and performed all personal services with his own hands, as is allowable in Europe, and entered Beijing clad in spring clothing.[^p107-98] Now there is nothing wrong with this, but the courtiers and the Emperor, seeing his spring clothing and the menial tasks which he performed for His Illustriousness out of Christian charity, thought that so excellent a doctor and so much valued by his Lord should go back with him to the Europe from which he came. What influence could the Beijing Fathers have had on all this?

Re 14: Since this complaint is about a serious crime, and against priests of God, it requires solid proof. We protest before God and the Holy See that this is a violent calumny against us which rests on mere suspicions. Many charges of this kind were made in Beijing by word of mouth and in writing, and we declare they are null since not produced in formal documents with sworn witnesses. If someone wishes us to refute these suspicions, let him accuse us in due written form, and if this does not happen, let us respond by appealing to the Holy See, to whose judgment we submit.

Re 15: I ask, when on earth did the Fathers arrange with the Emperor that they should not receive a visitation? This matter was first raised in the month of July 1705 when on instructions from His Illustriousness we informed the Emperor that this newcomer to the kingdom came wishing to be a Visitor, and would perform this task. We would have had to be out of our minds to petition for him to be admitted as a Visitor and simultaneously that he not be allowed to make a visitation. Secondly, on July 6th, after we made the two applications mentioned, the Emperor replied that this guest should not come, to avoid trouble. If at that time the Jesuits feared a visitation it was in their power to prevent a visitation. We had only to say that we agreed with the decision of

<!-- PDF page 108 | book page 98 -->

His Majesty in order to satisfy our obligation as ordered, which did not require a further attempt. But, on the contrary, we made a third and fourth application to allow the Illustrious Lord to come as a visitor. This certainly was not preventing him from making a visitation. Then the Most Illustrious Lord was carried into Beijing on his sickbed and was unable to carry out his duties on account of his illness from December 4th up until the end of January. So on January 22nd the Jesuits presented him with a memorial requesting that since the extent of his illness would not permit it, he should at least act through our Ordinary, the Lord Bishop of Beijing. Hence it may be inferred that we had no reason to fear that His Illustriousness would be unable to make a visitation in due time. If there was no fear, nor was there reason for us to prevent him making a visitation. It is generally agreed that His Illustriousness on December 25th and 26th—without any of us being present and without us knowing, closed up in his room—revealed to the mandarin intermediaries the nature and course of his duties as Visitor. On the same day, the 26th, he received through the same intermediaries an order from His Majesty that he was not to make a visitation of those of us who lived at the Court, giving as his reason the good living of our Fathers. We were totally ignorant of this until that evening when His Illustriousness himself told the Father Superior, Jean-François Gerbillon. When we heard it, we gave thanks for so great a testimonial from His Majesty. But we never impeded being visited. And certainly the Most Illustrious Lord did investigate the Jesuit houses in Beijing constantly, as is clear from his own accounts. This appears not only from the reports of the Most Reverend Lord Bishop of Beijing and of His Illustriousness but also from all the Christians and gentiles who, on the orders of His Illustriousness carried out by Fr. Appiani, were incited by scoundrels to tell tales against us with the promise of money. We knew about this but did not lay a finger on them.

Re 16: If any of the Jesuits exceeded the due limits of respect, or if they were accused and convicted in any particular case, then they deserve the appropriate punishment for their boldness. We often spoke about the [Lord Patriarch’s] outbursts of impatience, such as his trampling underfoot the petitions which greatly scandalized the Christians, and about the accusations of the French Fathers against the Portuguese, which gravely offended the mandarins and the Emperor. But these comments were due to an obligation on our consciences to, so far as we could, not allow the exuberant anger of His Illustriousness, the product of his sickness, to harm the innocent, ourselves, and the poor little flock of Christ. In doing this we thought only of acting for the common good.

Re 17: If we knew what this complaint of the Most Illustrious Lord about us not suppressing the agitation of the Christians means we might be able to give a correct answer. If by “suppressing” he means to dispose them to obey in silence <!-- PDF page 109 | book page 99 --> the arguments and exhortations of the Most Illustrious Lord, we were unable to suppress them. For it was a matter of the laws of their homeland and most serious penalties for breaking the law, from the temporal point of view, and from the spiritual, closing the doors of eternal life to their wives, children, and relatives, and to all their fathers. It was only right, then, for us not to impede, within the limits of the reverence due to His Illustriousness as Father and Judge, their explaining their reasons for rejecting his advice and their warnings about getting better advice on the opinions he was embracing. If by “suppressing” he means simply to quieten down agitation, we attempted to do so whenever we heard of it, and wherever this kind of bad behaviour could, humanly speaking, be prevented, we took care to prevent it. It certainly is up to him to prove the opposite.

Re 18: We don’t know what images this complaint may raise in the mind of Your Paternity. Let me expand on it. There were two bishops in Beijing: the Most Reverend Lord Bishop of Beijing, who was there for several months, and the Most Reverend Lord Bishop of Conon, who then came on June 29th. His Illustriousness, with laudable zeal, made every effort to announce to the Christians and gentiles the extraordinary event of the coming of these gentlemen. The Fathers did not block this effort. They faithfully interpreted these announcements every time His Illustriousness asked for it. There was no failure on the part of the Christians but the gentiles paid no attention, since it was difficult to persuade simple souls who knew nothing of Christ to honour someone for spiritual reasons when they think that they themselves are mere animals. The mandarin go-betweens were the worst offenders and were harangued by His Illustriousness through Fr. Appiani, who elevated the celestial qualities of bishops and lowered the Jesuits in their eyes, saying that the former were the ones who held the keys and handed on the higher sciences to the clergy while the latter and members of other religious orders were destined solely to teach children and the poor, and assist the sick and dying. They should look forward to receiving new and special benefits if His Majesty were to treat bishops and secular clergy with greater deference than the others. This speech made no impression because to gentile mandarins and a gentile emperor, no European is esteemed for his ecclesiastical rank alone; solely those who live under an ecclesiastical and religious discipline and ceaselessly perform services of a scientific nature day and night for them are esteemed. It is, God knows, no fault of ours if, after so many and such great promotions of spiritual power by His Illustriousness, the Emperor still doesn’t get it.

Re 19: This complaint is a vast and bitter sea. We know we are innocent but who can resist the waves and storms that will burst out of the Paris Seminary? The Most Reverend Bishop of Conon has suffered the humiliation of being <!-- PDF page 110 | book page 100 --> declared ignorant in Chinese affairs by the Emperor Kangxi. This is the man who in the year 1700 was proclaimed by the Seminary, before the Sacred Congregation and the Supreme Pontiff and even in print, as particularly skilled in the meaning of Chinese characters, of Chinese books, and of knowledge of things Chinese, and as having a reputation amongst the doctors of this nation as one of the most perspicacious and perceptive men in the world. Now, by trickery of the Jesuits, this Most Reverend gentleman is overthrown from his position of high reputation.

I beseech Your Very Reverend Paternity to cast your mind back to the extended exposition in section 5 of the test made of the Lord Bishop of Conon’s expertise in things Chinese which the Emperor authentically conducted in Tartary on 2 August 1706. If the Most Reverend Lord knew Chinese characters, books, and customs, he could and should have, better than anyone else, displayed it in this arena, where he could once and for all establish his reputation by giving a specimen of his teaching, his interpretations, and his practice. You would have had to be insane to predict that we would want to influence the Emperor to test the Chinese learning of the Most Reverend Bishop. [The Emperor] showed [the Bishop] some Chinese characters: he did not know them. [The Emperor] referred to some Chinese books: the Most Reverend Bishop knew neither the texts nor their interpretation and himself admitted his ignorance of them. If he had wished to practice humility certainly he could not have done more by renouncing his episcopacy and going off to a monastery. He cannot excuse himself on the grounds of humility for so enormously undermining his opinion as the only true one and promoting the opinion of the Jesuits, which he has up until now in what he said and wrote wanted to be thought false.

One question remains to be answered: who was responsible for the harm done to the Most Reverend Lord on this occasion? It is undeniable that he was invited by the Most Illustrious Lord on 29 January 1706 to come to Beijing. This was in the presence of our procurators: we did not ask for it nor did those who heard it even hope it would happen. A second thing that is undeniable is that, on the word of Fr. Appiani, the coming of the Lord Bishop was announced to the mandarin intermediary at the hot springs on June 1st. A third thing that is undeniable is that on the morning of the 16th, it was intimated by Fr. Appiani to the same mandarin that the Bishop’s arrival was imminent; he gave the man’s name, and that he held a European doctorate. And the final thing that is undeniable is that on June 30th the Lord Patriarch himself announced to the Emperor face-to-face, surrounded by all his household and the Jesuits, that this Most Reverend Lord together with his associate Fr. Guéty had arrived in Beijing the previous day; that he had been summoned by himself because he was outstanding <!-- PDF page 111 | book page 101 --> in his knowledge of Chinese and capable of explaining to the Emperor all those things which were in contention amongst the Europeans, and whether the teaching of Confucius could rightly be combined with the Christian religion. Given all these things, Your Paternity, do you believe it is right and proper for our adversaries to charge us, the Beijing Fathers, with being responsible for leading the Lord Bishop of Conon into a trap?

Re 20: How active we were in this matter the Emperor himself testified to when he complained indignantly that we were obstinate and impertinent in continually returning to the same sad song on behalf of those who had displayed such extreme hatred for us. In this regard Fr. Guéty had exceeded all the rules of prudence in the many things he said about us. I have given in the fifth section just a small sample of this. Although he later repented for what he said, he was not ashamed of having confessed in writing before the Court that he said such things inspired by hatred and envy. Although we also refuted what he said we nonetheless continued to intercede for him, and when the Emperor absolutely prohibited it we still put in a good word for him. Fr. Guéty remained dear to us despite his inconsiderate loquacity. It was not that the Emperor was greatly anxious that the Jesuits should be vindicated, but that he felt it was disgraceful to propose in his presence such wicked falsehoods aimed at harming the innocent. We fear that even worse things may happen than up to now and that we are not equal to preventing them.

Re 21: Your Paternity can find out what happened during the legation of Fr. Bouvet from the short letters he wrote during leisure moments in Guangzhou. Time is pressing on me and prevents me from dealing with it adequately.

Re 22: This complaint is an accusation of criminal conduct and the proofs should be presented to the recording angel by anyone wishing to make such allegations against God’s priests. More details are given under May 19th in the acts of what Fr. Appiani and Fr. Frossoloni did in Beijing to inject scruples in the Christians regarding the oath they had sworn, and how they spoke of the oath taken so as to question its truth. It suffices to say here that I have arranged for the oath taken by the Christians of Beijing, and duly confirmed by them, to be sent to Your Paternity through our procurators. It has been signed again at the bottom by those who are said to have withdrawn their assent. If these oaths are said not to have been accepted by His Illustriousness the Lord Patriarch, or that they did not reach the Lord Bishop of Beijing, the reason is that nobody named by the interpreters, who by their dubious methods had made them suspect to the Christians, were allowed to be admitted to the Most Illustrious Lord. Only those who swore oaths to the contrary were admitted.

Re 23 & 24: It is enough to refer to the oath [of denial] presented by Fr. Tomás Pereira to the Most Illustrious Lord on March 8th and to the repeated representations <!-- PDF page 112 | book page 102 --> of Fr. Kilian, as well as those of Fr. Antoine Thomas, which amongst other matters include authentic contracts relating to the purchase of a certain house made by our procurators. Re 25: Sad experience has taught us that, as the mandarin named Zhao suggested to the Lord Patriarch, the Emperor is like a very hard rock which cannot be scratched by cotton or silk; rub him with a gemstone and the rock proves harder than the gemstone, even if you persist in rubbing away at it. If you violate the respect due to him by resisting him, you will not get away without him losing his respect for you. But the respect owed is not measured by Roman scales but in a Chinese balance, which is considerably different. There are many things which in Europe are considered not blameworthy but here are regarded as a crime. If you read the daily records, whether ours or those written by the followers of the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch, you will see clearer than the light of day how often and how seriously the Most Illustrious Lord opposed the Emperor. But any opposition to the Emperor is, in China, to dishonour the Emperor, and to daily dishonour the Emperor is like jumping fully clothed into the Tiber and wanting not to get wet. Even if you did not want to get wet, how could we prevent it happening? All we can do is wipe you down and wring out your clothes. Once you have been wet you cannot be dried out without difficulty. In Beijing whatever happened had an effect on the reputation of His Illustriousness. The Emperor bestowed many disappointments as well as many favours on the Lord Patriarch, even dismissing him honourably, but his parting words were: “If you had sought to correct your errors, I would not have regarded you afterwards less kindly, but like a wise son who has acknowledged the error of his ways.”

A final comment to round out the twenty-fifth complaint. I add the following to conclude this long letter so that you can begin to grasp what the gentile courtiers thought of our subservience to the Most Illustrious Lord.

On July 26th, the Lord Patriarch complained to the mandarin Zhao that since Fathers Guéty and Appiani had been called to Tartary he would have nobody left now to be his interpreter. Zhao replied that all the others were still there and that he could call on anyone he wanted from those who remained. None would refuse to perform this service since he was the superior of them all. The Lord Patriarch followed this up by ordering his interpreters who were present to say to the mandarin that he was not their superior except in name. They were reluctant to interpret this since they wished to avoid giving scandal etc. and excused themselves. Finally the two of them went away despite the strong and angry insistence of His Illustriousness.

When Fr. Appiani came back in shortly afterwards the mandarin Zhao—an old friend of the Europeans and of our holy religion, who had understood the voice, expression, and gestures of the Lord Patriarch—had the following to say to the Most Illustrious Lord. For almost two hours, he strongly argued with him. Amongst other things he made the following comparison: “When you came to this court to conduct your business, let us say you saw before you a large and beautiful lake full of water which you thought was clean. Thinking to wash in it, you put your finger in it and it came out dirty. What would you do? Would you wash the finger that had been dirtied or wash the whole hand to remove the stain? But you believe that the whole thing has been dirtied. You were in too much of a hurry to put your hands in, and you are anxious to stick in your face and head too. Won’t this seem to the world to be what you are really doing?”

To this and the previous parables of the mandarin His Illustriousness replied: “What do you want me to do? Don’t you see how the Fathers of the Court treat me? What their opinion is of me? How gracefully they show their respects to me? You yourself can judge if this is done well or badly.”

The mandarin replied: “Don’t you pay your respects to the Fathers to some extent? I want you to understand this: that they partly respect you, but even more fear you. (The Chinese and Manchu words for ‘fear’ mean the highest degree of reverence). But I am not so easily deceived. I can tell the difference between a sincere and a feigned expression. I speak about what I see in the eyes and understand is in the mind.”

“The Emperor when he was in Tartary sent to ask me how the old Europeans were treating you. ‘Well,’ I replied. ‘The proof is that they are respectful to him in your absence, and foremost is their superior. Even more they fear him. They are very modest in his presence. They do not sit down unless he tells them to do so. They speak to him with an external appearance of genuine respect. If they read something to him, even if what they read is some kind of order of Your Majesty they read it standing up. Nor do they say a word that is not highly respectful.’ That is what I reported to My Lord Emperor.”

“You know, although I cannot understand you when you are speaking a European language, yet I can read your expression, see what is in your eyes and your violent gestures; your raised voice does not deceive me, I can follow the way your mind is working. I see them protecting your honour, when I and my associates ask those Europeans what you have been saying and they say nothing in your name except what displays a decent modesty and laudable mildness of manner. Hence we rightly infer that they pass over your words in silence from a desire to accommodate you and preserve your honour. If they had no regard for you, why would they continue to honour you for so many months? If they wanted to besmirch your honour, there was no need for them to do anything but <!-- PDF page 114 | book page 104 --> speak up and simply interpret everything that you uttered when carried away by your impatience. Believe me, we are splendid witnesses. What is more, they are the ones who point out to us that you are a man of high rank and outstanding virtue and ability; that you are highly regarded and for your good qualities have the confidence of the Supreme Pontiff. You are born of an illustrious family and your father has a rank comparable to our governors, etc.”

When the Most Illustrious Lord heard this he asked which of our people had said this, and the mandarin replied: “Does it matter who said it? It is enough for you to recognize that we cannot but have noticed these things, and from the moment you appeared in our land. If it were not for the Fathers who live with us and opened the way, you would not have come. They worked on your behalf in so many ways, so that the Emperor would be well disposed towards you. If this Appiani here, who is the only one of your men who can talk to us after a fashion, had spoken in this way to the Emperor, do you think he, a man unknown to the Emperor, would have had any influence?” This is what the mandarin said, and—on the same day, July 26th—immediately reported to us about his discussion with the Lord Patriarch in the presence of his servants, and witnessed by his associate the mandarin Fo.[^p114-99]
