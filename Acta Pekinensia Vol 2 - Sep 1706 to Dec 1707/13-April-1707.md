Although, at the beginning, I had undertaken to write solely a record of what happened in Beijing, and not to edit it as a history, but to relate events simply as they occurred from day to day in order to provide the material for a history to be composed at some future date, nevertheless since the departure of the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch from the royal capital of Beijing did not bring a conclusion to the whole affair, but rather brought with it a long chain of disasters which came tumbling down day after day throughout the whole of China, the sad necessity is imposed on me of recording many other things without which the events at Beijing cannot be properly understood. And so, whether I want to or not, I must include them although they are outside my original plan.

I made a similar statement before in the month of September 1706;[^p373-1] this now has to be repeated, because I am continually feeling that I am being called further away from the course I began, while to the reader who is examining the events at Beijing, my pen, even against my will, is presenting a hundred other matters gathered up throughout the provinces.

### 1. The order of the matters to be narrated herein

The events to be recorded for this month have to be divided into four classes, and they are not without connection to Beijing. Some of them are specific to those belonging to our house; another section concerns the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing and the Reverend Franciscan missionaries of the province of Shandong. Others relate to the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Ascalon, while still others relate to several other missionaries who met with His Majesty on his travels in order to request the permit. In the first place, I shall provide an account of the Shandong missionaries, because, with regard to them, there remain many matters which should have been mentioned in what was sent to Europe in the month of March in the year 1706, if I had known about them at the time.[^p373-2] Secondly I shall narrate at greater length the principal events relating to His Majesty’s journey. Thirdly, I shall give in greater detail the events at Beijing.

Fourthly, I will touch on the things which concern the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon, and they involve many events to be related in the months to follow.

With regard to those in Shandong, let us make a beginning with the record provided by the Reverend Franciscan Fathers, just as they sent it word for word in Latin, to their Rev. Fr. Provincial Commissioner, Fr. Bernardino das Chagas,[^p374-3] and to others. But let the reader take note that here we are dealing with a previous examination made at the time when the Reverend Fathers, as yet unaware of the publication of the Patriarch’s regulation, were seeking a permit from His Majesty.

### 2. The Franciscan Fathers are examined in the city of Linqing

The heading was as follows: An account of the examination of the Franciscan zhou[^p374-4] Fathers of the province of Shandong which was made by the Prince at the command of the Emperor. The text is as follows:

On the ninth day of March of the year 1707, in the city called Linqing zhou of the province of Shandong, at about the second hour after midday, the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing, Fr. Carlo da Castorano, Fr. Bernardo de la Encarnación, Fr. Francisco de la Concepción, Fr. Martin Alemán, Fr. Miguel Fernández, Fr. Francisco a S. José, and Fr. Antonio (Frossoloni), all of the Order of Our Holy Father Francis, were assembled at a secluded house within the walls. The first two, namely the Most Illustrious Lord and Father Carlo, were there to thank the Emperor for the piao they had received; the others, for the purpose of obtaining one. They were questioned by the Prince, the eldest son of the Emperor, regarding the Chinese rites and other matters, as reported below. They all arrived at the before-mentioned place and there standing in order outside the door awaited that Prince, who, immediately on his arrival, looked towards them kindly, | entered the house, and ordered them also to enter. He seated himself and they went down on their knees. Next their written petitions were shown to him, and also the Chinese translations made by Rev. Fr. Dominique <!-- PDF page 375 | book page 365 --> Parrenin of the Society of Jesus. They had barely glanced at the latter, although they believed that they had been made faithfully. The same Father had told them that their petitions would be written in the same form as that used by the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop and Fr. Carlo, a copy of which is included at the end.

After the due obeisances had been made to the Prince and answers had been given as to personal names, family names, age, religion, and time of arrival in China, he told them to be seated on the ground; then, when he had been informed of those who had already seen the Emperor, and about other matters which are irrelevant here, he called by name Fr. Antonio Frossoloni, whom he questioned about the following matters.

### 3. The list of questions posed by the Prince

1. Was he aware of what had been done with regard to a letter belonging to Father Guéty, which had been put into a secret place, and from whom did he learn this? Reply: Affirmative, and he learned this from Father Carlo who had been in Beijing.

2. Did he see the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch? Reply: He saw him both when he was passing through and on his return.

3. Did I or did we go out to meet him?[^p375-5] Reply: Affirmative.

4. Where is the Most Excellent Lord staying now? Reply: He does not know.

5. When we saw him, were we afraid of him? Reply: We were not afraid.

6. Did he abuse us verbally? Reply: We have heard no mention of such. Then, turning to Fr. Parrenin, he said with some show of compassion that the Most Excellent Lord had abused this fellow verbally, and the Father replied that such things were already over and done with.

7. Did we see Father Appiani when he went to Beijing? Reply: Fr. Parrenin answered that he could not have passed that way since he went to Beijing by the land route. After he said this, the Prince made some remarks deriding the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch, and especially saying that he is a man who does not obey the commands of the Emperor etc. To these remarks, no one gave an answer and moreover he had not told anyone to answer, but spoke these words in the form of a statement.

After this, he asked whether the Fathers would like a hot or cold drink. And when he heard that they wanted a cool drink he ordered it to be brought. In the meantime, so that he might more or less commit to memory our names, ages, <!-- PDF page 376 | book page 366 | manuscript page 551 --> etc. he began to read them and when he came to Lao Hongen[^p376-6] (Father Antonio da Frossoloni) he began to interrogate him again:

9. Was he aware of the things which the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Conon did in Fujian? Reply: Affirmative.

10. Did the Most Excellent Bishop of Conon act wisely or unwisely? Reply: I do not have a clear understanding of that, because at that time I had not yet arrived in China.[^p376-7]

11. Did the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon pass through this city? Reply: No.

12. Which of the two is right, the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon or Rev. Father Matteo Ricci? Reply: I came to China only a short time ago, and I know only a few characters, and as yet I have not yet seen the arguments or the reasoning of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon.

13. What is Father Antonio’s opinion regarding the Jing Tian (Revere Heaven) tablet given by the Emperor; or regarding the sacrifice for Confucius; or regarding the sacrifice for the dead? There was no reply to these three questions because they were asked rapidly, and immediately he put another question, namely:

14. Did they allow Christians to go to the tombs of their own dead? He expressed this by the words shang fuan,[^p376-8] which we will not translate.

15. A second time he asked Father Antonio to express his opinion by saying what he thought about the Revere Heaven inscription, about the sacrifice for Confucius, and about the sacrifice for the dead. And he added that Tian and the Christian Tianzhu are the same etc. Reply: I have not examined these questions in depth, and have cared only to preach the divine law.

16. Again he put a question: What is the Divine Law? Who is God? Reply: I said something in reply, and since this discourse seemed to be going on at length in explanation of the Divine law, the Prince interrupted it, saying:

17. Tell me, in summary (dagai,[^p376-9] briefly and to the point), | which teaching is more in conformity with the Divine Law (he used the term Daoli[^p376-10]), that of Fr. Ricci or the teaching of the Most Excellent Bishop of Conon? Reply: Since I have not examined these matters in depth, and since I have not come to know <!-- PDF page 377 | book page 367 --> the reasons and arguments of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, I do not venture to give a definite answer to this question.

18. Next the Prince questioned Fr. Bernardo de la Encarnación, asking him what were his opinions on these matters. Fr. Bernardo answered: I have followed the practice of Fr. Matteo Ricci.

19. A second time he asked: Fr. Matteo Ricci does not treat of the matters which are now controverted, and so tell us what you yourself think of them.[^p377-11] Fr. Bernardo, who did not hear this reiteration clearly, said: I have followed the practice of Fr. M. Ricci.

After he said this, there came drinks, which we in Italian call limonata. They were offered to the Fathers, who accepted them with due ceremony, and after this, they were offered a melon from the kingdom of Siam etc. The Prince started to speak in Manchu with Fr. Parrenin of the Society of Jesus after the drinks and the melon. He also put many questions to the Fathers and to the mandarin Zhang Bowang and also to Zhang Bitieshi.[^p377-12] He asked whether the former Chinese had been inside the church. He asked whether the building was new or old, and why it was not repaired if it was old, and why the Pope did not allow them to buy land to relieve their poverty.[^p377-13] He asked how far distant it was from the river. And when he had questioned them about many other things, again he asked Father Antonio Frossoloni:

20. To what religious order do you belong? Reply: That of St. Francis.

### 4. About the various religious orders and their names

21. Why did Father Guéty say he was of the Shengjiao hui?[^p378-14] Reply: If we are speaking of the Shengjiao hui— this is the Congregation of the Faithful, or the Congregation of the Holy Law—we are all ecclesiastics, and as such are all the same, but in addition to this, some join various religious orders. The Prince interrupted saying:

22. Father Guéty said that he is of the Shengjiao hui and you are of the Order of Saint Francis, and this man (pointing to Fr. Parrenin) is of the Society of St. Ignatius. What is the meaning of this? And regarding the Society of Jesus, he likewise added immediately: What is Jesus? Reply: He is the true God etc. and after his Incarnation in time he was made true man etc. Fr. Parrenin said the same.

23. Did he have knowledge of mathematics, arithmetic, etc.? Reply: No. And when the Prince said that he doubted whether in this matter we were telling the truth, Father Parrenin added: “Why would these Fathers want to lie? Truly they are unversed in these disciplines.” And he began to speak in Manchu with the Prince. And when Fathers Bouvet and Jartoux and Brother Paramino, members of the Society of Jesus, arrived, after many other remarks which are irrelevant, the Prince rose and departed and we followed him so that we could go in to the Emperor. Father Parrenin accompanied him, remaining close to him and speaking with him, and the Father, after returning to us, said in the presence of the Most Illustrious Lord and the Fathers these words: The Prince, just now, as we were walking along said this: that Father Bernardo is a good old man, without guile; but that Father Antonio is intelligent and very quick-witted, one who did not want to respond to his questions directly, but replied in a roundabout way and thus avoided them.[^p378-15] On that day we were not able to see the Emperor, because, according to what was said, he was too busy, nor did we see him on the following day.

### 5. The Franciscans are treated kindly by the Prince

I should mention that the Prince said many other things, and the Fathers too, and during the questioning the Prince and Fr. Parrenin spoke many times in Manchu, a language we did not understand. I should add that on this day the Prince was in a very good mood and treated us well, showing also by what he said that he <!-- PDF page 379 | book page 369 | manuscript page 552 --> wished to honour the Church and us. Moreover he was intrigued by our clothing since we wore no furs, and spoke with sympathy about our poverty. He also said that he would order the Governor to prepare our church since he would tell the Emperor to visit us on his way through; but if he does not come this way, that at least it would be something that does us honour and would be of benefit to the church. |

On the 10th, with much coming and going here and there, we finally accompanied the Prince, so that we could appear before the Emperor and receive the piao, etc. However since the Emperor was busy, as Father Parrenin told us from what he had learned from the Prince, in reading the letters he had received the same morning from the Governor of Sichuan, our insignificant gifts were presented only after a delay. From these, the Emperor accepted three or four things, and in the end ordered us to be told that we should not wait any longer for the permits, but that we should proceed to the bank of the river and go down on our knees as His Majesty passed by. But Father Parrenin, who reported these instructions to us, added with regard to the permits or piao that the Emperor on his return from the southern regions would give them to us in this city, and that we should be ready to come here to meet him.

To prevent any false statements in the future regarding the examination we had undergone, we agreed to make this summary report together and at the same time, and in testimony to this we wish to sign it with our own hands, in this city of Linqing zhou, on 11 March 1707.

I certify it is correct, Fr. Antonio a Frossoloni

I certify it is correct, Fr. Francisco a S. José

I certify it is correct, Fr. Miguel Fernández

I certify it is correct, Fr. Martín Alemán

I certify it is correct, Fr. Francisco de la Concepción

I certify it is correct, Fr. Bernardo de la Encarnación

I certify it is correct, Fr. Carlo da Castorano

Thus far we are following the report transcribed above, and which after considerable delay was sent from Guangzhou to Beijing on 22 September 1707, as can be seen in a letter of Rev. Fr. José Monteiro, Vice-Provincial, written on the day mentioned, in which he writes as follows: “With this dispatch, I hope, you will find an account that the Franciscan Fathers of Shandong have sent to their commissary relating the way in which they were given the piao, and which differs from what we had heard here earlier. I believe the Shandong version is not accurate in all respects.” Therefore let the kind and good reader refer to this place where in its own proper month I have provided the account which I promised, and I did this at once in the first place where it was possible for me to provide it.

### 7. Certain matters which should be noted in this account given by the Franciscan Fathers

In addition, let it be noted: 1. That in the report that was dispatched, on one and the same page there were two parts, the first of which was written in Latin as presented here, and which contains the petition for the letters patent; the second part was in Spanish and relates the events involved with the reception of the permits. The latter is kept back until June, in which month the reception of the permits took place, and this provides verification of the words cited from the letter of Father Monteiro. 2. The present account has been written out by me word for word in such a way that I did not venture even to correct grammatical errors.[^p380-17] 3. It is fairly evident that this was composed by an Italian rather than a Spaniard.[^p380-18] 4. That the author betrays himself, when, in the third question put to Father Frossoloni by the Prince, he writes in this form: Did I or did we go out to meet him? 5. That <!-- PDF page 381 | book page 371 | manuscript page 553 --> some matters are related in a rather hostile manner, especially with regard to the conversations in Manchu | between the Prince and Father Parrenin. 6. That the statement that the Chinese translations of the applications were shown by Fr. Parrenin to the Prince but not shown to the Franciscan Fathers is incorrect. For in discharging the duties of my office I met with Fr. Parrenin, and he said quite clearly that there was one document in Chinese which the Reverend Fathers had sent to him containing only the names, country of birth, and date of their arrival in China, and that there was another translation of the document, a copy of which is included at the end of the narrative, but this one was in Manchu and therefore was not shown to them, because the Reverend Fathers did not know Manchu. On this first occasion, when missionaries were arriving from distant places, the Jesuit followed the ordinary curial style. But then, later on, in the case of other missionaries from the South and those from Shandong too after the return of the Emperor, a change had to be made in the month of June; all the other applications of the missionaries from then on were translated into Chinese, to avoid many difficulties that could be occasioned by mistrust 7. It has come to my attention that this narrative was signed on March 11th, and the document, as Fr. Frossoloni noted, on March 7th, although it is said to have been presented only on the 9th. But that happened because Father Parrenin sent to the Most Illustrious Bishop on the 7th the permit which the Emperor had promised to His Most Excellent Lordship in Beijing, and also advised that the missionaries who were going to request the same should immediately prepare written petitions after the model of the ones made by the Most Illustrious Lord and Fr. Carlo da Castorano.

What has been reported so far happened in Linqing up to March 11th and on the 12th of the same month. Fr. Girolamo Franchi went there from Beijing to bring to the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop a letter from the Lord Patriarch with each of his decrees, as well as a letter written by Fr. Antoine Thomas, the substitute Vice-Provincial, on March 12th, and also one from Father Gerbillon, as well as a general instruction. What this Father did in that place would certainly have been explained in the month of March, had not his letter, although sent by express messenger, arrived only when April was well advanced, at the same time as the reply which he had brought back with him from Beijing. And, indeed, I think it will be the case that if I relate the points of this statement of ours before I relate the outcome, posterity will understand better on what foundations the Beijing Jesuits wished to build everything which they foresaw had to be done and endured in such a fateful situation.

### 8. The opinion of the Jesuits of Beijing as to what should be done after the publication of the Lord Patriarch’s decree

Therefore, they recommended to Fr. Franchi that he should make humble and efficacious representation to the Lord Bishop, his Ordinary: 1. That the Jesuits do not oppose the Patriarch’s regulation, nor do they seek an exemption from it, nor up until now have they appealed against it: not because they judge it cannot be appealed but because they believe that any action taken against it would be useless, since they feel it is impossible to prevent such a prohibition of the rites, its publication, and the pulling down of the Jing Tian tablet from becoming known to the Emperor. And when they become known, they can come to no other conclusion than that the Emperor will do to all the missionaries at the very least that which he did to the Lord Bishop of Conon, and to Fathers Mezzafalce, Guéty, and Appiani. Therefore, since the inflexibly stubborn will of the Lord Patriarch is well-known to them from other situations, the Fathers consider that he should not be further provoked by making a reply, or by making appeals on the basis of the certain destruction of the mission, but that they should submit in humble obedience. All this was based on the supposition that the Pope had indeed made his decision. And they decided this, even though they had no doubt that as a result of their obedience they would all be driven out of China and even though they knew that there were excellent grounds for thinking they were not bound to give their obedience—and that even if they did submit their obedience they would not be bound to execute it until such time as the Lord Patriarch, with due process, placed before the local ordinaries and others the decision of the Supreme Pontiff on which he based his regulation.

2. It is in every respect desirable that the Most Illustrious Ordinary of Beijing should request that, according to the requirements of Law, | he should be shown the decree of His Holiness our Lord Clement XI from which the Lord Patriarch has derived all the force of his regulation.

3. If the said decree should be shown to him either in the original or in an authenticated transcript, he should see to it that it is communicated to the missionaries who are his substitutes in the preaching of the Gospel, in legal form.

4. That if inspection of the document is denied, the Most Illustrious Ordinary will take into consideration not just a fear of some grave danger but the present and actual peril of his flock, and therefore will have recourse either to canon law or to entreaties to influence him not to put this regulation into execution.

### 9. What should be done by the Bishop of Beijing

5. It seems that the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop himself will be under threat of rather grave danger because after long hesitation on the part of the Emperor, he finally was given a residence <!-- PDF page 383 | book page 373 --> permit on the grounds that he had previously observed all those things which the laws of the country prescribe for the civil rites; in addition he promised to observe them, at least according to the Emperor’s understanding. If it should now happen that, openly disregarding the undertaking signed with his own hand after an audience with His Majesty and after so much difficulty experienced in obtaining the permit, he should make no attempt whatsoever, either by word or by written submission, to divert the Lord Patriarch from putting something like this into execution—hindering him in every possible way, whether by persuasion or by warning—and if also he does not attempt to preserve the sheep of his flock and the subjects of the empire in the freedom of the law of their fatherland according to the permission granted by Alexander VII, then certainly it appears that the Emperor will be enraged and there will follow all the consequences of an emperor’s anger. He will be all the more enraged on this account when he gets to know that the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing, after receiving the imperial permit, has taken an oath to obey the Lord Patriarch’s regulation, since His Majesty will take it as having been done with intent to insult him personally.

### 10. The Emperor acts with fairness; his kindness to the Christians

6. The Emperor will consider that he has a just cause for being angry and the whole of China will approve of the consequences of the royal anger, because the Europeans are rejecting His Majesty’s declaration with regard to the Chinese rites and teachings. For everyone will say that no European has a better understanding of their meaning than the most learned of the Chinese. It is also beyond doubt that no interpretation of this has been given or ever will be given by anyone at all, whether Chinese or European, with authority equal or greater than that demonstrated by the Emperor for everyone to see. Since, however, all authority to the contrary is based on the Bishop of Conon, and the Emperor has formally demonstrated this gentleman’s ignorance of Chinese matters, and His Majesty himself has moreover publicly given an interpretation of these teachings and rites and has caused his royal interpretation to be officially conveyed to the Lord Patriarch, to be dispatched to the Holy See, and when [the Patriarch] refused to send it, [the Emperor] sent that document reinforced by all the formalities of a legal appeal to the Supreme Pontiff, leaving it entirely to His Holiness to judge whether such an interpretation of the teachings and rites of the Chinese was in conformity or not with the Christian religion, and whether to leave the missionaries in China or to recall them according to the information and knowledge he derived from this exhaustive appraisal; since, I say, the Emperor considers that he has acted wisely, moderately, lovingly, and with zeal for the Christian religion, which he has said he wants to flourish in his empire;

<!-- PDF page 384 | book page 374 | manuscript page 555 -->

since, in addition, he has imposed fairly heavy penalties on those who refused to assent to his imperial decisions, as is clear from his cancellation of the legation to the Supreme Pontiff, from the exile of the Lord Bishop of Conon and of Father Mezzafalce and Father Guéty, and likewise from the arrest of Father Appiani; therefore, it cannot be doubted that those must be considered by the whole Chinese nation as having with audacity insulted the Majesty of China who now have raised their right hand to take an oath against the Chinese rites, and then stretched out their left hand to accept the permits which reflect the opposite view and are normally granted by His Majesty Himself. |

### 11. In these matters the Beijing Fathers have no power and no authority

7. The Lord Patriarch is looking to the Beijing Fathers for a remedy for the misfortunes which are the consequences of such things as these. But they, being as it were virtually powerless, could find absolutely no remedy after they had tried everything and done all they could, as the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing partly saw for himself and partly without doubt learned from others. The Emperor is refusing to admit the Jesuits to his presence, and has forbidden them to intercede in the future for any Europeans in order to obtain permits, and forbidden them to say a word about the person or the business of the Lord Patriarch. How, now after a new cause of offence, and one far more serious than the former ones, will they be able to go so far as to intercede and to seek to undo this regrettable action, which is detested in the whole of China, namely the publication of the regulation? The only thing which perhaps could be requested by the Fathers would be that like those found guilty of a crime they might be allowed to prostrate themselves before the doors of the palace to beg for forgiveness or compassion. But just now the Emperor is away, and covering long distances every day is hastening to that place where he will look on the one who roused his anger (which perhaps by now is increasing to rage) and watch his tragic activity from close at hand.

### 12. They had absolutely nothing to do with influencing the Emperor to require permits

8. The Beijing Fathers, since there was nothing else they could do, sent a letter to the Lord Patriarch through their Father Vice-Provincial begging him to deign to take into consideration that, from mere suspicion conceived against the Jesuits of Beijing, he had reached the point of driving the mission to destruction. Therefore, for the present he should take a step backwards while there was still time, especially since, as Fr. da Silva the Vicar Apostolic and Fr. Monteiro the Vice-Provincial have testified, His Excellency has acknowledged that the destruction of the mission would result from this regulation of his. To allay suspicion, the Beijing Fathers testified by an oath given in writing that they <!-- PDF page 385 | book page 375 --> did not concur in any way with the Emperor’s plan for the permits. Much less did they do anything to promote it, but on the contrary used their best efforts to obstruct it, until they were rebuffed by the Emperor with the command that the law regarding the reception of permits should be published throughout all the provinces without delay, and that all the Europeans who did not have them should be expelled from China, or should immediately present themselves at Beijing to apply for them.

### 13. The advice given to the missionaries belonging to the Society

9. In the situation as it existed, it seemed good to the Beijing Fathers, after the publication of the Patriarch’s regulation, to advise their men scattered throughout the provinces that those who were unwilling to take the oath to obey the regulation should leave the country voluntarily, and that those who wished to take the oath should be free to do so, but let them refrain from coming to receive the residence permit. It was deemed inappropriate for them to leave immediately because this perhaps to some people in Europe would seem to suggest impudence, and to others cowardice; therefore the men in Beijing advised their companions rather to incline to the choice of showing themselves ready to take the oath in obedience to the Holy See, on the supposition that His Holiness Our Lord Clement XI had actually made that decision. But they were to refrain from accepting permits and should wait at their own churches until they were ordered to leave either by the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch or by the mandarins, or some miraculous remedy came from heaven.

### 14. The reasons for this advice

10. The reasons why the Beijing Jesuits thought this way, and gave this advice to others, in addition to those mentioned, were that by accepting His Majesty’s permits and taking an oath to the contrary according to the regulation, the Emperor would judge that an obvious deception had been practised at his expense: one that was unworthy of the missioners and all Europeans, one that should be avenged with fire and sword. Secondly, that if the Emperor resorted to such ferocity, no missionary, even if he shed his blood, would in the present circumstances be a martyr because he was responsible for his own death by doing what he could have and should have avoided. That in itself could not be the basis of martyrdom; instead we would all suffer as rebels, criminals, deceivers, and men disdaining the secular authority over civil ceremonies which no one so far has been able to prove are evil in themselves. And no one is certain that they have been prohibited by the Holy See, nor does the Lord Patriarch himself assert this, except in an obscure package of words by means of which he presents his regulation in the form of a pontifical decision. In fact he does this not without a very strong presumption <!-- PDF page 386 | book page 376 | manuscript page 556 --> to the contrary | because he refuses to show the pontifical decision as required by canon law, and from his first setting foot in China, at a time when it was certain and evident that as yet no pontifical decision had arrived, he not only boasted that a pontifical decision to the contrary had been made and ordered the Chinese rites to be abolished without any investigation but also constantly and methodically refused to allow anything relating to them to be submitted to his tribunal in legal form, when by this the foundation of the permission given by Alexander VII might have been established. But the Emperor’s decision which could not be denied was disregarded by him right up to the time of his departure and its effects thwarted.

### 15. The Lord Patriarch wants his decree to be concealed from the Emperor. It is impossible to do this altogether, or certainly for long

11. As was mentioned above, the Lord Patriarch acknowledges that the destruction of the mission is certain on the supposition that the regulation published by him is revealed to the Emperor. He so testified to certain people in Nanjing, especially in the presence of the Vicar Apostolic of the place,[^p386-19] and of our Father Vice- Provincial. And so, through these mentioned, he made a request to the Beijing Fathers in general and individually by name, that by every means in their power they might prevent the missionaries who should come up to receive their permits from being questioned about those matters concerning which he had formulated definite responses in his regulation and had ordered them to be given under penalty of excommunication.

12. Let us suppose, but not concede, that this oath to abide by the regulation and the regulation itself can be concealed from the Emperor for the time being; nevertheless it will not be able to be concealed for one or two years because under penalty of excommunication the missionaries are ordered to swear that they will judge and thus will teach in opposition to the rites. And what will be taught in China will be proclaimed everywhere from the rooftops. And whatever will be said against them will be accompanied by ecclesiastical penalties, by the denial of the sacraments to those not assenting to the patriarch’s regulation. All of these things certainly cannot be concealed for long from the Emperor, who is now extremely watchful and interested in the missionaries and Christians. It is therefore physically and morally impossible for the Beijing Jesuits to bring it about that knowledge of this does not reach the

<!-- PDF page 387 | book page 377 -->

Emperor. And since it is so impossible to keep such things secret from His Majesty, it is also morally impossible, and the attempt definitely cannot and should not be made to conceal by positive action by the Fathers such things which, as it can certainly be foreseen, will shortly afterwards burst forth causing greater indignation and more extreme danger for our holy religion.

### 16. What responsibilities the Bishop of Beijing has at this time of very great peril to his flock

13. In regard to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing there are several considerations which give him the right and perhaps an obligation that, for the sake of preserving his flock which is now in a situation of extreme peril, he should attempt seriously, according to law and with authority, to deflect the Lord Patriarch from such a plan which will be destructive of the Christian cause in China and neighbouring kingdoms. For example:

First, because the Lord Patriarch rests his case on the decision of His Holiness Clement XI, which he neither claims he actually has nor has shown to anyone who asks to see it, as the law requires if anyone wishes to cast their eyes upon it. Moreover he wrote to the Bishop of Conon and to the Bishop of Beijing himself in the month of January saying it was not yet in his possession, and he further said when in Nanjing that it was stolen from him by the people of Macau before it came into the hands of his men, to be sent on to himself.[^p387-20]

Second, because when the Vicar Apostolic of Nanjing and the Vice Provincial asked that the Lord Patriarch should at least testify in writing that they requested the pontifical decision be shown to them, he refused their lawful petition.

Third, because in his regulation, right at the beginning, he set down propositions which in the pontifical decision (supposing that it existed) could not have been included, since there was never any consideration of such matters in Rome; but these came into existence only in China when the Bishop of Conon, to avoid discussion of the controversies, produced them to the Emperor, as was known to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing who was then present in the palace.[^p387-21]

<!-- PDF page 388 | book page 378 | manuscript page 557 -->

Fourth, because these very propositions have no other purpose except to ensure that all the missionaries are compelled to say exactly the same, namely what was at that time through ignorance presented by the Bishop of Conon, as the Lord Patriarch himself knew. And so | on July 2nd he did everything in his power to have it suppressed by the mandarins, as is well known to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing who was then present in the capital.

Fifth, because in the said propositions presented by the Bishop of Conon, the Emperor showed that he was grievously offended by what was said and written by someone who to some extent represented the Lord Patriarch and the Supreme Pontiff himself. Wherefore to blatantly produce all these once again in the person of all the other missionaries has no other purpose except the obdurate defence of the position taken by the Bishop of Conon, which, (at least according to Chinese ideas of rights) involves the most serious possible insult to the Emperor.

Sixth, because the remaining propositions in the regulation are extremely indefinite and vague, so that it seems that no one, by invoking the name of God regarding a meaning so vague and indefinite, could take an oath without a suggestion of temerity etc.

### 17. The decree of Alexander VII still stands

Seventh, because from the wording of the regulation it is clear enough that the decree of Alexander VII still stands, since the Lord Patriarch does not dare to state that it has been revoked, much less declared null, as is obvious to anyone who thinks about it.

Eighth, because the Lord Patriarch only prohibits interpretation of his regulation in any other way by invoking the decree of Alexander VII.

Ninth, because he says that the interpretation of that decree pertains to him by reason of a special faculty he has; if therefore he alone has the right to interpret it, the decree has not been revoked or annulled.

Tenth, because the Lord Patriarch has not proved to any local ordinary that this extraordinary faculty extends so far that by his interpretation he can change anything that has been decided by Supreme Pontiffs.

Eleventh, because even if such a faculty had been granted him, nevertheless in actual fact the decree of Alexander VII would not be being interpreted, and he has not proved that it has been revoked, but is himself revoking it and annulling it.

<!-- PDF page 389 | book page 379 -->

Twelfth, because under oath, and under the penalty of excommunication, he wants to oblige the missionaries to believe and teach that the Chinese, by the words Tian and Shangdi, have not signified or do not signify the true God. This is something on which Europeans may not lawfully and validly take an oath, because it can never become so evidently true to any one of the Europeans that he could, with the Emperor declaring the contrary, without temerity invoke the name of God in relation to it and hold the Gospels in his hand—although perhaps a European might be able to take an oath on the grounds that the authority of the Emperor etc. is not convincing for him.

### 18. Various reasons which weaken the Lord Patriarch’s decree

Thirteenth, because not even the Supreme Pontiff can impose the obligation of such an oath unless he has already had a private revelation or some physical evidence, etc.

Fourteenth, because the Lord Patriarch is ordering an oath to be taken rejecting the words Tian and Shangdi, because they do not signify the true God, at this time when their meaning is by far more clearly and evidently established than at any other time. For, although the Lord Patriarch—to the detriment of one of the parties and of our holy religion in its entirety—in the month of July 1706 prohibited discussion with the Emperor regarding the sense of these terms, nevertheless the Emperor has spontaneously and quite frequently testified formally that he has read the book written by Fr. Ricci in which this man proved that the words Tian and Shangdi signified previously and still do signify what Tianzhu signifies for Christians. He has also testified that in this matter Ricci wrote what was correct, and that no European has written better about God and this whole subject. Moreover, in agreement with Ricci, he has said and he has also put it in writing that these very words, on the strength of the idiom of China, express the idea of the divinity better, more perfectly, and more sublimely for the Chinese than the words Tianzhu.

Fifteenth. The present situation is also aggravated by this: that the Lord Patriarch wants an oath to be taken, subject to the most severe penalties, that the true God has never been known nor is now known by the Chinese under the names of Tian and Shangdi, when the whole of China happily agrees with the Emperor’s declaration which was mentioned above; this is a most powerful argument in favour of the conversion of this nation and the good of the Church of God, and it demonstrates, in spite of the wickedness of Satan, that from the descendants of Noah the Chinese have not been atheists, and that a clear path is opened for the Gospel. And also the missionaries are able to show starting from the heart of Chinese teaching that they are announcing what was being worshipped by the Chinese even though they were unaware of it.

<!-- PDF page 390 | book page 380 | manuscript page 558 -->

Sixteenth, because the Lord Patriarch | does not allow dispensations from his regulation on the grounds of great danger; however he does not forbid them in a situation of very great danger, because he recognizes the existence of a great danger but gratuitously chooses to believe that a very great one can be averted by the Beijing Jesuits. But the Jesuits, through long experience and through knowing accurately the disposition of the Emperor and of the whole court, have taken a canonical oath that, as they hope for eternal salvation, they judge with their whole hearts that they have no remedy to avert this very great and very imminent peril of the destruction of our holy religion and of its exclusion from this empire forever.

Seventeenth, because the frequently mentioned declaration of the Emperor still stands, and he has rejected the authority of any Chinese who hold the contrary opinion, the missionaries would be taking a false oath if they took an oath according to the regulation of the Lord Patriarch, since it is now evident that the terms Tian and Shangdi signify the true God, at the very least, with the same force as the evidence that in China the word ren[^p390-22] signifies a rational person consisting of a body and a rational soul, or that the word shen[^p390-23] in China signifies “spirit.”

Eighteenth, because the Lord Patriarch wants his regulation to be based solely on a pontifical decree which he does not yet possess, and even if he did have it, would be based on the presumed truth of the facts presented, and on the competence of the informant, that is of the Lord Bishop of Conon; and, the falsity of the facts presented and the incompetence of the informant have been exposed with all the proper formalities.

Nineteenth, because the Lord Patriarch, having suppressed the pontifical decree (supposing he actually has it) wants the missionaries to rely entirely on the authority of his person, and he wants the Emperor to be told through them that the Supreme Pontiff is infallible and that he is in himself an infallible spokesman[^p390-24] for the Supreme Pontiff, and the last part of the proposition as it stands cannot be upheld in any sound theology.

### 19. These reasons are communicated to the Lord Patriarch

These were more or less the points of the information which you should know, and they were communicated to Fr. Franchi on the 10th or 11th of March at the same time as Rev. Fr. J.F. Gerbillon had also written to his subjects that they should give consideration as to whether it was more advisable to accept the permit and <!-- PDF page 391 | book page 381 | manuscript page 559 --> to remain suspended according to the decision of the Lord Patriarch until such time as Rome should give an answer in these matters. Likewise, you should note that this was not sent to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing but was provided for the information of Fr. Franchi, so that when opportunity presented itself, he might submit it to his most reverend Ordinary and should explain to him in what sense the Jesuits were saying that they would take an oath in compliance with the regulation.

Now let us take a look at the outcome. The first is from a letter of Fr. Franchi written from Linqing to Father Soares and dated March 21st. This is what it says:

After a journey of seven days, that is on morning of the Feast of St. Joseph,[^p391-25] I arrived in Linqing zhou. All the Franciscan Fathers together with Father Frossoloni asked for a piao. They were all well-received by the Prince and were promised a piao, which however will be given to them only when the Emperor returns from the South. No one saw the Emperor except from the bank of the river. Among the letters which I was delivering to the Most Illustrious Bishop there was included the regulation sent by His Excellency. Whatever considerations I heard in Beijing, whatever I brought with me in writing, and whatever occurred to me on the way as well as from the time I arrived here, I proposed to the Most Illustrious Bishop: namely that for the mission which was about to perish (and this was after I had already given up hope of inducing him either to appeal or to suspend it) he might at least allow a delay in pressing for the execution of the regulation. And I did this to the end that at least the Beijing Jesuits would not be bound to give the answers required by the regulation to the Emperor when he returned to the court (I understand this in case they also were bound to receive the piao) and consequently arouse the Emperor’s anger against themselves on the grounds that they were, as it would seem, following the Bishop of Conon, who had been convicted of ignorance in preference to the Emperor who, on a question of fact, can and ought to be relied upon rather than any European, whoever he might be. And the result would be that they would be banished from the empire, and along with them religion would be driven into exile forever; but I was able to achieve nothing.

### 20. He does not consent

The Most Excellent Bishop proposed three things: 1. The example of Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial and of the Most Rev. Fr. da Silva. 2. That it should be written to Beijing | that all of those who are prepared to do so should <!-- PDF page 392 | book page 382 --> take the oath if there is persistence in requiring that oath. 3. In the regulation, excommunication is mentioned also for bishops.

To this last, I answered, that, by virtue of the regulation, bishops are able to defer pressing for the execution of the regulation at least for a time, especially if, otherwise, the certain ruin of the mission is imminent, without incurring excommunication. I said that the two Fathers who had been mentioned had no other course than to be exiled from the empire, and also that Beijing Jesuits would take an oath regarding everything which the Holy See prescribed; nevertheless, that they were earnestly requesting that the decree of His Holiness should be made available in the normal way, so that with the greater composure they may be able to face not so much exile as the damnation of souls which hereafter will cry out before the Divine tribunal against those responsible for the destruction. But in response to all of this, there was nothing to raise my hopes. He merely said that already in two letters he had described to the Lord Patriarch the danger to the mission, but now in fact he would request that the decree of His Holiness should be produced (in preceding letters the Lord Patriarch wrote to the Most Illustrious Bishop to say that he did not yet have the decree). I replied that it would be quite useless to produce the decree after oaths had been taken, and after all the missionaries had experienced the effects of the Emperor’s just indignation. If it were to be produced before the oaths were taken, it might perhaps be seen to contain a clause by means of which both the mission and the intention of the Holy See might be preserved intact.

His Lordship said he had already written to the Lord Patriarch to the effect that if in fact he did not have the decree as yet he should not hasten to make any promulgation. These letters from the Bishop reached the Lord Patriarch on the second day after they had been sealed. The Lord Patriarch replied to them at the time but made no mention of that matter. There is here and now no hope left except in God and in the holy patron of the mission. And this is the unhappy result of my journey … Now only Father Frossoloni is here, who after he heard that the Regulation had been promulgated gave up all hope regarding the piao.

### 21. The reasons why the decree of the Supreme Pontiff should be shown to the missionaries, supposing that there is one

But now, when the basis of our oath cannot be settled except by evidence of the mind of the Holy See from the Lord Patriarch, a man whom the courtiers have at times censured for lying, it will happen <!-- PDF page 393 | book page 383 | manuscript page 560 --> that the Emperor will be all the more embittered against the Jesuits because they put their trust in such a man. ### 27. The formula of the oath, or the regulation, is promulgated in various places

Fernández, who affirmed that it was promulgated also in the cities of Linqing zhou and Dongchang fu.[^p398-37] In fact this is so well known that it needs no proof, and in what follows it will quite frequently be obvious. There is still more which seems to settle what happened between the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop and Father Franchi with regard to the obligation and the promulgation of the Patriarch’s regulation. The Father was about to set out for the district of Guangwuer in Holy Week on an annual mission to provide for the spiritual needs of those converts. Since he knew that a Jing Tian tablet was still kept there, he first had recourse by letter to his Ordinary to find out what was to be done regarding the actual promulgation of the Patriarch’s regulation. To be brief I shall give here word for word the second answer which the Lord Bishop gave. With regard to the matter about which you consulted me relating to the village of Guangwuer zhuang, I answered you promptly as I did likewise in the case of your second letter on the same subject, when you were about to depart from Ji’nan fu; on both occasions I said to you the same in substance. And to that first letter of mine, Your Reverence replied to me immediately on 26 April from the said village in a manner that seemed to me to be not very friendly and contrary to all my expectations; after this I myself was not able, with the circumstances being as they are, to respond to you with more courtesy and friendliness than I did, restricting myself to a general response, which is to obey the orders of the Holy See and of Monsignor the Patriarch. As a result of this <!-- PDF page 401 | book page 391 | manuscript page 564 --> you are resentful beyond what I could ever have imagined; because you immediately abandoned the Christian community, you are unwilling to offer Mass and to give communion to women who have already made their confession on the grounds that they have already fulfilled the paschal precept. Did you want me to say to you that you should leave the Jing Tian in that church after the publication of the condemnation by the said Monsignor the Patriarch, and after it was observed by the Very Rev. ### 29. The Lord Patriarch is annoyed because some have requested permits

This month, first a rumour reached Beijing, and then it became quite certain that the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch was not only annoyed because the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing and Rev. Fr. Carlo da Castorano and others had asked for permits in the month of January, but also wished to oblige them to make reparation in some way or other on the grounds that in that signed document which they presented to the Emperor in order to receive the permits, they were guilty of many offences; for example, against ecclesiastical liberty, the rights of the papacy, and the rules of the canons. These complaints made by the Lord Patriarch had an upsetting effect on the spirits of the missionaries, especially of those who after they had presented their written application had not yet received their permits. On this matter there arose various opinions as to what should be done. On the one hand the fact could not be denied for the document was there written with their own hand, and on the other the Lord Patriarch’s displeasure could not be restrained. And their fear was all the more intense, because he had equipped himself with new decrees already drawn up and with ecclesiastical penalties. It was scarcely possible to take a middle course. Finally, an excuse or some form of satisfaction was discovered which would suffice at least for the time being, unless the Lord Patriarch made some other demand.

The following is a copy of the apology which was drawn up and delivered by the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing and which corresponds with the authentic transcript kept in the archives of the Society of Jesus in Beijing.[^p402-44] There was little difficulty in obtaining it, because His Illustriousness sent it not only to <!-- PDF page 403 | book page 393 | manuscript page 565 --> the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch, who was staying at Macau, and to the Most Excellent Bishop of Ascalon, but communicated it to others also in the province of Guangdong and of Shandong. And it reads as follows: |

### 30. The Bishop of Beijing explains to the Lord Patriarch why he requested the royal permit

Jesus Mary Francis

When first we came to know in the month of December of last year, 1706, of the decree issued by the Emperor of this land of China by which some ministers were driven into exile from this kingdom, and that orders were being given to the viceroys and military officers of the gentiles that no European missionary was to be permitted to live in this his empire unless he had his own royal permit, we considered that our office required us not be like a hireling who at the first sight of wolves and on hearing their howling should run away and abandon the sheep entrusted to our care, but to follow the example and the instruction of Our Lord Jesus Christ to guard them and to defend them as far as was possible.[^p403-45] And so, even though we were not in the best of health, and in the rigours of mid-winter, being prepared freely to lay down our lives for the sheep entrusted to us, we resolved to hasten to that place from which disaster threatened; namely, to enter the presence [of the Emperor][^p403-46] and to request a permit which would provide us with the right to remain in his kingdom. For without such a document, as we found out from the Jesuits who lived in the court, and as Doctor Giovanni Borghese wrote to us with great insistence, we were all to be driven out of China. And the said gentleman earnestly exhorted us, for the sake of souls, not to refuse to undertake the toil of this journey. Therefore we undertook the journey with one companion and gained admission to the royal court. When the Emperor was informed of our presence and our intention of requesting a permit, on 22 January 1707 in the presence of the Prince, the Emperor’s eldest son, we were brought in and questioned by him about our birthplace, our age, etc., as is contained in the enclosed document. He ordered us at the end of the examination to present on the very next day all the questions and answers we had given, written in our own handwriting. We did this, and it went as follows:

I, Friar Bernardino della Chiesa, an Italian, of the Franciscan Order, 62 years of age, from my boyhood studied the Humanities and then Philosophy and Theology. I have lived in China for 23 years. At present I am living in Linqing zhou, assisting those working for the Holy Law and preaching the same. I have <!-- PDF page 404 | book page 394 | manuscript page 566 --> followed the way of Father Matteo Ricci and of the other ancient Fathers, and for that purpose I will remain in this kingdom for the whole of my life. As a guarantee of this (for thus the Emperor orders) I have written this with my own hand, at Beijing, the 23rd day of January of the year of the Incarnation of the Lord, 1707, the 47th year[^p404-47] of the Emperor Kangxi, the 12th month, 20th day.

Friar Bernardino as indicated above, with my own hand.

However, because we had been recognized by the mandarins and by the Prince as being in some way in the employment of the Lord Patriarch there was a bar to my remaining in China.[^p404-48] But with the Fathers of the Society of Jesus who lived at the court interceding on my behalf by various means and over several days, not without great difficulty they finally obtained for us the permit we sought. And for this we offer them thanks and a grateful heart for the favour. From more than one source we have come to hear that this action of ours is not being truthfully reported by some and by others is being criticized and judged in a pejorative sense, but we believe and hope that in the eyes of God, for the protection of whose sheep we endured this labour and overcame feelings of reluctance, it will be pleasing and meritorious, as our conscience testifies to us. To justify ourselves in their eyes, (for we are debtors both to the wise and the unwise) in this present document we inform them and draw their attention to the facts that: 1. The said document which was tendered to the prince was prepared by us only at the order of the said prince or the emperor and not contrived or requested in any other way; 2. it was provided by us so that we would be able to remain in China for the good of souls, and to avoid banishment from this empire.

Secondly, as regards the content of the document, we declare: 1. That we did not mention our episcopal dignity to | avoid giving an opening for irrelevant questions; 2. so that, as the ordinary of this episcopate, we should not be forced to write anything about the Chinese controversies to the Pope or against the person of the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch; and 3. to avoid receiving a command from the Emperor to take care that his commands were carried out by the faithful in our diocese.

Thirdly, we remark: Nevertheless the nature of our office was explained, and the reason for our remaining in China, by these words: “assisting those working for the Holy Law and preaching the same.”

<!-- PDF page 405 | book page 395 -->

### 31. He says that he has followed the practices of Father Matteo Ricci

Fourthly, we said that I have followed the practices of Father Matteo Ricci and of the other Fathers of earlier times. Regarding this, I point out: 1. That the questioning was a general one, and the answers likewise were in general terms. For since we have observed many ways and customs in preaching the Holy Law of God in this kingdom since the time of Father Ricci, our intention was to follow those which are in conformity with that same Divine Law. 2. That the questioning was not about the teachings of Father Matteo Ricci, but about the customs and the way of publishing the Holy Law. For they used the words guiju and not daoli.[^p405-49] In fact, in the way we preach the Gospel in this kingdom, as in vestments, procedure, and preaching, etc., we all follow the practices of Father Matteo Ricci and of the other Fathers of former times who first opened up the mission in this empire of China. 3. If it should be said that guiju also includes Father Ricci’s teaching, we also said that I followed it, but only by permitting it, since many thousands of Christians in this diocese of mine and in many provinces of this Chinese empire who have been signed by me with the Sacrament of Confirmation have been brought in to the sheepfold of the Lord by missionaries of the Society of Jesus. Likewise we have distributed to the faithful and to unbelievers alike the book of Father Matteo Ricci, the Tianzhu shiyi, on the grounds that it provides excellent proof (so I am told) of the existence of one God and of the immortality of the soul. And this was done because we had as yet no proof that they have been condemned by the Apostolic See. 4. We said that I have followed, and not that I am following now, or that I will follow in the future, both so that we could leave room for implementing any apostolic decision and also to avoid seeming to have made any promise in the said document that we would follow this practice in the future. 5. We used the words “and of the other Fathers of former times,” which include all the priests, including those of the venerable Order of St. Dominic, of St. Augustine, of St. Francis, as well as of the Society of Jesus.

Fifthly we wish to note: 1. That we were not questioned in detail, either about Shangdi and Tian or about Confucius and the cult of the ancestors, and much less did we offer any replies voluntarily. 2. And that Father Matteo Ricci did not <!-- PDF page 406 | book page 396 | manuscript page 567 --> treat of all the present controversies, but tried to show that the Chinese, under the name of Shangdi, worshipped the true God; the said Prince drew attention to this very effectively, for when on 9 March of this present year 1707, in this city of Linqing zhou, he was examining six missionaries of this province of Shandong, he said to Fr. Bernardo de la Encarnación, who was claiming that he followed the practices of Father Matteo Ricci: Father Ricci does not deal with the current controversies. But what do you yourself hold with regard to present matters? Therefore it remains quite clear that by consenting to the practices referred to above, we did not mean to give approval in the present controversies in favour of the Jesuits, nor did we wish to condemn the opposite opinion.

### 32. In what sense did he promise that he would remain in China for the rest of his life

Sixthly we said that for this same purpose I will remain in this kingdom for the whole of my life. And here we make the point: 1. That this is said in the same sense as in used in the Council of Trent, Session 23, Chapter 1, on Reforms.[^p406-50] 2. That was the intention we had when we spoke, namely that it was on the supposition that superiors did not decide otherwise. For when someone who has subordinated his will to the will of another who is his superior promises something, he promises it on the condition that it can be fulfilled in so far as it depends on himself. Likewise by what we said, we did not incur any obligation, firstly because we did that under orders from the gentile Emperor, and secondly because the formula we used in the document has the form neither | of an oath nor of a promise, just as we had no such intention. It merely contains an expression of what we were thinking, namely that we were willing to remain in China for the whole of our life for the purpose of preaching the Holy Law. After all, what we said was that we wanted to stay in China for the whole of our lives in accordance with the direction and the encouragement of His Holiness Innocent XI of happy memory. For when we were at his feet to kiss them, he asked us for how long we wished to serve God in this Chinese mission. We answered him that in accordance with the orders we were given, we would serve God there for ten years. And in response to this the Supreme Pontiff answered: “No, my sons! Not for ten years only, but dedicate the whole of your lives to the service of God in that mission.”

Last of all we make this point: that in the past we did not have any misgivings, nor at the present time are we in any way troubled as though by what we did on that occasion and in composing the document we had failed in any way, for as <!-- PDF page 407 | book page 397 --> we have said: 1. For the reasons already mentioned; 2. because although questions had been put in a general sense, we had made an explicit profession of faith in God when we said that we would remain in Linqing both to help those working for the Holy Law and to preach the same either personally or through others. And we confirmed this same when we said that for the same purpose we were willing to remain in this kingdom for the whole of or lives. But even though that is the way it appears to us, and we are conscious[^p407-51] of no blame in the actions described and in what we wrote, nevertheless “we are not justified in this,” and so we submit both what we wrote and ourselves to the judgment and the correction of Holy Mother Church.

Given at Linqing zhou. 25 April A.D.1707,

by us, Friar Bernardino della Chiesa, Bishop of Beijing, with my own hand.

I testify that this is in accordance with an authentic copy.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.

### 33. A few matters to be observed in this statement made by the Bishop of Beijing

1. That the nature of the explanation is such that it can be concluded that no plea for pardon and no apology from the Lord Bishop had been demanded by the Lord Patriarch, but that it was provided only on account of the complaints of others. However, what actually happened is something of which we have no definite knowledge. Nevertheless, the Illustrious Bishop himself, not just once and both orally and in writing, indicated that he had been put under pressure by the Lord Patriarch to clear his name on account of the permit he had requested and received in the form previously described. And in the month of December 1707 he repeated the same in Linqing to Fr. Franchi, who was passing through that place on the occasion of his performing mission duties there. Regarding this, he wrote to Father Kilian on 14 January 1708 in these words: The Lord Bishop says that the Lord Patriarch has written him a very strong letter with threats, to the effect that he will be treated even more severely, and forced to rescind what he had done with respect to the request for and the acceptance of the Imperial permit.

<!-- PDF page 408 | book page 398 | manuscript page 568 -->

2. It should be noted that this account in itself is something personal only to the Most Excellent Bishop himself, but was applied to themselves likewise first of all by his associate Rev. Fr. Carlo da Castorano, and then by the other Rev. Fathers of the Franciscan Order, who in association with the Lord Bishop, in the same way and manner, requested the permit at that time and were prepared to accept it on the same conditions although, as it happened, a delay occurred in granting it.

3. That the “lax theology” of the Jesuits, so disparaged by many, had no influence on this exposition; the whole force which the argument displays is to be attributed to another school which is responsible for its persuasiveness.

4. That the generosity of the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop, by which he rejected the suggested method of making his excuses, is praiseworthy; he freely proclaimed that he was not influenced by the urging of others (namely the Jesuits) or by any requests made to him, and that everything he had done in this area had been done for a higher motive and for the salvation of souls. And hence, the presumption of the Lord Patriarch, who writing from Nanjing to Doctor Borghese in Beijing claimed that the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop had fallen into the Jesuits’ snares, is falsified.

5. It is extremely difficult to unravel the reasoning which led His Illustriousness to explain the suppression of the indications of his episcopal character; namely lest as ordinary of this episcopate he might be compelled to write something | to the Supreme Pontiff about the Chinese controversies and against the person of the Lord Patriarch. For if he knew things that should be written, how was it that his office of ordinary and the safety of his imperilled flock did not bind him to write? And if he did not have anything to write, by whom could he be forced to write, since he was at liberty either to request or not request the permit, and to leave the country of his own free will? Moreover, if he had given a promise to a gentile prince, he considered that the promise was void, according to what he said elsewhere in his explanation. Therefore he must have considered that the reason there presented perhaps had its own weight because his explanations were ad hominem.

6. In the fourth paragraph of the explanation, under number 2, it is said that the interrogation did not relate to the teaching of Father Matteo Ricci, but to his practices, on account of the words guiju and not daoli. Nevertheless in the account of what was done in Linqing on March 9, it is expressly mentioned in question 17 that it was asked by using the words daoli.[^p408-52] These facts can perhaps be reconciled if the distinction is made between different times and places;

<!-- PDF page 409 | book page 399 -->

nevertheless since the 17th question was asked on March 9, and the account was given on April 25, the two should have been collated, so that the arguments should not appear to be founded on sand.

7. That which the Illustrious Lord proposes as a logical consequence at the end of paragraph 5 is altogether illegitimate, unless perhaps the Most Illustrious Lord is there responding to some imputation of wrongdoing made against him, which he considered to be more prudent at that time to suppress. For what weight could this conclusion have as self-defence: therefore it remains quite clear that by consenting to the practices referred to above, we did not mean to give approval in the present controversies in favour of the Jesuits? Rather the correct inference to be drawn from this is that it remains clear that a charge of wrongdoing was made against the Lord Bishop, on the grounds that from what he said, he wished to approve of statements made in favour of the Society of Jesus.

### 34. The mistake that many make with respect to the Chinese case

Alas for the unhappy mistake that so many are making, those who look upon this case as if it were a dispute between the Jesuits and the Bishop of Conon and his associates, and not as a case involving the salvation or damnation, or the conversion or the abandonment, of an infinite number of souls! And if in fact it were nothing more than a scholastic debate between two parties, one which did not imperil the salvation of souls, like the old conflict between the Seraphic School and that of the Society opposing those who call themselves Thomists regarding the Immaculate Conception of the Blessed Virgin, justice would require that wrongdoing was not attributed to bishops if they did not condemn the side which had the more compelling arguments before a definition of the Holy See rendered the arguments valueless. The reader will find many other matters to reflect on.

The second section for April contains the records for the remainder of the Emperor’s journey, and begins where the appendix for March ends. However fewer documents were available to me than the curiosity of the readers may expect. The sole reason is this: that Frs. Joachim Bouvet and Pierre Jartoux diligently kept records of every single thing that they themselves witnessed and which they played a part in, and these, when lack of time prevented them from being copied out, were sent in the original to Europe through Father Antonio Provana, our procurator. From there it will be only after a number of years that any copy of them will be able to be brought back to China. And if I were to await its return, the information which I do have would perhaps perish. And likewise some accident might deprive us of those commentaries for which we waited so long, which would be a great loss both to history and to appropriate supplementing <!-- PDF page 410 | book page 400 | manuscript page 569 --> of our archives. And this same reason applies for May and for part of the following June. Therefore I shall write from the material I have gathered so far, and the said Fathers Bouvet and Jartoux, who are still alive and present, will guarantee its accuracy. I am submitting these things for their appraisal, and I shall include either the originals or authentic transcripts of the letters of other people, | which I am going to provide with complete sincerity and truth.

First of all, from Father Jartoux I received the following:

On April 1st, Father Parrenin, on returning from Zhenjiang, brought the Chinese names of five Jesuit Fathers who were gathered there, namely José Monteiro, José Pereira, Antonio Ferreira,[^p410-53] Manoel da Mata,[^p410-54] and Manoel de Sousa. After he had received them, the Prince went up to the Emperor, on the same occasion bearing news of the death of Father Superior Jean-François Gerbillon, which had occurred on March 25th. He had already learned of this from a letter from his third son, who remained in charge at home. The Prince suggested to his father that it seemed appropriate that one of the Europeans from those present in the group should be sent back to Beijing to arrange the funeral, and for this duty he himself nominated Father Parrenin, and the Emperor agreed.

### 35. Five Portuguese Jesuits take the oath to observe the decree of the Lord Patriarch in the presence of the Prince

April 2nd. The five Portuguese Jesuits who were mentioned before had taken up residence in Baotawan[^p410-55] on the previous day, having been summoned from Zhenjiang by Father Parrenin on the Emperor’s orders. But Father Parrenin himself, at the very moment when they were being brought in to the Prince, was obliged to mount a horse so that according to what had been determined yesterday, he could as quickly as possible ride back to Beijing.

When the five Jesuits had performed the customary rites of greeting, he asked them if they would testify in writing that they would follow Li Madou or Father Matteo Ricci. They said they could not do that at this time. Why, asked the Prince? The Jesuits answered it was because the Lord Patriarch, on behalf of the Pope, had forbidden it. The Prince was astonished, and began to ask them, one by one, about the word Tian, the cult of Confucius, and that of the ancestors <!-- PDF page 411 | book page 401 --> and the tablets. The Fathers answered concisely according to the prescription of the decree without adding or omitting anything. In addition, they declared that things of this kind had been allowed by them before, within certain limits, but in the future could not be permitted. Against this, the Prince cited the Emperor’s orders and explained the situation regarding the Chinese rites so often laid down by the Emperor, and held up before their eyes the present peril of our holy religion in China, gravely warning them to beware of provoking the anger of His Majesty with the resulting destruction of all the Europeans and of the whole mission. In this way, the discussion between them went on for four hours and more, with the Prince trying with extremely patient insistence to change the minds of the said five Fathers, which were unchangeable in their obedience to the Patriarch’s regulation. He wanted to avoid being forced to communicate this to his father, for he knew for certain he would be displeased.

I consider that there are many things which must be omitted at this juncture, questions which were put in the course of this verbal contention to the Fathers regarding the person of the Lord Patriarch, what he did at Nanjing, his illness, his delay in leaving, the house at Nanjing, his departure, his associates, and his other pretensions. In answer to all of these, with the assistance of the angels, they answered consistently in such a way that they did not in any way compromise His Excellency nor stir up the anger of the Prince. But this finally erupted when he heard that an order had been issued by the Lord Patriarch to the effect that when the missionaries were in the process of receiving a piao in the presence of the Emperor himself, a declaration had to be made that it was subject to due obedience to the Supreme Pontiff and to superiors.

Regarding the controverted points, the Prince asked these questions: Why now do they agree with the Lord Patriarch, when up till now they have held the contrary opinion, and when they knew previously in what way his opinion was slanted since everywhere he spoke freely against the rites? They answered that then he was expressing his personal opinion, which no one was bound to hold, especially when the Chinese facts had been made clear by the explanations given by the Emperor; but now after he had begun to act with the power of the Supreme Pontiff and had issued a decree in his name, they were not permitted to hold an opinion contrary to the decree, or to speak against it. The Prince asked: “Did he provide the Supreme Pontiff’s decree for your inspection?” The five Fathers answered that he had not provided it, adding the reason given that he did not have the right to meet their request to see it.[^p411-56] Moreover, an order <!-- PDF page 412 | book page 402 | manuscript page 570 --> had been issued by the Lord Patriarch that they were to give the answer that he had certain knowledge of the decree | even though he refrained from producing it. Because they wanted to moderate this reply, they added that perhaps others, whose concern it was, had requested to see it. But, said the Prince, can it be presumed that there is a decree from the Pope? Did not he himself tell my father that nothing as yet had been decided by the Supreme Pontiff? Has any missionary recently arrived who might have brought the decree? Father Monteiro answered that a European ship had come, and that it had brought letters for the Lord Patriarch, among which there could have been the decree of His Holiness.

When he heard this, the Prince asked Father Bouvet whether, at the time he was staying in Guangzhou, he had heard anything of this. The Father confirmed the fact that an English ship had put into port at the time and had brought letters for the Lord Patriarch, and a rumour had gone around that a pontifical decree had been delivered. The Prince in a burst of anger said: “And is this the way you performed your duties as my father’s ambassador in Guangzhou, not communicating a single word of this? What were you doing, not giving a thought to your responsibilities, and failing in the loyalty you owed to my father?” The Jesuit wanted to make excuses for himself on the grounds, that owing to the uncertainty of the rumours, he did not dare to write. But the Prince ignored him, and began a tirade against the Lord Patriarch. Among others things he said that his word was not to be trusted, because in Beijing he said many things and then later denied these same things, and that he had not yet proved that he was actually a legate. The answer was given by all the Fathers that they could not doubt that he was a legate because, even though they had not seen the documents authenticating his legation, nevertheless they did have certitude from what they had heard from Very Rev. Father General and from letters of their brother Jesuits which had been sent from Europe.

### 36. The Prince urges them to follow the example of the Bishop of Beijing

The Prince restrained his anger somewhat, but began to strongly urge the five Jesuits to follow the example of the Lord Bishop of Beijing, and to request and accept the permit. But when they persisted unshakeably in strictly keeping to the Patriarch’s decree, he wanted Fathers Bouvet and Jartoux, who were present, together with those who were living in Beijing to stand surety for <!-- PDF page 413 | book page 403 | manuscript page 571 --> these five and to interpret their mind in such a way that the Emperor would not justly complain that he had been offended. Each of them answered that the decree of the Lord Patriarch was still unknown in the palace at the time when they left there with His Majesty, but in the present circumstances they would not possibly dare to go guarantor for these five in respect of the said decree, although they had no doubt that they were well intentioned. In reaction to this answer, the Prince allowed himself to become agitated, with indignation recalling the declaration ordered by the Lord Patriarch, and while uttering complaints, declared loudly that he had ordered it so that he, the Lord Patriarch, could exert his authority in the face of the Emperor. He said the Supreme Pontiff was acting as though he had complete authority and could do as he pleased, that he could order any Europeans to enter China and then return, and at whatever time he wished, that is to say after they had sufficiently examined the weaknesses of the land and were able to give an account of the condition of the roads, of the mouths of the rivers, and of the fortification of the areas. But what he complained about more bitterly than anything else was that, under the strictest orders, this declaration was to be thrown by the Europeans into his face and that of the Emperor.

### 37. While he is in this state of anger and rage, Fathers Bouvet and Jartoux try to placate him

Burning with such anger and suspicion that he was shaking, he asked first Fr. Jartoux then Fr. Bouvet what they thought with regard to the four controverted points. Fr. Jartoux, refraining from inflaming the Prince’s burning anger to greater violence, said that he was under a special obligation to serve His Majesty and from this he could not withdraw of his own free will. He said that the Christian religion taught that a monarch as mighty as he was should be obeyed, for he is one who does not give orders for the Christian Law to be violated. He said nevertheless that if the Supreme Pontiff forbade him to preach the law of God, the result would be that he would live on simply as a Religious, attending to his Religious duties and to the service of His Majesty, at least to render thanks for the many and magnificent favours granted by the Emperor both to him and to all the European missionaries. He said that this was his mind, and that Fr. Parrenin who had left a few hours previously | to arrange for the funeral of Father Jean-François Gerbillon at Beijing seemed to be of a similar opinion.

### 38. The Portuguese Jesuits stand by their decision, and say that they cannot with a good conscience accept the royal permit

Fr. Bouvet, who wanted to give a more detailed response, gave an answer which was less to the taste of the Prince. He vehemently inveighed against the Father and upbraided him on the grounds that it was as if, misguided in his ideas, he was agreeing with the Bishop of Conon. Then confronting the five Fathers again, he ordered them to accept the permit and to follow the old practices. Resolutely, the Jesuits asked to be excused, saying that this was impossible because the Lord Patriarch had declared that if they acted in this way they would be offending God and would be cast into hell. (This was the way they tried to explain in Chinese the meaning of indefinite excommunication; for there were no suitable words in the idiom of this empire to explain such matters nor could a gentile prince find it easy to understand what Europeans meant by excommunication).

To moderate the Prince’s anger, Fr. Jartoux suggested that the five Fathers deserved some sympathy because they were inexperienced in the ways and customs of the court and today, all of a sudden, they found themselves between a rock and a hard place, between two mighty lords, the Supreme Pontiff and the Emperor, each of whom was forcefully pressing his own rights by demanding contrary things from the same people. He said the last thing they wanted was to offend His Majesty, and they did not dare to oppose His Holiness, and that they remained uncertain as to what they should decide. They were all the more perplexed because in these unhappy and uncertain circumstances they were the first up, without any example to follow, without advice, and without the help of others. If the Prince in his kindness were able to turn a blind eye, let him not think it too much to wait for some time until some other missionaries arrived. The Prince would not grant this request, and when the five Fathers had answered several times that they could not accept the royal permit, unless at the same time they had the right to hold and teach in accordance with the Patriarch’s decree against the Chinese Rites, the Prince proposed a new plan: that they should remain quietly in their own churches, in the meantime refraining from putting the decree into effect until there was an answer from the Supreme Pontiff, which people were waiting for Fathers Barros and Beauvollier to bring. He put pressure on for the adoption of this plan, now on all of them together, now on each separately, advising, persuading, and trying to frighten them using every possible and increasingly strong means. He also declared that not one of them who left China, whether voluntarily or expelled by imperial mandate, would be allowed into the empire again, even if it happened that the whole business was smoothed out ex officio[^p414-57] by the Supreme Pontiff in Rome.

<!-- PDF page 415 | book page 405 | manuscript page 572 -->

Father Vice-Provincial José Monteiro resolutely rejected the condition mentioned, and without hesitation, Frs. Sousa and da Mata followed his example, saying that given that if by accepting such a condition they were able to remain in their churches, they would nevertheless be obliged to think and teach against the Chinese rites if, when he learned what they were doing, the Lord Patriarch so ordered. And therefore it seemed better to leave the empire now, to avoid being involved in the impossible situation either of offending His Majesty or of violating a promise made under oath.

Frs. Ferreira and Pereira thought that they could see in the Prince’s plan some form of remedy which would be permissible without offending God, the Supreme Pontiff, or the Lord Patriarch. Hence they spoke to this effect, so that it might be clearly understood that they considered a practical solution had been offered which would save the whole mission from being abandoned by everybody, thus leaving no room for His Holiness to restore things after the case had been well understood and judged. They argued on until evening and the Prince, when the day was far spent, dismissed the Fathers, giving strict orders that each individual with his own hand was to write what his decisions were, to be presented tomorrow to His Majesty.

April 3rd. The Prince summoned Fr. Jartoux, and when he found that the five Jesuits still maintained the position which they had adopted on the previous evening, he first commissioned the Emperor’s chamberlain, a eunuch named Li Yu, | to communicate what had happened to His Majesty as he saw the matter, not exactly as it had actually happened. For he took it on himself that the Emperor should be told that Fr. Monteiro and two others gave answers in such a way that it was evident that they agreed with the Bishop of Conon, and that therefore equally with him they deserved to be exiled, but that the other two had spoken more reasonably, and as it seemed should be allowed to remain in China. He said that Fr. Bouvet did not speak his mind clearly, was on the side of the three former, and in the depth of his heart approved of the opinion of the Bishop of Conon. This report involved danger. To prevent it reaching the ears of the Emperor, three times Fr. Jartoux went down on his knees before the eunuch making this request: Would he tell the Emperor that none of these Jesuits agreed with the Bishop of Conon, nor do they speak their minds with freedom, but are rather under constraint on account of the Lord Patriarch’s decree? They have to abide by it although they are in a dilemma, being unwilling to offend His Majesty yet being unable to act in any other way because they are acting under another’s will and power.

### 39. The Emperor’s commands are communicated to the Jesuits by the Prince

Soon after this, the Prince made the following announcement to the seven Jesuits mentioned so frequently above. 1. That his father had made a decision, and that those who wish to wait for the response from Rome, to be brought by Frs. Barros and Beauvollier, are permitted to remain in China on condition that in the meantime they take no action against the rites, while those who reject this condition are to be sent into exile as soon as possible and, once exiled, are never to be admitted again, whichever way the Supreme Pontiff decides. 2. That the Chinese who follow the opinion of the Bishop of Conon are to be considered rebels, and will pay the penalty for treason.

After this, he asked which alternative the five were choosing. These, finding themselves in a difficult position, thought of something new, and requested that permission should be obtained on their behalf from the Emperor through the Prince that all five together should be ordered to wait not in their own churches but in some other church, on the condition already mentioned several times. The Prince, drawing a parallel with what they had admitted on the previous day, objected: “In that church, if the Lord Patriarch orders you to preach against our rites, what will you do?” They replied that they would not be bound by such an order, since they had not accepted the condition voluntarily, but were waiting there, bound by an imperial command. The Prince asked for this in writing, accepted it, and repeated the same instructions he had given before to the eunuch who was the chamberlain, speaking to him in Manchu. Father Bouvet heard him speaking to this effect and arranging the exile of the three Jesuits, and immediately, whatever peril it involved for himself, decided to save the Jesuits. Prostrate on the ground, he explained to the eunuch that the Jesuits simply did not agree with the Bishop of Conon. They could not avoid giving answers in accordance with the decree of the Lord Patriarch and they were unable to make any decision according to their own choice, but were bound to follow what was determined by a higher will; within the constraints of the present circumstances, they could not act in any other way. He added some things in his own defence. The Prince listened to all of this in a very hostile fashion, being extremely offended by Fr. Bouvet because he was not afraid to form ideas contrary to his own, even to his face.

### 40. The Portuguese Jesuits are ordered to go into exile and to be deported to Guangzhou together with Father Bouvet

After this information had been conveyed to the ears of His Majesty, the eunuch returned to announce this order from the Emperor: His Majesty orders those five Europeans to depart to Guangzhou, and there to await the return of Frs. Barros and Beauvollier. “You likewise, Bo Jin (Fr. Bouvet), are to go into exile to the same place <!-- PDF page 417 | book page 407 | manuscript page 573 --> with them.” The five Jesuits humbly accepted the sentence passed against them, and Fr. Bouvet, taking into consideration the rigour of the Emperor’s displeasure and the unrelenting hostility of the Prince who was present, refrained from making an appeal on his own behalf, submitting to the penalty imposed on him and saying that he would with respect obey this order. Fr. Jartoux strongly urged on this Father that such obedience would not please the Emperor, that is, if he were to go into exile without any admission of wrongdoing. Let him first ask for pardon, and if he were not listened to, let him depart peacefully with | the others, and let him recall from past experience that the Emperor seems to be offended if he sees those whom he has perhaps driven away from his face departing as if it meant nothing to them.

### 41. The Emperor grants Father Bouvet permission to remain

Shortly afterwards, both Fathers on bended knee requested the eunuch to present their petition to the Emperor and to beg for mercy. In reply to this the eunuch finally brought back the answer. The Fathers were granted fifteen days in which to take care of their affairs. And Father Bouvet was granted the favour of being allowed to remain where he was before.[^p417-58]

The Emperor, in these unexpected circumstances in which he experienced the Lord Patriarch resisting him in the person of all the missionaries, was roused to a state of considerable anger, the Prince adding fuel to it, as was mentioned above.

### 42. The Emperor’s moderation. He restrains his son who is furious with the Fathers

The Emperor, mindful of his long-standing and well known moderation, as best he could restrained his own rising anger and, to avoid going too far, he would not allow the Prince to speak to him of these matters. It was also because he perceived that his son was too worked up either to correctly understand what the Europeans had answered or to remember it accurately. And so, without the Prince knowing, and without the knowledge of the Fathers who were involved, he took a brush and wrote to the Palace, to the mandarin Hescken, an account of what had happened there so that he could give it to the Beijing Fathers to read, and so that he himself could receive in writing an idea of what their feelings were, as an indication of whether they were more inclined to leave China or to accept the condition offered. With regard to this letter, I shall give more under April 10, which was the day when it arrived in Beijing. But now let us return to the due order of these records.

April 4th. In the morning, all the Fathers rendered thanks, | according to the custom of the country, for the clemency which mitigated the earlier sentence. When the Prince looked upon the five who were about to go into exile, as if by a gratuitous act he could make amends for the wrongs of yesterday, he said: “Earlier we received some insignificant gifts from these men; they are poor men and are about to begin a long journey, and they should be helped in some way.” He immediately ordered 50 ounces of silver to be brought to help them on their journey. The Fathers accepted them, and on the same night they set out towards Nanjing. Whether the Emperor knew beforehand of this act of generosity, or had agreed to it, there was no opportunity to explore in such a confused time; nevertheless it seems certain that the Prince, without the orders or consent of his father, at that time and place would not be doing anything of that nature.

April 5th. And now, through sad experience, it was becoming quite clear that the Emperor could not be influenced to act in any other way, nor was there any hope that a remedy would be provided by the Lord Patriarch, who fifteen days before had sailed from Nanjing. The wind in his sails swept away all hope of the letter which he declared to the Beijing Jesuits and his own followers that he would write, and which he had also promised in letters to Fr. da Silva and the Vice-Provincial, opening up an approach to the solution of these very grave matters.

The Fathers were distressed and the Prince enraged, and today he ordered Fr. Jartoux to go ahead to Nanjing. He was to present to the missionaries whom the Prince had learned were assembled in that city the conditions laid down by the Emperor for those who were going to receive the permit, and at the same time to announce the decree of banishment for all those who would not accept the condition that was stipulated. In addition he was to advise everyone how they were carefully to prepare their own hand-written applications in preparation for the arrival of His Majesty.

### 43. Some eleven Jesuits assembled at Nanjing

April 6th. At about midnight, or rather at the beginning of April 7th, Father Jartoux reached Nanjing and found our Fathers gathered there to the number of about eleven. Some of them, when they heard what had happened on the 3rd and 4th in the case of Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial and his four companions, were of the opinion that they should employ every possible means to obtain the royal permit. Others were choosing to follow the example and fate of Fr. Monteiro, considering it better to go off immediately to Guangzhou. But they had not stopped to think and were overlooking the fact that this could not be done just now without special permission of the Emperor, which they certainly could not promise themselves.

### 44. They discuss what should be done

In this matter, they all came to the very reasonable conclusion that, completely disregarding all bias resulting from personal feelings, they should weigh up together all the arguments proposed on both sides. And each one, as his conscience and love for God dictated, would either stand by his current opinion or embrace that which, after considering all the arguments and as if standing before the judgement seat of God, seemed the one to follow.

### 45. The arguments against the decree of the Lord Patriarch

The arguments of the first party were: 1. In this situation suddenly brought about by, or being the consequence of, the actions of the Lord Patriarch, the mission should not be completely abandoned without an express order from the Supreme Pontiff. Nor are so many of Christ’s sheep to be exposed to inevitable persecution by the gentiles, which is certain to happen by the very fact (if fortune should determine it this way) of all the missionaries being sent away to Guangzhou. 2. Since the Emperor has declared that no one of those who are banished or who shall leave spontaneously will in the future be allowed to return to China, some individuals are bound to prevent the banishment of everyone. 3. From the very act of all missionaries leaving or being banished all the churches would be deserted, therefore quite obviously the weaker converts, even the majority of the converts, would be exposed to the danger of apostasy, the dying deprived of the sacraments, and the infants and catechumens of Baptism, and an infinite number of spiritual ills are going to afflict an infinite number of souls among the Chinese. 4. If by the Emperor’s decision, all must be subject to the same conditions as the five Fathers, and in fact must live useless lives in Guangzhou, it seems more expedient that they should endure this in their own churches, both because then missionaries may be able to produce some fruit | in matters not touching on the four controverted points, and also to keep intact so many hundreds of churches together with the flock of Christ, and repel the reckless onslaughts of an angry populace by putting their bodies and their lives on the line between them and the faithful. 5. It is certain that the Lord Patriarch’s decree, however strictly it is interpreted, does not oblige with more force than the precepts of the Church, or even that precept of Our Lord Jesus Christ to go into the whole world … teaching, baptizing, etc. But this divine injunction does not apply in Turkey, where even Rome has forbidden baptizing on account of the danger. Therefore it can be concluded that the Lord Patriarch, in the case of obvious peril to the whole mission, cannot impose an obligation to teach etc. against the Chinese rites. 6. It does not seem that the Lord Patriarch is taking this action except on the supposition that there is no certainty of a very grave danger. If he had known of such, or believed there was such, he would <!-- PDF page 421 | book page 411 --> never have published such a decree. This presumption may be drawn from the following words of the decree: under pretext of some sort of great danger and notwithstanding any sort of grave danger, where otherwise it should have specified of greatest danger, and of very grave danger. 7. Because the decree itself is based on the doubtful foundation of a pontifical decision, the very existence of which is regarded as doubtful in China. For the Lord Patriarch is refusing to produce it, and moreover its existence has not been demonstrated to anyone. 8. Finally in the decree the author uses deliberate obscurity, indeterminate meaning, improbable statements, and impractical language which can have no other purpose than to annoy the Emperor without good reason, and to bring about the complete destruction of all we have built up over the years in the mission.

### 46. The reasons of those supporting observing the decree

But against these arguments the other party argued: If our holy religion is to perish in China it will not die out through the fault of individual missionaries, or of those leaving of their own free will or banished by the Emperor, but through the fault of a church authority, of one who knows and whose duty it is to know that there is the danger of destroying our religion. And our holy religion will not die out merely because this one or that departs, and so a choice may be made by this man or that to depart voluntarily. 2. They do not want to abandon China completely, but to remain in Guangzhou. And since several of those present have already taken the oath to obey the Patriarch’s decree, even though it can be said that they took the oath hurriedly and without due consideration, nevertheless they are now bound to observe the decree.[^p421-59] 4. And even if, in spite of the oath to observe the decree etc. with a good conscience, they promise to abide by the condition prescribed by the Emperor, yet it is rightly to be feared that the Lord Patriarch would annul such a promise and enforce the execution of his decree with censures. 5. Since the Lord Patriarch so emphatically declares that the Church of God and the Supreme Pontiff have condemned the rites, it should not be presumed that at Rome deference will be paid to the information sent by the Emperor, and even less that deference will be paid to the representations of the missionaries, whether against the substance or against the form of the Patriarch’s decree. And so it is absolutely certain that sooner or later the mission will have to be abandoned, since it cannot be saved while the rites are prohibited. Hence it seems less burdensome to leave voluntarily than to be compelled to depart at another time by censures from Rome, or to leave by command of the Emperor. 6. To save the Chinese, our own consciences are not to be put at <!-- PDF page 422 | book page 412 | manuscript page 576 --> risk, and it can scarcely be reasonable to say that we, by the law of charity for the saving of the mission, are bound to endure all those trials of body, soul, and reputation which the Lord Patriarch is going to inflict whether on the missionaries who are willing to remain voluntarily on such conditions, or even on the whole Society. In addition it is to be feared that his notorious and extreme resentment is going to attempt to wrest the mission from us or us from the mission. 7. The example of the Vice-Provincial can serve as a reason for some, and for others it can rightly be supposed that this Jesuit who has debated with the Lord Patriarch, consulted the Beijing Jesuits by letter, and has risked confronting the Prince face to face | has rightly come to know what can be done and what is to be avoided.

### 47. Father José Soares is nominated Vice-Provincial by his predecessor

April 7th. On this day, Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial disembarked at Nanjing. This is proved by a letter of his which was dated there on board the ship, one in which he sent to Father Antoine Thomas the authorization for Father José Soares to succeed him as Vice-Provincial.[^p422-60] However I cannot find anywhere that he did anything to persuade or dissuade the group of Jesuits who were deliberating, even though he had come to know the arguments each party was using. He indicates this in that same letter which has been mentioned, and he prays for the grace of the Holy Spirit for them, to make the right choice.

Fr. Jartoux, while not completely in favour of seeking a permit,[^p422-61] nevertheless strongly urged everyone to consider before God what the limits of obedience were if they felt there was clearly danger of sin, so that they should look not so much to themselves and to avoiding any inconvenience to themselves as to the serving of the flock which, for Christ’s sake, they were bound to assist irrespective of what people might say etc. It would be unconscionable to think that the Lord Patriarch knowingly and willingly intended to obstruct the salvation of so many myriads of souls and of the whole of China. Even if His Excellency had reasons which he thought sufficient for making such dangerous moves against those remaining in China under the above-mentioned conditions, God would have sufficient reason for punishing those who abandoned their flock, whose salvation was in certain peril, through fear of some doubtful penalty.

The Fathers were taken from the hall and were ordered to remain on call for the rest of the day without any dinner, so that if the Prince called them, they would not be found absent. However he did not give them the opportunity either to ask for or to say anything because it was now late in the night, and he was suddenly called to His Majesty.

April 10th. The eleven aforementioned Fathers came again to the Palace, postponing their journey for several hours. This is what happened, as Father Bouvet wrote down from the Palace records during the journey:[^p427-71]

On the 7th day of the 3rd Moon (the ninth of April), the Prince and Zhang Changzhu both using notes held in their hand gave the Emperor an account of what concerned the eleven Europeans. They said that Bayard, d’ Entrecolles, de Prémare, Testard, Goville, Noëlas, Chavagnac, Hervieu, de Mailla, Duarte, and da Silva of the Society of Jesus—who arrived in China many years ago; who now reside in the provinces of Huguang, Jiangxi, and Jiangnan; and who do not have the intention of returning to Europe—have studied philosophy and theology from their youth and while here have always followed the practices of Matteo Ricci, opposing the teaching of Yan Dang, the Bishop of Conon. They now humbly petition the Emperor to permit them to remain in China while they await the return of Fathers Barros and Beauvollier, and the command of the Supreme Pontiff. But if their superiors recall them to Europe | they will not depart without the Emperor’s permission. But if they are banished by the Emperor, they will respect the order and will depart, etc.

### 55. The questioning of Fathers di S. Giorgio and Hervé

On this same day, April 10th, the Revs. Hervé and San Giorgio were summoned and questioned by the Prince and the mandarin Zhang Changzhu, firstly as to whether they followed the practices of Father Matteo Ricci. Fr. di San Giorgio replied that as yet he had not learned Chinese letters and had not read the books of Fr. Matteo Ricci, but that he did not follow the teachings of a dead man. Father Hervé explained himself in this way: “I agree with Father Ricci insofar as he says and approves that God or the Lord of Heaven is to be worshipped. Father San Giorgio said he could not accept this. Father Hervé hesitated for some time and said something in French from which it appeared that he was in a state of doubt, that he was continually changing his mind, at one moment accepting the condition, and the next moment refusing it. But since he gave no definite answer in the Chinese language, the Prince went away with the mandarin to give a report to His Majesty. ### 56. The Emperor is offended by their responses, and passes a heavy sentence against them

On the 8th day of the 3rd month (April 10th) the Zhi junwang (that is the title of the Prince)[^p429-73] and the mandarin Zhang Changzhu made a report to the Emperor about matters pertaining to the European He Xuan[^p429-74] (which is the Chinese name of Father Hervé), who lives in the city of Qizhou,[^p429-75] and to the European Shi Tiren[^p429-76] (the Chinese name of the Abbate di San Giorgio), who lives in the city of Jiangning.[^p429-77] They reported that on questioning Shi Tiren they discovered that he had been in the company of Duoluo (the Lord Patriarch), that he had come to China only recently, and that he had not yet learned Chinese letters and did not know whether or not it was right to follow the practices of Li Madou (Father Ricci). Under orders, Fathers Bouvet and Jartoux were also present with Brother Paramino, and all stood by right up to the end for the promulgation of the royal decree. The content of the royal decree is as follows:

From the time that Li Madou entered China, that is for more than two hundred years, because no obstacle had been raised against Chinese teaching permission was granted to Europeans to live in China, according to their Religious practice. But now, what you are doing is not in accordance with the custom of Father Ricci and it is contrary to Chinese teaching. You do not honour Confucius, and we absolutely refuse to allow this way of acting.

### 57. The death penalty is decreed for those who in the future oppose the Chinese rites

If in the future, there are still found Europeans who say inappropriate things like that, however many there may be, they will be put to death. Besides, | before the arrival of Father Ricci in China, for some thousands of years, there was no European preaching the Law, yet the empire was not lacking in anything. Taking into consideration that you came from afar to this vast empire of the Chinese, and that you placed no impediment to Chinese teachings, we granted you permission to preach the Law. Now you are maintaining that Confucius must not be honoured by the Chinese who follow your European Law. It seems right that men of this sort should not be tolerated in China. Moreover in Japan, which is a small kingdom, any European who comes is arrested immediately on arrival and put to death; I don’t know how many have been killed in this way. You Europeans have been much alarmed by this, and up till now none of you have dared to approach Japan. In the future, if there are found any amongst you Europeans who in preaching your own law oppose Chinese teachings, they will be arrested and put to death. You (Fathers Hervé and San Giorgio) should hurry after Duoluo and make sure he understands this. Suggest to him that he should stop trying to inflame the crowds. If henceforth anything like this happens, Duoluo will be arrested, brought here in custody, and put to death. If as the result of the execution of Duoluo you will not permit Europeans to preach the law in China, and as a result no European comes to China, so much the better. The Europeans who are now in China, even if they do not preach the Law, have permission to live and practise their religion in their own churches. Also, if you Europeans were to come with soldiers, will not our own soldiers fight against them? If your army were able to travel nine thousand leagues in order to get here, will our soldiers not equally be able to do so in the other direction? You are granted five days before your departure. When, as you follow Duoluo, you reach Guangzhou, you must immediately leave that city.[^p430-80] If there is any <!-- PDF page 431 | book page 421 --> delay beyond that deadline, the zongdu and fuyuan have orders to arrest you and to take you in chains to Macau, a city of the province of Guangdong.

### 58. Fathers di San Giorgio and Hervé are expelled from the Chinese Empire

The implementation of the before-mentioned imperial decree both before and after publication was assigned to the zongdu and the fuyuan. There they were handed over to two officials belonging to the prefects, and by them, by various byways, either by mistake or by design, they were eventually brought on foot to the College of our Society. He set a table for them and after they had been refreshed with fruit and the other usual Lenten fare, they each climbed into a sedan chair and returned honourably and peacefully to their own church accompanied by some Christians and our servants.

### 59. The Emperor leaves Nanjing

April 13th. The Emperor moved out of Nanjing. ### 62. Rumour and suspicion that Father Gerbillon had been poisoned

Naturally of course, when we were already distressed by the death of the said Father, suspicion like that on the part of others added to our distress. Bo Jin will perhaps write of these things, and if he should write, his letter is to be considered worthless.

### 67. Certain things which should be observed in the Emperor’s letter

This response of ours and the aforementioned letter from the Emperor to the mandarin Hescken require a few annotations lest the reader should be tripped up on them, just as, of their own free will, some have already stumbled driven by either good or bad intentions.

1. It is clearly a matter for wonder that the Emperor, who now more than a year ago had decided to transact all business with Europeans only through his eldest son and in fact did this, nevertheless should without his knowledge write the letter in question with his own hand, and that he alone would receive the answer to it, without the intervention of his son. And from this we concluded that he was acting with extreme moderation, to ensure that the immature impetuosity of his son did not rouse his anger immoderately while the truth was concealed from his father.

2. The Emperor writes that these events took place in Yangzhou. Father Jartoux relates that they happened in Baotawan and the twenty-four Fathers in their appeal allege that it took place in the village of Sanchahe, but in this instance the difference of the three names does not detract from the truth. For the Emperor was travelling by boat, and his entourage, which was at anchor, (as anyone with experience would know) could easily have been extended over one French league. Just as happens with naval anchorages, they may be named now from this, now from that adjacent place, village, or city. The Emperor wrote as from the city of Yangzhou as being the more important place, and worthy of His Majesty, although in fact the Emperor was not at that time in the said city, but nearby. Fr. Jartoux adopted the name of the land as he learned it from the sailors, and the twenty-four Jesuits wrote down the name of the village where

<!-- PDF page 439 | book page 429 | manuscript page 592 -->

Fr. Monteiro with his companions had taken up their positions for the time being, near the royal ships.

3. We readily concede that the Emperor’s letter does not report accurately the replies given by Fr. Vice-Provincial and his associates, but records them as he had heard them from a eunuch, and as the latter heard them from the Prince.

4. The Emperor writes as if he himself did not send the five Jesuits into exile. In a way, he could write this, because he gave them the option, if they so preferred, of remaining in their own churches or houses; then, when he was writing to discover the attitude of the Beijing Jesuits, he did not want to declare bluntly that it was he himself who sent these Fathers into the said exile. Actually, he did not send them, except insofar as they themselves rejected the condition which would have made it possible for them to remain.

5. We at Beijing did in fact receive a letter from Fr. Bouvet which he wrote while on his journey, but an earlier one which contained nothing of the events of this sort since they had not yet taken place.

6. This letter of the Emperor should be checked against that one which is preserved among the revised records and which was communicated to us from the Palace so that they could be sent to the Supreme Pontiff.

April 11th. ### 72. Fr. Grimaldi, under orders from the Emperor, writes to the Lord Patriarch

April 26th. There arrived from the South an order from His Majesty which was very disturbing. The Europeans who lived in Beijing, and in the first place Fr. Grimaldi, were to write, not in the name of the Emperor but in their own name, to the Lord Patriarch as follows:

Upon your first arrival, you told the Emperor that you had come here for no other purpose except to render thanks. But now you do nothing else than endlessly stir up matters which concern only a few of us here in China, and are not the business at all of the many people who live outside China. You should think of the kindness with which so great a monarch has in the past treated foreigners, and you should reflect whether and why in the future you should heed the words of Yan Dang (the Bishop of Conon) and people like him, when all they do here is to stir up trouble and strife. If the Emperor becomes even slightly <!-- PDF page 445 | book page 435 | manuscript page 595 --> angry, he will drive all of us out of China and if afterwards you have a change of heart, your change of heart will be too late. If you take our advice, you will leave the country as soon as possible.

This is what the Emperor ordered them to write. But who would have the courage to write this to the Lord Patriarch? And who could disregard the order of the Emperor with impunity? Who would be believed if he maintained that this had been ordered by His Majesty in writing with all his authority? Finally with what profit, or rather loss, particularly in the case of the Jesuits? If it had been a question only of the final point, where it is suggested that the Most Illustrious Lord should be advised to leave the country, that might have been done with due respect, as if it were being done humbly and with solicitude out of desire to protect him from the rather serious trouble which the Lord Patriarch himself could see was threatening him, and from which he asked to be saved by the Beijing Jesuits in the month of February.

In fact this very advice had been humbly suggested to His Most Illustriousness by Father J.F. Gerbillon in the month of January, when the Most Illustrious Legate in a letter dated 14 January 1706 | and sent in the same parcel with that very bitter and calumnious one of January 18th, in which he attacked all the Beijing Jesuits, made a request to the same Father not to be afraid to tell him whether and in what way he should come face to face with the Emperor who was going to come to Nanjing. To this question the Jesuit replied candidly that after the banishment from China of the Bishop of Conon and his associates, after the imprisonment of Father Appiani, and after the very stern decree issued by the Emperor in the month of December, it seems by far the wiser course for His Excellency to avoid the face and ire of the Emperor, and to wait for more peaceful times outside China. The result of this advice sincerely expressed in a friendly manner was that the Most Illustrious Lord immediately published his regulation, which he was well aware would be regarded by the Emperor as a deliberate insult to His Majesty.

To what purpose therefore were we to advise him to depart? To what purpose were we to present him with other reasons? But since we had to obey the Emperor we wrote with all possible mildness, and in such a way that it was sufficiently apparent that the pen was ours but the content was another’s. And these letters which were sent from Beijing never reached the Most Illustrious Legate, because the Emperor all of a sudden made other arrangements, as will be related in May. He sent to Guangzhou two mandarins who conducted His Excellency from there to Macau to wait there until the answer from Rome arrived.

### 73. A distinguished Christian abandons the Faith

April 28th. In Beijing, most of the household of a former distinguished Christian, Ignatius De, lapsed back to the basest form of idolatry in sad disarray. The said Ignatius De was a man who was distinguished among the Manchus, a nobleman with a wife from the imperial family of Tai Ming,[^p446-94] a dedicated army officer, and one who practised our holy faith with distinction, a man who survived so often the attacks of his relatives and the threats and the open denunciations made to the Manchu Prince.[^p446-95] He was a successful general and always gave witness to our holy religion. He died more than two years ago in the province of Liaodong, leaving a widow and fifteen-year old son. His body was brought to a place near the Palace, and with the consent of his wife was buried by his relatives according to the gentile rites, with his young son continually rejecting the idolatrous rites to the extent that he had to be held by the arms by the head of the family, who was a gentile, and be forced to the ground while the other bowed his head before the idol. The son boldly declared that the inclination he had been forced to make was regarded by him not as honouring but as deriding a false god.

But now that his mother has died, he yielded to the importunities of flesh and blood and together with many of his household celebrated the rites of the bonzes publicly, even to the extent of admitting that he was doing it voluntarily, and he allowed the images of Christ and the Blessed Virgin, along with all the sacred furniture with which a chapel had been equipped by his father in his own dwelling, to be sent back to our Eastern Church. The principal weapon used by his gentile relatives to conquer the immature heart of the youth and to terrify the household was that they were saying that the Emperor was now hostile to our religion and was forbidding it, and had banished the missionaries and was regarding those of his subjects who were followers of the Holy Law as rebels. It was certainly a powerful weapon, not only against the heart of a fifteen year-old youth who could be easily swayed, but capable also of turning thousands of Chinese converts from our holy faith.

<!-- PDF page 447 | book page 437 | manuscript page 596 -->

Let people in the future consider what were the feelings of the Jesuits of Beijing when, in the sight of the whole world, we watched an illustrious household, one close to us and one which could scarcely be called an ordinary one, falling into idolatry. We were being forced to fear that following their example very many disasters of the same sort would follow in the Palace, and numberless others throughout the 15 provinces of China. We did not cease searching for remedies. The Jesuits approached the third son of the Emperor who was acting in place of his father in Beijing, and likewise the fourth,[^p447-96] to make use of his influence over the former, making serious efforts so that the freedom for the practice of the Holy Law only recently granted by the Emperor should not be permitted to be infringed. But nothing was gained, except that by the artifices commonly practised in the Chinese tribunals we were sent on from one to the other. Those who received the information were discouraged from putting on any pressure, and in solving difficulties, they entangled difficulties in difficulties, until at the end of it all the body was brought to burial with idolatrous rites. |

### 81. Reasons for the appeal

For what person, unless he were quite irresponsible, would approach the Emperor for a licence while holding those opinions on account of which the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon was recently banished from China together with his two associates? And because the bearer was present, I wrote immediately to the Lord Patriarch that it was my duty, for very grave reasons, to make an appeal against the execution of the decree, but this did not suit him because there were other matters to be referred to the Holy See, and so I accepted the decree with respect and with the intention of putting it into execution, and in fact I did begin to put it into execution. But on reflection I recognized that I could not do this without putting a very heavy burden on my conscience. For it is not lawful for me to renounce my own rights, much less the rights of so many of the faithful of this province. Therefore in accordance with the provisions of the law, I have appealed to the Apostolic See against the restrictions of the said decree, in my own name and in the name of those faithful. And I am now sending the text of the appeal by express messenger to the Lord Patriarch, trusting that in accordance with his sense of justice, his prudence, and his zeal, he will not find it too difficult to accept my appeal to make a stand against such great evils. I want Your Paternity to be advised of this, so that he will not make any request either personally or through another for permission from the Emperor for me to remain in China, for I do not want to request such a permission unless beforehand the Lord Patriarch accepts my appeal. For if he does not accept it, I shall choose the lot of all the others, and if travel expenses are available, I shall go in person to the Apostolic See to save this mission. For it is being dealt severe blows in China, but I am afraid that very soon and later on it will suffer even heavier ones.

The grounds of my appeal are these:

1. Because this decree (of the Patriarch) and what is alleged to have been done by the Holy See are grounded on a presumption and on information supposed to have been provided by competent men; consequently if the presumption is disproved and incompetence is established, they are reformable and must be annulled, just as that decree of Alexander VII of the year 1656 is <!-- PDF page 455 | book page 445 --> supposed to have been revoked.[^p455-114] And the events at Beijing in the past year, which happened in the presence of the Emperor, disprove the presumption and prove the incompetence of the informants.

2. Because the mission of Fathers Beauvollier and Barros, which has been sent by the Emperor, is a virtual appeal from the Emperor to the Holy See, and while it is pending, nothing may be changed; in fact, what has been done after that are attempts which must be revoked.

3. Because I, although I am the least of all, have a right to be heard, just as the Bishop of Conon has been heard, and my written representations sent at about the end of the year 1705 have not yet been examined in Rome.

4. Because the Emperor, even though he might not be listened to by the Holy See, should nevertheless be heeded; if the opposite has happened, he is capable of developing great hostility towards the Apostolic See. And in the event of rejection, I have appealed against that, and I have requested those who were sent to pursue the appeal to the limits that the law allows.

It is a fact, as I have said, that unless the Lord Patriarch assents, I shall not request a permit. Meanwhile I shall remain in Nan’an entrusting myself to the Providence of God. If therefore Your Paternity wishes to reply, let him do so by way of Guangzhou. This letter is to be shared with Rev. Fr. Gerbillon. I have been very wearied by this writing, and am not able to write any more.

Ganzhou fu, 3 April, 1707.

The most devoted servant of Your Very Rev. Paternity. Friar Alvaro. Bishop of Ascalon.

### 85. The formal appeal made by the Bishop of Ascalon to the Supreme Pontiff

A transcript of the formal appeal made by the Most Illustrious Lord Alvaro, Bishop of Ascalon, Vicar Apostolic in the Province of Jiangxi, which was sent to the Lord Patriarch on 13 April 1707.[^p460-128]

Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lord,

Friar Alvaro de Benavente, Bishop of Ascalon, Vicar Apostolic of this Province of Jiangxi in the Kingdom of the Chinese, in the presence of Your Most

<!-- PDF page 461 | book page 451 -->

Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship, namely Lord Charles Thomas Maillard de Tournon, Patriarch of Antioch, Commissioner and Visitor Apostolic with the faculties of a legate a latere in these parts, etc., in the way and form to which I am entitled and bound according to law I present myself and declare that, in this city of Ganzhou, on the 8th day of April of the current year, I received the decree issued by yourself in Nanjing on the 25th day of January of the same year.[^p461-129]

In this, a regulation is laid down for giving answers to the Tartaro-Chinese emperor regarding the rites, which are a matter of dispute not only before the Holy See but also before the Emperor himself. And on the pretext of a decision about these laid down by the former, the content of which does not seem to be included in the decree, it is enjoined by Your Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship on all the missionaries, under pain of excommunication latae sententiae, that when questioned they must answer that they cannot permit the Christians ji or sacrifices for Confucius and dead ancestors, nor the use of the tablets of the dead, nor furthermore say that Shangdi or Tian is the true God of the Christians, because Your Most Illustrious and Most Rev. Lordship declares that those things, as they are contained in your decree, are defined by the Holy See

Moreover these answers are directly opposed to the declarations made by the Emperor in the year 1700, and on the 2nd day of August 1706, and since it cannot be reasonably doubted from the events we have already experienced, that they will bring about the expulsion of nearly all the missionaries, and the ultimate ruin of the Christian cause, as will be established from what has to be said, and as is sufficiently known to Your Most Illustrious and Most Reverend self.

Therefore, even though on the 9th of this month I replied to you through a courier who was hurrying back in your direction that I did not choose to use the remedy of an appeal against the said decree, even though in other respects it seemed that I had the right to do so and for very grave reasons even the duty to do so; also, that I nevertheless accepted it with reverence and would put it into execution, for other reasons which I reserved for referral to the Holy See. In fact I had already begun to put it into execution by communicating it to the three missionaries who were present. This course of action was the result of my pressing and earnest desire to obey Your Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship and to be of service to you in everything pertaining to your office, and to the glory of your legation.

<!-- PDF page 462 | book page 452 | manuscript page 603 -->

But after pondering the matter for three days, and commending it to God, and after weighing up the tremendous devastation which is bearing down on us at this very moment, I simply cannot forfeit my own rights in this matter, and even if I could, it would not be right to renounce it to the very grave detriment of souls. For in this province the number of the Faithful is about 7000. It would be wrong to abandon them entirely, nor would such desertion and omission on my part on the pretext of showing due respect be able to be reasonably excused from serious culpability before God and the Apostolic See, particularly when it is a matter of supreme importance. Nor would the fact that I wrote as above to Your Most Illustrious and Most Rev. Lordship deprive me of the right of appeal, because we are still within ten days from the notice of the decree and what it obliges, and I did not renounce my rights, and much less the rights of the souls of the faithful committed to me. Furthermore, the obligations which that decree entails are not momentary, but are to continue through time as a continuous factor which will not only affect the present but the future, with effects as in Japan, which may God avert, for very many years. Wherefore in my own name and in the name of all the faithful of this province for whom I provide a voice and care for what is right and good, I appeal to the Most Holy Lord the Pope against the execution of the said decree because it involves a most serious and irreparable burden which will damage the entire Church in this kingdom.

I say emphatically that the answers prescribed to be given to the Emperor, if I may with due deference so speak, are entirely inopportune, and do not serve to promote the glory of God but only to provoke the anger of the Emperor against the Apostolic See and do harm to the Christian religion.

For, since on the one hand it must be obvious to Your Most Illustrious Lordship that your decree, and especially that part of it which is supposed to have come from the Supreme See regarding the said articles, was passed as a result of presumptions and information from men who are reputed to be experts | in the language and writing of China, it follows that if this presumption is proved false, and if their ignorance is revealed, these articles are reformable and may be revoked. And from another point of view, it is well known to your Most Excellent and Most Rev. Lordship that you yourself introduced to the Emperor the said Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lord Charles Maigrot, Bishop of Conon and Vicar Apostolic of the Province of Fujian, as being learned and an expert in Chinese literature, to provide an explanation of the said controverted rites. And you know that the Emperor received him and at the same time ordered him to present in writing an account of his opinions, and that for this reason the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon presented two documents to the Emperor to substantiate his opinions.

<!-- PDF page 463 | book page 453 -->

### 86. He gives the reasons for his advice

The truth is this, that in those documents he proved nothing, or virtually nothing, and when he was summoned by the Emperor to give the same explanation in his presence, he offered nothing that was satisfactory. And something was discovered by the Emperor which he himself openly declared to Your Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship through a decree issued on August 3rd of the year which has just passed, and which I would be ashamed to include here. And the same fact was clearly revealed a second time, when he was brought to judgment before the royal judges along with two scholars from Fujian who had previously been his tutors in Chinese letters. For, while he was present, they affirmed what I myself heard them say four years earlier, namely that they did not explain the Chinese books to the said Lord in the sense which he adopted; rather he adopted it as his own on the word of a Dominican, Father Varo. And the Illustrious Lord did not deny these charges. For these reasons he was rejected by the Emperor, and by two decrees declared ignorant both of the language and of the written word, and with even more reason, of any familiarity with the canonical books. And rightly so, since all of us Europeans who have devoted ourselves to the same study for many years are not the equals of any one of the ordinary Chinese graduates. This is something which would not be denied by any missionary who loved the truth. Therefore it is impossible to avoid giving the most serious offence to the Emperor and to the other scholars of the court when they see themselves held in low esteem and regarded as inferior to a foreigner who is also illiterate, and when they are not only not heeded by the Holy See but not even given a hearing.

Nor is it sufficient to say that the Emperor’s declaration made in the year 1700 has already been examined in Rome because, perhaps on the grounds that it was not appropriately authenticated, it was not at that time admitted in evidence.

Then the Bishop of Conon’s party attacked it with a great deal of quibbling and made it difficult to understand, alleging that the Emperor was rather saying in it that the true principal and lord of everything is the material heaven, or at least that its power is identified with matter. It is certain now that that is false, since the Emperor himself revealed his mind and gave an explanation in the decree of August 3rd which has already been mentioned. He said that the character Tian in the Jing Tian tablet is the same as Tianzhu, the Lord of Heaven and the God of the Christians, but the Holy See is unaware of this declaration.

It cannot be objected that the Emperor is ignorant of what Christians understand by the word Tianzhu because, regarding our idea of God, he has been given instructions repeatedly by the Religious of the Society of Jesus who are his assistants, and particularly, at his own request by Father Ferdinand Verbiest, <!-- PDF page 464 | book page 454 | manuscript page 604 --> and more than once he has read Father Matteo Ricci’s book, which is written in the Chinese and Manchu languages, about the true understanding of God. There the idea of God is explained very correctly and at great length. Likewise he exposes the errors of the other sects, and what is more it was twice cited by Father Beauvollier in two memorials presented to the Emperor in response to claims in other memorials presented by the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon. And after an examination of all of them, the before-mentioned imperial decree was issued. But as yet, all of this is not taken into account in Rome.

And then also, because it is well known that the Emperor, in the month of October which is just past, has sent the said Father Antoine de Beauvollier and Father Antonio de Barros of the same Society of Jesus to bring information to His Holiness our Pope, also taking with them a transcript of the process or acts, and of his declarations, under the imperial seal. This recourse has the force of an appeal against the obligations which even in his face were being imposed on the Church of China by Your Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship; while this appeal is pending, nothing should be changed, and any acts which followed should be regarded as tentative. And the opposite course cannot be pursued without serious discredit to the Holy See. And the Emperor doubtlessly will not consider that the Holy See is the perfect model of justice and consideration, but rather unjust and unyielding, and consequently his anger, which has already been aroused, will increase against the missionaries and the Christian cause to the ultimate extermination of the Divine Law in his empire.

In addition, because in the matter of these controversies I myself, although the least of all, am nevertheless a bishop and a vicar apostolic just like | the Most Excellent Bishop of Conon, and have not been heard in Rome. This was because I was unable to complete my writings about them until the year 1705; in them I set out to prove that the opinions of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon are altogether false and contrary to the real beliefs of the Chinese. Towards the end of that year, I forwarded them by two different routes to Rome and later again by means of the previously-mentioned men sent by the Emperor. For there is no reason why the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon should be listened to rather than I. I arrived in China four years before him, and during the fourteen years in which I have been here, I have laboured no less diligently than he in studying Chinese books, and even though in many and other areas I acknowledge that I am a lesser man than the said Most Illustrious Lord, in the matter in question I do not see why I should be considered second to him. This is something I say unwillingly, but it is better when the mission is in desperate straits to make some sacrifice of modesty rather than of the truth.

<!-- PDF page 465 | book page 455 -->

### 87. The Emperor’s decree regarding the banishment of the missionaries

Besides, since it has become widely known from the imperial decree of the 19th of last December that all of us missionaries who do not have a royal permit and who live in the provinces of this extremely vast empire are bound to go into exile from China, the Religious of the Society of Jesus, who are close to the Emperor, have pleaded with tears and at their own peril for a postponement of its publication throughout the provinces for the time being. They wanted an opportunity to be given to the missionaries, who are scattered throughout very far-distant places, to be able to come individually to Beijing to request the permission to remain, which none of them yet had.

They finally obtained this, although with difficulty, except in the cases of the provinces of Guangdong and Sichuan in which the decree had been already published. The Emperor added: “Write to the missionaries to tell them that if anyone refuses to come to me, for I wish to get to know and to examine everyone, let him leave China as soon as possible and certainly not wait to be driven out of the country by my officials to their own inconvenience and disgrace, and that of your Law also.”

### 88. The practices and usages of the old missionaries are contrary to the decree of the Lord Patriarch

On account of this, the Most Illustrious and Most Rev. Bernardino della Chiesa, Bishop of Beijing, even though he was more than sixty years old, immediately hurried off in the middle of winter with his associate to go to the court and request and obtain his permit, and at the same time Father Sabino Mariani also requested one. The former in writing and the latter verbally explained to me the examination which they underwent, and in fact all who requested the letters patent were required, if they wanted to receive them, to give an assurance both verbally and in writing, in the Emperor’s presence, to follow the practice of Father Ricci and the old missionaries.[^p465-130] This practice is directly opposed to the answers attached by Your Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship to the aforementioned decree. Therefore no missionary can approach the Emperor to request the permit, unless perhaps he rashly hopes he will secure them while affirming those opinions on account of <!-- PDF page 466 | book page 456 | manuscript page 605 --> which recently, by royal decree, the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Conon with Fathers Mezzafalce and Guéty were banished and exiled from China. And this was done, even though the said Father Mezzafalce protested that he was new to the mission and was unfamiliar with Chinese literature, and on account of this neither approved nor disapproved of the opinions of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon.

This being the case, it necessarily follows that unless God prevents it with his very special and miraculous providence, all the missionaries except a few who are in Beijing are to be banished from China, and consequently the propagation of the Faith ceases, the new converts who are not yet strong in the Faith are going to apostatise, the stronger will be without the sacraments, the churches and houses of the missionaries are going to be occupied by the pagans, and perhaps become temples of idols, and other numberless and irreparable evils are going to follow.

### 89. The material heavens are not worshipped by the Chinese, sacrifices are not offered to Confucius, etc

It is not sufficient to say that these and even worse disasters should be chosen rather than approving of sacrifices which are due to God alone being offered to Confucius and to dead ancestors, of the keeping of tablets of the dead according to the Chinese custom, and of the teaching that the God of the Christians is Heaven or its power, as is stated in the decree. I am saying that this is not sufficient because the Emperor, quoting Ricci and the most learned missionaries, and even the Chinese who have published books on these matters, does not assert that Tian and Shangdi are the material heavens or their power, but rather that this is not the case; nor does he say that there are sacrifices which must be offered to Confucius and the dead. In fact | he declares that the offerings which are made to them are not sacrifices, because no honour which is more than human is offered to them, nor are any petitions made to them, nor are any favours expected from them, and they are treated and regarded as human. Therefore the difficulties which are named in the said decree are suppositions rather than established facts, while on the other hand, the results that follow from its execution are clearer than the midday light and consequently must be avoided.

Likewise, because the Emperor is favourable to Europeans, and is not averse to the Christian Law, and cherishes and protects missionaries, has permitted a church to be built inside the palace walls and has himself provided a large part of the cost, does not prevent members of his own household from becoming Christians, and finally treats the Supreme Pontiff with respect, and is having recourse to him in this dispute; on account of these and all the other splendid things he has done, he deserves to be given a hearing by the Apostolic See in the <!-- PDF page 467 | book page 457 --> matters that concern his own kingdom, and his authority and majesty should not be despised by foreigners. This is in complete conformity with the instructions from the Sacred Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith which were given to the vicars apostolic and is based on the chapter of canon law: Si quando de rescriptis.[^p467-131]

For these reasons, any decree whatsoever, even one issued from the Apostolic See, should have its execution deferred until the Holy See is more fully informed, and especially in matters which notably alter the case, and which could never yet have come to the knowledge of Our Most Holy Lord. This would avoid putting greater stumbling-blocks in the way of the natives.

### 90. He answers some objections

Nor is it sufficient to say that the same problems are to be feared if after the Apostolic See has listened to the Emperor, it will not change its decision regarding the Chinese rites. For this is doubtful; rather it is more probable that those decisions will be revoked which were made as the result of a false presumption once the truth, as well as the ignorance of the informants, has been recognized. Or at least it would be somewhat modified lest this huge empire should be reduced to that state which the Church mourns in neighbouring Japan. And also because there are two entirely different ways of acting: one is to spurn the Emperor, to refuse to give him a hearing, while the other is to accept him and listen to him, and after that to instruct him in a spirit of gentleness, in a civilized way, and with kindness (which it is rightly to be hoped would be done by Our Most Holy Lord)

If explanations were suggested to him by which he might come to agree with the Holy See without hatred and aversion, he may not expel the missionaries or reject the Christian Law as irrational. For these reasons, and others that are well-known, I appeal again to Our Most Holy Lord the Pope against the execution of the said decree and its obligations, and against the excommunication imposed in it, and above all, against its publication, and against Your Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship. And urgently, and as urgently as possible, <!-- PDF page 468 | book page 458 --> and as often it may be necessary according to law, I am trying to influence my messengers to persist in this appeal before the Holy Father.

And in the event of a rejection, against the obligations inflicted on me and the faithful of this province, unjustly and a second time (and I speak with due reverence), I am making a second appeal to that same Holy Father and most insistently urge my messengers to so act.

And because Your Most Excellent and Most Reverend Lordship is not here present, I make this appeal according to the requirements of the law, in the presence of trustworthy men, namely Fathers Juan Fernández Serrano, Diego de S. Rosa, and Miguel Roca of the Order of Friars Minor in this city of Ganzhou fu on the 13th day of April of the current year, 1707; that is on the 6th day after the above-mentioned decree came to my notice. In witness to this, those present sign this with their own hand.

Friar Alvaro, Bishop of Ascalon, Vicar Apostolic of the Province of Jiangxi.

We the undersigned Friars of the Order of Minors Reformed, of the Spanish Congregation, testify and guarantee insofar as we are able that the Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Friar Alvaro de Benavente, Bishop of Ascalon and Vicar Apostolic of this Province of Jiangxi has in our presence presented and signed this letter of appeal. As guarantee of this, we also sign the same in the city of Ganzhou fu, on the 13th day of April of this year, 1707.

Friar Juan Fernández Serrano, Friar Diego de S. Rosa, Friar Miguel Roca.

I the undersigned Apostolic Notary, certify to all who are to examine them that this letter of appeal written above agrees with the 1st and 2nd copies of the letter which were sent through the Most Illustrious Lord Friar Alvaro de Benavente, Bishop of this Province of Jiangxi to the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch of Antioch, Apostolic Visitor, with the powers of a legate a latere, and of which a copy remains with the same Lord Bishop of Ascalon.
