November 1st. The Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon gave Father Jartoux who was visiting him a bundle of unsigned writings. The Father had asked for it to be first signed and to be addressed to him. But the Bishop was unwilling, saying that he would prefer to expose these writings to danger rather than hand them over signed as requested. The Father took the bundle, and signed it himself, and found it had been against marriage between one of the faithful and an unbeliever etc.

A rather notable event that occurred today was that the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon approached the Sacrament of Penance and, having made his confession, received absolution after easily sixty-four days. This seemed all the more unexpected to us in that from the 30th of June until the 28th of August, while he was still in Beijing, the Lord Patriarch was accustomed to go to confession at least every second day.[^p208-1]

### 1. Peter Wang and Thomas Gu are carried off to prison

November 8th. The two mandarins Zhao and Henkama came to our northern house seeking Wang Baiduoluo and Gu Duomei,[^p208-2] who were to be bound. Father Superior asked that this should not be done in our house. They partly gave in to the request but, however, threatened that unless we allowed our servants to be used to guard them on the way, the matter would be dealt with in the usual way of the public tribunal with clamour and noise, and they demanded that our Chinese servants who knew the houses of the said men, should go before the officials to give evidence about this. This also the Father asked should not be done. However, finding another way of doing it, they sent a Christian mandarin, who was authorized to bring both men in chains to the shenxing si,[^p208-3] the Tribunal, which is the criminal tribunal[^p208-4] in the interior of the Palace.

November 9th. In the morning I had hastened to the Royal Villa, where I heard from the mandarin Zhao, that today Wang Baiduoluo, Gu Duomei, and Jin Xiu would be submitted to their first examination. Around midday, returning to the city, I met the assistants of the mandarins who asked if the three captives had already passed by. I had not long passed the very gate of the city into the royal street when I saw on a cross street Peter Wang and Gu Duomei, both bound with nine chains, and Jin Xiu with six, being led into the Royal Road to be taken to the Prince at the Villa. I hurried home and learned that at that very hour the assistant of the mandarin Henkama had rushed through our door and before the Fathers had been informed, laid hands on the catechist Jin Xiu. The Fathers hurried there but were unable to take the chains off the man or prevent him from being led away against their will. However, they sent one of the servants to find out what was happening. This came to nothing, since the servant went to the tribunal but the catechist was taken to the Villa.

### 2. And are examined

They were examined in the first instance by the Prince in the presence of the mandarins Zhao, Henkama, and Charki,[^p209-5] and the President of the shenxing si Tribunal. Their replies were taken down in writing.[^p209-6]

November 10th. The Prince told Father Pereira in the Villa that the worst of the three was Thomas Gu, a man of little ability. Zhao Laoye secretly added that Wang Baiduoluo said a great deal which would have serious consequences for the mission. Jin Xiu was a simpleton and a fool who did not seem to have been much involved in affairs.

Today also the Father Superior had received a letter sent from Nanjing by Father Sala[^p209-7] saying that there was a report in the South that the Lord Patriarch was reversing his journey to return to Beijing. This news the Father Superior communicated | to the Most Reverend Bishop of Conon, and also reported on the condition of the three Christians who had been dragged away in chains.

<!-- PDF page 210 | book page 200 -->

His Reverence tried to demonstrate that what had happened could not be without great prejudice to the mission, because, as he said, Peter Wang knew the affairs of the mission better than any other person among the Chinese. Next he explained more in detail what part of the mission he feared for, and he began to complain about Father Monteiro, who had formerly written that Peter Wang had used the name of Father Grimaldi[^p210-8] to get entree to mandarins in Fuzhou fu etc. and at the same time extolled his talents which, if not to be feared, at least should be considered. This agreed with the views which the Most Reverend Lord had expressed on October 8th, warning that Peter Wang was a young man skilled in negotiations, and that the Fathers should take care not to anger him. From this the only conclusion to be drawn was that the Most Reverend Bishop of Conon wanted to believe that there could be such activities on the part of the Jesuits against such men and others like them.

### 3. Various deceits of Peter Wang

On this occasion the Father Superior replied that when Peter Wang visited mandarins, and was received by them, it was necessary that he should make use of the authority of someone else and the Most Reverend Lord agreed with this: this was due to the shrewdness of the man; even when he was in Beijing, he had received a certificate from some tribunal which he himself had seen and which he used when visiting mandarins. The Father wanted to learn what tribunal had issued the certificate which he had seen but the Most Reverend Lord either through lapse of memory or some other cause did not know. He informed him better of Chinese customs, showing that the Beijing tribunals did not give such certificates except to mandarins or to those they were ordered to by a special command of the Emperor. Neither was the case with Peter Wang. However, this practise of Peter Wang misusing the names of others was familiar from previous occasions. For example he had used his own name (that is the name of Father Gerbillon) to falsely gain entry to the zongdu[^p210-9] of Nanjing, and also the names of Fathers Pereira and Antoine Thomas to gain entry to the zonghe,[^p210-10] who was in charge of the rivers.

<!-- PDF page 211 | book page 201 | manuscript page 456 -->

The Most Reverend Lord admitted that Peter Wang had often used deceits of this kind but did not cease to praise him on account of his acuteness, and he repeated the same commendation on the following day at the common meal saying that the man’s spirit was sublime and that he was always striving to better himself. Father Kilian replied: “It is alright for him to strive by good means but if he goes on as he has begun, he will end up tied to a gibbet as spirits of this kind in Europe who wander through the land are eventually lifted up and stuck on a yoke.” This bold remark the Most Reverend Lord was rebutted only by his silence. This point seems worthy of more discussion, but this is not the place for it.

### 4. Two servants of the Fathers are arrested

November 11th. Between five and six in the evening men came who arrested and bound John Lu,[^p211-11] our graduate[^p211-12] domestic catechist, and the old janitor.[^p211-13] Warned in time the Father Superior intervened and prevented them from being bound, bringing forward this reason. “Look at what you are doing,” he said. “You have no orders from the Prince or the Tribunal to bind these men, why then are you forcibly loading them with irons? If you declare that they must be bound, because three greedy men have merely mentioned their names and not even accused them of anything, who would avoid being tied up on these grounds?” The officials abandoned the use of force and a horse was given to both, and a servant as well, so that they might go to the Prince in the Villa now that night had fallen.

November 12th. Late in the morning Fr. Parrenin going to the Imperial Villa, met on the way the mandarin Charki, who advised him against coming in sight of the Prince since it was not a good time. Nor should he enter the orchard as he usually did, but should first diligently investigate the state of affairs. | The same advice was given by Zhao, who sorrowfully informed the Father of the following matters. Peter Wang had strongly given evidence against many missionaries. Firstly they had harmed the people of Macau. He then related a case in which the Christians had had to force the Bishop of Conon to his knees before an image of Christ Crucified,[^p211-14] and in addition complained that <!-- PDF page 212 | book page 202 --> the Bishop of Conon had so little reverence for his God and the God of the Christians that (so the non-Christians said) God was unable to vindicate himself for the injury he had received from the Bishop of Conon. The Father also understood from the eunuch Zhang Qilin that the court was of the opinion that the Lord Patriarch imagined that the Emperor could not do without the services of the Europeans, and it was on this false supposition that he had dared so many and so serious actions against His Majesty.

Secondly, Father Guéty when examined in the Villa on the previous day had declared that there had been something of a disturbance in Fujian, and the Bishop of Conon had received blows from Christians, and that on this account he had suspended other missionaries. The eunuch said that he had heard nothing about the Jing Tian tablet,[^p212-15] and by this very fact showed that he had heard it discussed but was silent on the instructions of the Emperor. For he was sent by him to talk to the Father, as will soon become clear. Which, if he did not understand, he should have asked for it to be explained before acting. The Prince gloried in the fact that he had thrown infinite insults in the face of Fr. Guéty, and likewise thrown them against the Bishop of Conon, accusing him of being guilty of an offence against His Majesty by forbidding the use of an imperial yu bi.[^p212-16] If he did not understand it, he should have asked for it to be explained before acting.

### 5. The danger to the Bishop of Conon and Father Appiani

Finally, the Father was ordered by the Emperor through the Prince, that he should inform the Bishop of Conon, according to the statements of the men who had been bound, that he deserved likewise to be bound, and examined under torture, but that this was postponed until the scholars summoned from Fujian should come. Finally, the Prince himself in anger said many things against Fr. Appiani, attributing to him the greater part of the blame because the Lord Patriarch could not speak and hear except through him. He said that Jin Xiu had given very clear testimony against him and, in the presence of Father Parrenin, he called one of Presidents of the Criminal Tribunal, ordering him in the name of the Emperor to send men to seek out Fr. Appiani in Yangzhou; if they had already passed through there, they should pursue him as far as Nanjing, and bring him in chains to Beijing.

### 6. The servants of the Fathers, after various interrogations, are released

While the Father himself, in tears, was telling this very sad news, unexpectedly there arrived at our door our catechist and janitor to relate to us what had happened to them on the previous day. They told us, first, that they had been asked what men were accustomed to enter our house, to speak with the Lord Patriarch. What did they have to do with him? How often did Wang Baiduolu and Thomas Gu? They replied that many people came to satisfy their devotion in our Church but that they did not know how many of them or on what matters spoke to the Lord Patriarch; that Wang Baiduolu and Thomas Gu were accustomed to come on Sundays, and remained sometimes alone for a rather long time with Father Appiani; and that one mandarin named Han had made a submission to the Lord Patriarch.[^p213-17]

Secondly, the Prince began to speak evil against God and Our Lord crucified for love of men, but the catechist had bravely defended the humility of the Crucified Jesus, and was on that account rebuked by the retinue of the Prince and told that he should not speak so boldly in the presence of their master, but that he was not in the least intimidated but confidently explained the treasures of the Divine Wisdom and Goodness latent in the Mystery of the Cross.

<!-- PDF page 214 | book page 204 | manuscript page 457 -->

Thirdly, the Prince forced the catechist to write down the replies given by Fr. Guéty to the questions concerning the actions of the Bishop of Conon in Fujian.

Fourthly, they had not been ill-treated and were sent off peacefully.

Fifthly, they had seen two men outside the door of the Prince who asked if they knew them, and who when they replied they did not, said that they were two Muslims in the service | of the chief of police, sent frequently in previous months by the Prince to the Church on a spying mission, on the pretext that they wanted to accept and to hear the Christian Law preached.

What in the name of the Emperor was ordered to be communicated to the Bishop of Conon was passed on to the said bishop by Fr. Parrenin, who was accompanied by Father Superior J.-F.G.,[^p214-18] and the two Fathers lamented both the present and future ills of the mission.

### 7. The obstinacy of the Bishop of Conon

The Bishop of Conon, as if serene at heart, although trembling in body, said to them that if this case were to begin again, he would not begin or carry it on in any other way than he had done. Father Superior replied that he would never believe that in matters so dangerous he would again issue an order, without consulting the Pope. And thus this frigid exchange ended.

We were all most anxious and full of bitter thoughts, nor was there anyone of our friends who did not warn us that the mission seemed to be in dire straits. In the midst of these anxieties, when we heard that the mandarin Zhao, detained on previous days by the Prince in the Villa, had returned home, Fr. Parrenin visited him, begging his advice and assistance, and pointed out to him how unreasonable were the many things that the Prince had said about the weakness of Our Lord God; nor was the Cross a reason for scandal etc.

### 8. The Eldest Son of the Emperor most hostile to the Christian religion

This speech pleased the mandarin. But he said about the mission that it seemed now to be in danger of ruin; everyone he met thought this, and no remedy appeared because the case was firmly in the hands of an enemy of our holy religion (the Firstborn Prince). He promised in the meantime his efforts in hindering chains being placed on the Bishop of Conon, and helping Fr. Appiani when he had come to Beijing. He praised our catechist in that in spite of the anger of the Prince, he had courageously preached the Christian Faith. Furthermore, he did not want us to be ignorant of the way the matter had gone in the presence of the Emperor that morning. This is what he told the Father: Yesterday we <!-- PDF page 215 | book page 205 | manuscript page 458 --> examined those men of yours. Today in the morning I accompanied the Prince to present their evidence to the Emperor. When the Emperor understood what the Bishop of Conon had done in Fujian he was so moved with anger that his whole face changed, and he remained silent for a long time. All present in the Court were terrified and so restrained their mouths that not even a breath could be observed.

### 9. The Emperor orders Father Appiani to be bound

The Emperor allowed his first impulse of anger to die down, and then, beginning to speak, ordered through the Criminal Tribunal that Fr. Appiani should be arrested and that the Bishop of Conon should not be further disturbed. It would be sufficient if he were bound when the others who had been summoned came. But he wanted the Bishop of Conon warned about this.

November 13th. The mandarin Zhao, as usual, went to the Villa and was admitted into the presence of the Emperor. On his return he told Fr. Parrenin that the opportunity had been given him by His Majesty to narrate everything he had heard from Fr. Parrenin but he was unwilling to reveal if and what he had replied to the Emperor. The Father sorrowfully asked why Fr. Appiani had been ordered to be bound since he had committed neither theft nor robbery nor anything of this kind. The mandarin said that this was the will of God and added the reason that when we saw the anger of the Emperor against the Bishop of Conon we asked that the latter should be burdened with chains and the former simply recalled from his journey. The Emperor on further reflection had resolved on the contrary that the former should be bound and the latter left at liberty. It seems certain to me that God thus disposed the heart of Our Lord the King.

The Bishop of Conon today made two requests of Fr. Superior Jean-François Gerbillon. The first was whether there was any way of informing the Lord Patriarch | of the calamitous state of affairs. The second was whether it was possible for he himself to obtain an audience with the Emperor.

### 10. The fear and danger of the Bishop of Conon

The Father replied that before anyone sought an audience he must first know whether [the Bishop] was prepared to placate the anger of His Majesty, and to give some satisfaction on the point on which the Emperor believed he was injured, namely that a foreigner throughout a whole province had proclaimed a law contrary to Chinese laws and bound the subjects of His Majesty to violate the laws. The Most Reverend Bishop replied it could be said to the Emperor that he would never deal with such matters, that he wished to abstain in the future from the functions of his office, and it would be enough for him to live privately in his church.

At five o’clock in the evening there appeared in our house the brother of Peter Wang carrying the luggage of Fr. Mezzafalce, with whom he had come by way of the river. He thought that he would find here his employer who had preceded him from the boat, not knowing that he had been taken by the mandarins to the bingbu[^p216-19] to await a decision by the President where he should be lodged. The arrival of this young man, and the fact that Fr. Mezzafalce had already entered the city together with Fr. Carvalho,[^p216-20] a priest from Manila, was announced to the Bishop of Conon, not without fear on his part. For he rightly feared that this young man might get involved in these matters and that Fr. Carvalho, if he fell into the hands of the Prince, might be forced to give much information about Manila which could not be made known without harm to many.[^p216-21] Therefore he had wanted to persuade Fr. Mezzafalce when he arrived that he should immediately send away the aforementioned priest and the young man.

### 11. Father Mezzafalce comes to Beijing

Fr. Mezzafalce had entered Beijing carried on a chair with four bearers accompanied by one mandarin of the place from which he had come, and another from the Beijing Tribunal. The President was away at the Villa and when, having finished his business with the Emperor he returned to the city, it was night. It did not occur to the mandarin that the previous day it had been ordered by the Emperor through Henkama that Fr. Mezzafalce should be housed in the Dong Tang. So he gave him a choice of accommodation for this night, asking to what Church he wanted to be carried. The Rev. Fr. replied that in which Duoluo Da Laoye[^p216-22] had lived and he was immediately carried from the Tribunal to our gate between 7 and 8 in the evening.

<!-- PDF page 217 | book page 207 | manuscript page 459 -->

### 12. The advice given him by the Fathers

The Fathers came to meet him in the street, and after they had greeted him, he was informed by Fr. Superior that he could not be received in our house, but that he should go to the Eastern Church as His Majesty had laid down. He should also know that Fr. Guéty was the sole cause of his being summoned to Beijing. The best advice seemed to be that if he were questioned by the Prince he should say that he could not yet make a judgment on Chinese matters; that hitherto it had been permitted for all to follow the custom of the old missionaries; that he had never involved himself in intricate matters; that he was of the common opinion until it was otherwise decided by the Pope. Fr. Mezzafalce willingly listened to this, and ordered his chair to proceed to the Eastern Church. We have, however, his letter to Father Faglia of pious memory[^p217-23] in which he shows that he blamed us at the time for being compelled by the Tribunal to come to Beijing.

The Most Reverend[^p217-24] Fr. Mezzafalce after taking supper spent almost the whole night conversing with Doctor Borghese, from whom he received much news which the doctor himself believed true although much of it was false. Previously this man had shown special concern for his guest, praising him often, and frequently on his behalf making threats and foretelling much harm to come, because Fr. Mezzafalce | had seven or eight brothers at Rome living in the palaces of influential prelates. Moreover, he had prepared for him clothes of expensive furs, spending more than 120 Roman scudi to buy clothes for the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon and the Most Reverend Mezzafalce. Neither when they were first offered wished to make use of them and Fr. Mezzafalce had in fact brought with him in the boat tailors to make suitable clothes. Finally, however, they accepted the more expensive ones, and on leaving Beijing gave them back to Dr. Borghese.[^p217-25]

November 14th. The Emperor had ordered that Fr. Mezzafalce on arrival should not be permitted to speak with either Europeans or Chinese, and the same prohibition bound his servant; but an error had been committed by the

<!-- PDF page 218 | book page 208 -->

Prince on October 30th, so that he met that night with Dr. Borghese, nor were we able to prevent this. This morning, when the mandarin Henkama heard of the arrival of the guest, he strongly expostulated with the mandarin from the bingbu, arguing that without advising him he had committed this error and that he brought it about that the Most Reverend Mezzafalce was moved to the house of the mandarin Zhang or Charki, as the Emperor had already decreed, although we were totally ignorant of this.

Before His Reverence left the Eastern Church, Father Régis[^p218-26] and Father Antoine[^p218-27] took care that, as the Bishop of Conon had also instructed in a letter sent to him, he should send Fr. Carvalho at least as far as Linqing together with the servant, the brother of Peter Wang. The Bishop of Conon also advised that if the Lord Patriarch had any letters to him (for they had stopped for a day with the Lord Patriarch) he should keep them in a safe place or hand them over to Father Antoine, until a suitable time came for him to receive them. In fact he had two, and he entrusted them to Father Thomas.

### 13. The Bishop of Beijing freed from danger by the Fathers

Today, Father Parrenin revealed to the Bishop of Conon that on the previous day the Prince, throwing up many things heard in the examination of Fr. Guéty, said among other things that he had learnt that there was a certain European Bishop of Beijing living in Linqing, an old man totally white-haired; this man should be called to him so he could extract many things under torture. To this the Father replied that the man was weak through old age, that he had always remained quietly in China for more than 20 years, and that even now he did not now know the Chinese language. He enlarged on this to show that questioning should not be attempted if one knew the questioner could not understand and could not reply in Chinese. This duty of charity Father Parrenin undoubtedly owed to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing, and he is deserving of praise for rightly doing what he could not leave undone. However, the reader will wonder, when he finds out in the account of following months that this very Christian love spent on the Bishop by a Jesuit, others attempted to depict as a fault. Patience! Through this alone will the Jesuits be victorious.

As night was coming on, an instruction came from the Palace, ordering that Fathers Gerbillon, Pereira, Kilian, and Parrenin should be present in the Villa on the following day.

November 15th. Two of the Fathers being ill,[^p219-28] Fathers Parrenin and Kilian went alone. While they were standing before the doors of the Palace, the Prince entered, leaving them there and not long afterwards the President of the Tribunals brought in Fr. Mezzafalce, leading him by the hand, and had his presence announced to the Prince. The Prince came out and, sending away the two Fathers, questioned Fr. Mezzafalce: what was his name, age, and Society or Congregation? Fr. Mezzafalce did not sufficiently understand his interrogator and he now called for the Fathers to assist him. Neither the place nor the business | which occupied him allowed the Prince to ask more. He returned inside again and when he had finished his business he had to leave the palace and hasten into the city to bring his little brothers by order of the Emperor. For this reason he ordered all to wait with a new prohibition on the Fathers speaking to Fr. Mezzafalce. Through a servant they informed the new guest of the prohibition, and gave orders that if he wished to have a meal, the servant should prepare somehow something he could eat, which he could enjoy in his chair after it was warmed up. A little before four in the afternoon the Prince having returned from the city called the Fathers into his palace, and sitting there in the library with the three mandarins Zhao, Henkama, and Cherki made them also sit down. Then at his order the Most Reverend Father Mezzafalce came in, trembling, and was ordered to kneel down and answer the questions. Disturbed by the few questions proposed, or by the strangeness of the place, or his insufficient knowledge of Chinese, his answers were not to the point. The Fathers therefore acted as interpreters. Father Kilian was ordered to come closer for some reason, but Father Parrenin interpreted almost all the questions and replies, while in the meantime the Prince asked other questions through Father Kilian.

### 14. Father Mezzafalce is questioned by the Prince

Question 1: What was his name? Reply: He Nadu.

2. How old was he? Reply: Forty-five.

3. What was his Society or Congregation? Reply: None.[^p219-29]

4. How many years had he been in China? Reply: Six.

5. Was he in the company of the Lord Patriarch for forty days while the latter was on his way to Beijing? Reply: He was in his company but only for three days.

<!-- PDF page 220 | book page 210 | manuscript page 461 -->

6. What did he speak about in those three days with the Lord Patriarch? Reply: Concerning the supply of money on which I live in China, concerning letters sent to me from Europe, of affairs and friends left behind at home.

7. Did he know Yan Dang and Fang Zhou?[^p220-30] Reply: Yes.

8. Whether he was of the same opinion as Yan Dang concerning Chinese rites? Reply: I am not yet skilled in Chinese books and affairs, and therefore I do not make any judgements about them. I am waiting for the Pope to reach a decision.

9. Did he know that Yan Dang says that it is wrong to honour Heaven, ancestors, Confucius, the tablets, and that Yan Dang had written about them to the Pope? Reply: I know this from Yan Dang himself, who told me when I entered China and lived with him (the last bit no one interpreted for the sake of Fr. Mezzafalce).

10. What are the reasons why Yan Dang says these things are bad? Reply: I don’t know his reasons; his reasons must certainly be in the Chinese books. I have not yet learned to read them, and therefore I am still ignorant of them.

11. Whether he follows or ought to follow Yan Dang? Reply: I have no control over him and he does not want to control me. I live in Zhejiang, he in Fujian. I am not dependent on him.

12. Whether he approves or disapprove that Yan Dang says the Chinese Rites are bad? Reply: I neither approve nor disapprove. I leave him to do as he sees fit. (This was the response that the Emperor on hearing took very badly, even though during the examination neither the Prince nor the Mandarins judged it to be badly given.)

13. Does he consider Yan Dang to be a good man? Reply: I consider him to be a good man.

14. If he is a good man, why does he say things are bad, which he does not understand? Reply: He thinks he has his reasons for this which I do not know.

15. Whether he is of the same hui or Congregation as Yan Dang? Reply: No. We do not have one hui but are separate, each of us lives for themselves. This point Fr. Mezzafalce wished to explain at greater length, saying that the French clerics could be considered one hui because they make one community in their Seminary and are a particular group under one Superior etc. (This last point nobody interpreted lest the way should be opened to new complications.)

16. Whether | he had seen and sometimes spoken with Fang Zhou?

Reply: I have seen him and spoken with him.

<!-- PDF page 221 | book page 211 -->

17. Did you say to him, that the Holy Pontiff hated, or was angry with Jesuits? That he has sent Duoluo to punish them? Reply: I said nothing of the sort. I have never heard that the Holy Pontiff was angry with the Jesuits, indeed I have heard that he loves the Jesuits. Duoluo came, not precisely to make a visitation of the Jesuits but of all missionaries and for the affairs of the mission. I said this to Fang Zhou.

18. Whether he knew that there were disturbances among the Christians in Fujian in connection with the Bishop of Conon? Reply: I know. The Bishop of Conon told me this himself.

The replies given up to this were heard kindly enough and gave hope to all that the Most Reverend Father Mezzafalce would be easily dismissed. But the Prince broke off the examination, and ordered that Fr. Guéty should be called, so that he might interrogate him while Fr. Mezzafalce was present. It was then close to 6 in the evening and at 8, by then already night, Fr. Guéty came to the Prince’s Villa. At the same hour Fathers Parrenin and Kilian were again ordered to be present and when they entered outside the pergola which leads to the Prince’s library, Fr. Kilian recognized Fr. Mezzafalce’s presence in the darkness, and congratulated him on having answered well and briefly exhorted him not to make any changes in the future. For the Father believed, and had heard from all present, that he had behaved moderately and prudently. On this occasion there was no further examination, because the Prince was already asleep, not that he was of a somnolent disposition but when his body felt it needed rest, he was accustomed to casually lie down and take some sleep, while requiring others to keep watch when it was time for him and everybody else to sleep, and so his restless spirit allowed him to accommodate himself to the common life of men.

### 15. Father Guéty examined again, and accuses the Fathers

November 16th. At first light, Fr. Guéty was heard alone by the Prince, together with the mandarins Zhao, Henkama, and Charki. The Fathers stood by outside the doors. Just before 8 o’clock the Prince, who was about to mount his horse to go to his father, said with much laughter to the Fathers who were standing next to him: “Now I know everything. Your Pereira has said many nasty things against the Lord Patriarch: I am going immediately to tell my Father.” We looked at each other in astonishment and could not but doubt that Fr. Guéty had woven a new web. Following the Prince we entered the orchard, full of anxiety about the new dispute, but nowhere could we get to the bottom of this new affair. Nine o’clock had already passed, and the Emperor had not yet made time to hear in greater detail about such matters. Saying that he would give an audience after dinner, he ordered a new deposition from Fr. Guéty, and that the replies of Fr. Mezzafalce <!-- PDF page 222 | book page 212 | manuscript page 462 --> should in the meantime be committed to writing. The Prince hastening home ordered that Fr. Guéty should be examined again by the mandarins. Sitting in the inside room were Zhao, Henkama, Charki, and the President of the shenxing si Tribunal. Fr. Guéty knelt before them. Fathers Kilian and Parrenin were given a specific order to stand outside at the windows. With or without an order, one of the mandarins from the household of the Prince joined them. It was he who the previous year had received baptism whether out of conviction or pretence. A crowd of servants were also standing around.

The second examination began in this way. “Do you stand by what you said some hours ago in the presence of the Prince?” Reply: “I stand by it.” “Is there anything you wish to add?” Fr. Guéty hesitated for some time, and said: “I can’t think of anything.” Then after a brief silence, he burst out: “When I was in Tartary in a place called Rehe, and in another called Karahoton, Fathers Pereira and Barros and Brother Paramino said many nasty things against the Lord Patriarch, and Father Pereira wanted to induce me | to accuse the Lord Patriarch.” Question: “What kind of nasty things?” Reply: “They said he had come with the intention of getting rid of the Jesuits, that he had offered the Emperor only trifles but that they had always offered gifts worthy of His Majesty.” Zhao broke in: “But this is not saying anything nasty but simply the truth. We say the same. Have you anything else?” Reply: “No.” Hearing the denial, Zhao ordered that it should all be written down. But when Fr. Guéty heard that the name of Brother Paramino was going to be written down, he said that it should not be included. But the mandarin said: “It was you who came out with that name.” Fr. ### 17. He took it badly that he was named less than honourably

The Most Reverend Father mildly heard all this, but then complained that the Prince and the mandarins, when speaking about him, had baldly referred to him and taken care to write it down as Xiyang ren[^p224-34] (that is the European) without any other more honourable designation. The Fathers judging that this complaint came from his inexperience of Chinese matters, and wanting to instruct him in them more gently, pointed out that it was the Chinese custom that all people when accused in a tribunal, or when their accusation and evidence were being treated before the Emperor, were referred to only by the short and humble name they had received from their father and mother without any title even if they were princes. However until then both Fathers did not know that this error had settled so deeply in his mind. They learned this only on the following day from a letter of Fr. Contancin, soon to be referred to. When this conversation was finished, all were sent to their homes with this command, however, that what Fr. ### 19. How the humility of Christ should be commended to pagans

It is indeed true that the intention of such reverend gentlemen demanding such honours in China can be regarded as the high spiritual aim that the Supreme Pontiff should be honoured in them, as they say, and that glory should accrue to our holy religion. But equally it must be agreed that the Pope is dishonoured, and he whose vicar he is, when among the gentiles missionaries demand more human glory among the magistrates of the Empire than the Viceroy of Naples would grant to them. Nor can the gentiles be brought to appreciate the humility <!-- PDF page 227 | book page 217 | manuscript page 465 --> of Christ when his preachers are not merely forced to accept some honour but strongly exact it of people in authority.

### 22. The Jesuits had the right to ask for the documentation of the accusations made against them and to keep them

To this perhaps some may say, as they said of the previous process made in September in the province of Guangdong and as they still say in the province of Shandong, how did such Guétian manuscripts come into the hands of the Jesuits? ### 25. The Fathers exhort Bishop Maigrot to satisfy the Emperor. They propose an easy way

The Emperor was preparing to come from the Villa into the city so that for a few days he would live in the Palace before the winter fowling and hunting season, by which he was accustomed to relieve the miserable winter period. ### 26. They were talking to a deaf and obstinate man

The Father insisted: “However evident it may seem to Your Reverence that the Chinese books are to be understood in this way, is it not however any less evident that in the understanding of the books the Emperor is superior; if he contends that the words of some deceased emperors are to be explained in this way, why cannot you accept the explanation of a living emperor, and one indeed in favour of the conversion of so many souls, and perhaps the whole of China?” But the Bishop of Conon stood his ground with a firm front and gave no means on his part for placating the anger of the Emperor.

November 23rd. The Emperor asked us gathered together in the Palace what the Europeans elsewhere[^p234-43] were doing. ### 30. Remedy proposed by the Mandarin Zhao

November 24th. ### 31. Concerning all the missionaries being called to the court and examined

Since we were so hard-pressed our reply in part was: If all the missionaries should be called it was unavoidable that the Christians would be frightened by news of this and the enemies of the Christians would derive comfort. When the Emperor was informed of this by the Prince he responded: “We ourselves should call all the Europeans under the honourable title that His Majesty wants to see them all. In this case those who do not wish to come can stay away voluntarily.” We saw that we had suddenly fallen into greater difficulties since already there was no doubt that the Emperor, following the advice of the Prince, had laid down that all should be called, and moreover suspected that we were resisting lest some should come who might wish and be able to serve His Majesty. And so, in order to avert his indignation, we humbly replied: “If His Majesty orders, we will immediately call all of our Society, but we cannot call others without complaints on their part, etc.” The Prince went and returned several times, but then angrily and impatiently brought back this response: “The Emperor says: ‘You will not be helped by your fine philosophical and theological distinctions. Take a look at yourselves; you are not being reasonable. Black is proposed to you and not accepted by you; white is proposed, and likewise refused. It would be best if you all are called, otherwise those who reject the laws of the empire will have an opportunity to get away.’ My father the Emperor orders you to deliberate again, and reply tomorrow.”

### 31. Reasons brought forward by the Fathers to prevent it happening[^p236-48]

November 26th. When we got home we again considered the reasons we could bring forward why this plan of the king to call all the missionaries should be reconsidered, to see if those we previously thought true were indeed true in the sight of God and therefore unchangeable. The first of them was the previously mentioned great harm if all the churches were bereaved of their pastors for a number of months so that baptism, help for the dying, and <!-- PDF page 237 | book page 227 | manuscript page 471 --> the other necessary sacraments were lacking. 2. It was rightly feared that more than one of the missionaries would die on account of age, weakness of body, and the labour of the journey. 3. Several would spend on the journey the whole of their stipend and would suffer no small loss at home. 4. If among the rest came the Dominican Rev. Fr. Luxán and the Franciscan Rev. Fr. | Lucas, nothing was more likely than that from weakness of mind they would cause much harm to the missionaries of their Orders.[^p237-49] 5. If the two literati from Fujian,[^p237-50] one of whom is an apostate, or the three prisoners[^p237-51] should get to hear that those who are to come include one who had thrown down the Jing Tian tablet and one who threatened to jump on it with his feet, then there is reason to fear that they might testify against them in their presence. 6. It seemed impossible that if many more were to be called to be examined for the first time by the Emperor that they would not commit errors similar to these committed by the Lord Patriarch, the Bishop of Conon, Appiani, Guéty, etc. 7. It could not at all be presumed that those who were assured in their opinions would chose to be absent, as the Emperor was persuaded, but rather in imitation of the Bishop of Conon, they would become so excited that they would rush eagerly into battle and excite a manifest persecution. 8. We had no authority to call missionaries, and not only would we be given the blame by the Lord Patriarch who was still present in China, but if we communicated to the missionaries this royal will, all the bad effects, of which a great crowd would necessarily follow, would be attributed to us. These reasons, as far as we could, we communicated to Zhao Laoye, and to our other friend. But he replied to us that the opinion of the Emperor was even more firm, and that all that remained was to accommodate ourselves to his decision.

### 32. Reply of the Prince

November 27th. Well into the morning the Prince spoke to us gathered in the Palace. “Do not think that my father the Emperor has called you for a deliberation over whether all the Europeans are to be compelled <!-- PDF page 238 | book page 228 | manuscript page 472 --> to come to Beijing. This has already been concluded in the heart of His Majesty. In matters of government he does not need the counsel of others, much less in your trifling affairs. Remember how in the splendour of his wisdom he has so often dispelled the misty plots of rebels; how by his strong counsel he has prevailed over the enemy Eleuths. He has granted you a delay for discussion on account of your slowness in doing things, not because he needed any suggestions from you. Besides, he orders you to call to memory your error last year in asking for your Duoluo to be admitted against the judgment and explicit advice of His Majesty. If again your untimely prayers are heard, how much harm, how many inconveniences will arise? Consider briefly whether it is not expedient for all to be called so that it will be clear how false is the accusation of the Lord Patriarch and François Guéty that the Portuguese and you are preventing others coming into the service of His Majesty. Now reply quickly what means are to be taken, lest things of this kind happen.” To these harsh words he added a few about Father Visdelou,[^p238-52] whom the Emperor understood was also acting against the laws of the Empire.

It was clear that the anger of His Majesty was not to be provoked any more. On our knees we all said this one thing. Whenever the Emperor has decided to call all Europeans to the Court, he will be acting in accordance with his power, but he should not exclude mercy, lest missionaries be punished and the holy religion suffer harm before their guilt was established.

For easily an hour the Prince discussed our reply with His Father, and on his return told us the following.

### 33. The business is deferred

My Father the Emperor says that you are men who think of lesser affairs than those of the Empire—who wish to avoid anything inconvenient to yourselves, to freely dispute, and to seek by disputing to conquer—and are not afraid to put your private interests before those of the Empire. Now he orders you to go away and he suspends in the meantime this business of calling | all the Europeans. He wishes however that you <!-- PDF page 239 | book page 229 --> not be unaware that a time is coming when you will realize that it must be done, as His Majesty has proposed.

We heard all this in silence with foreheads pressed to the ground, regarding it as a gain that the danger of the mission had at least been postponed, with the slight hope that with the passing of time a milder counsel from Heaven might flow into the mind of the Emperor. However, there was fear from another quarter that very soon the arrival of the literati from Fujian and Fr. Appiani might stir up an even graver danger. Furthermore, both our people and our friends at Court openly noted that the Prince was aggrieved that for the time being his desire to harm Europeans had been frustrated by the determination of the Emperor.

November 28th. This was Sunday, and the Most Reverend Bishop of Conon, as his custom was on Sundays, dined with us. After the meal he said, at the beginning of the conversation, that he felt much relieved even in body. When we asked the reason he explained that he had resigned his office of Vicar Apostolic into the hands of the Lord Patriarch, and that he had no doubt that his resignation would be accepted by the Lord Patriarch because he had used a manner of writing which would move his spirit and would be able to make his resignation welcome. So it would be agreeable for him to return to Fuzhou fu and, presuming the Emperor allowed, spend the rest of his life as a simple missionary.

November 30th. The Fathers dared to return to the Palace. Asked why they came, they replied that they sought the pity of the Emperor. This day they did not reach persons of higher rank. But the inferiors having received permission from their superiors treated them very badly, not giving reasons but throwing at them insults full of shame.

### 34. Insults thrown at the Fathers by the courtiers

For example: Why are you wearying the ears of the Prince? Will it not be glorious for him to arrange with his father that there may live in China others sent to China by your Holy Pontiff like Fang Zhou and co.: liars, imposters, slanderers, drinkers of strong wine? Those are the arts and sciences of such a man, if indeed he is a man. The Prince gave him the honour of serving food to him but he would not allow even a morsel to pass his throat unless he had first poured down it half a pound of brandy and, in the presence of the Prince, like a slave charioteer, drunk that sour cup of evil, to the shame of the Prince and to the disgust of the small children of the Emperor etc.[^p239-53] We realised that such things were hurled at us so that full <!-- PDF page 240 | book page 230 --> of shame we should speak nothing that might be regarded as prayers of intercession. But Father Parrenin, given an opportunity, did not cease to speak very well on behalf of Fr. Appiani and to bring it about that these words might be passed on to the Emperor when occasion presented itself.
