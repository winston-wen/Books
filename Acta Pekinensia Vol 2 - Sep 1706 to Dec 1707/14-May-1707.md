### 1. The arrangement of the material to be related

This month was almost a blank so far as we Beijing Jesuits were concerned. We remained at home, alone and miserable, waiting for new terrors to come from elsewhere almost hour by hour. I would happily leave out the entire month, were it not that this might strike some minds as suspicious, or that too great a curiosity might make someone suspicious. So I shall relate the very few things that were done, although they would be hardly worth mentioning under any other month. Then I shall stretch my pen to cover the provinces. And although in April I did promise, firstly, to say something here about the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon, the reader might nonetheless excuse me if I begin by speaking directly about the Lord Patriarch and only indirectly about the aforementioned Bishop of Ascalon. Secondly, I will add here some comments on the appeal made against the patriarchal ruling by the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Macau, João de Cazal, which he sent to the Lord Patriarch while he was still on his way. Thirdly, I shall give an account of what was done in Hangzhou, from where 7 Dominicans and 3 secular priests were expelled by the Emperor, and how two agents were sent by the Emperor from Suzhou and the Lord Patriarch was ordered to be taken away to Macau. Fourthly, the appeal lodged by 24 priests in Sanchahe along with other matters related to them. Fifth and last, I will give an account of the journey which was made to Yangzhou to seek residence permits.

Among the other disasters it happened that missionaries’ letters sent to Beijing from the South could not get through, as will be obvious from the letters from various people which are reproduced here in the course of this month. Indeed there were none from the North either, apart from one from the Lord Bishop of Beijing saying that he had absolutely no powers to help the mission in its extreme difficulties and that help must be sought from God alone. So, we just carried on with the Christians and completed the novena we had started last month in honour of St. Joseph, who had been named by the Holy See as patron of the China mission, asking him specially to intercede for the mission and to be our protector as we teetered on the edge of the abyss.

May 4th. Fr. Antonio Provana got things together for his return to Jiangzhou from Beijing. He had stayed on with us here for quite a long time, detained both by his illness and by our desire to have him here. It was a good idea to have yet another of the missionaries here in the capital at this extremely difficult time, so that in person he could see and read about everything which was going on in the house or which people wrote about from elsewhere. This <!-- PDF page 470 | book page 460 --> same man wrote a letter in his own name to the Lord Patriarch from Beijing, protesting vehemently and urging him to consider again and again how much irreparable damage and spiritual harm was being done to the Chinese Church because he was being led by his feelings of suspicion of the Beijing Jesuits; his suspicions were quite groundless, but even had they been well founded, they would never have given him the right or even any sound reasons to uproot the vineyard of the Lord planted by the Jesuits, or to eradicate the Gospel entirely from the empire.

As things worked out, before Fr. Provana could leave, and entirely without his knowledge, we had unanimously voted to have him appointed as procurator to Rome. Father Antoine Thomas, the acting Father Vice-Provincial, informed the Reverend Fathers José Monteiro, the Vice-Provincial, and Fr. José Raymundo, the Visitor. What was being proposed was no light matter: deciding that an extraordinary procurator had to be appointed beyond those procurators laid down by the customs and statutes of our Society[^p470-1]

May 11th. A letter arrived in the capital from the mandarin Zhang Changzhu addressed to his colleague Hescken or Henkama, ordering us to write once more at the Emperor’s command to the Lord Patriarch. The contents of the letter, which was originally written in Manchu, were, in accordance with a true and faithful translation, as follows:

On the 26th day of the 3rd moon,[^p470-2] the emperor was informed that Fr. Grimaldi and his fellow Jesuits have written to Duoluo (the Lord Patriarch, exhorting him etc.).[^p470-3] When the Emperor heard this he ordered the following to be written to him immediately:

<!-- PDF page 471 | book page 461 | manuscript page 607 -->

When you first came here, you informed the Emperor face to face that you had come here for no other reason than to express your thanks. But now all you do is endlessly raise matters which have nothing to do either with the few of us | who live in China nor with the great many who are outside China. You should note the kindness with which such a great Prince is treating Europeans, foreigners though they are, and you should reflect carefully whether in future they should be allowed to listen to the words (decrees) of Yan Dang (the Bishop of Conon) and others like him, which cause fuss and disturbances here. Were the Emperor to be even a little angered, he would expel all of us (from China), and if you later come to regret it, you will have regretted it too late. If you wish to take our advice, you should depart as quickly as possible.

See the proceedings sent to the Holy See from the Palace.[^p471-4]

### 2. The Emperor orders the Lord Patriarch to be taken to Macau, and be held there

This letter seems to contain a lapse of memory, as if it had slipped his Majesty’s mind that he had previously ordered us to write using the same argument. So he did not expect either that we would be unwilling to write in this vein, or that we would tell him that we had written. However, on his own initiative, he sent two Tartars on May 18th,[^p471-5] one called Foiorò, whom the Prince employed as a kind of secretary while on his travels, and the other Pagharà, a Mandarin from the Ministry of Works, with the order that they should hasten to Guangzhou and arrange for the Lord Patriarch to be escorted to Macau and held there until the replies from Rome arrived.

Those of us who were in Beijing were very upset by this latest thunderbolt. We did finally send the letter I referred to under April 10th although, as I pointed out at the time, it was not given to the Lord Patriarch. But it would not have been enough to prevent the two delegates carrying out their orders. I attach a copy of the letter. It should be noted that it was written in the name of all the Beijing Jesuits, since the Emperor’s command was addressed equally to everyone. The fact that the Emperor was told that Fr. Grimaldi had written his name first was because we wished to observe the Chinese custom in accordance with which <!-- PDF page 472 | book page 462 --> in any community, precedence is given to the more senior, and also because we wished to keep from His Majesty, as we had managed to do so far, that in this very month the previous year Fr. Grimaldi had, wrongly, been dismissed by the Lord Patriarch from his office as Visitor and any position as a superior.

### 3. The letter from the Beijing Jesuits to the Lord Patriarch

May 12th Excellent, Most Illustrious Reverend Lord, etc., Although Your Excellency would not listen to our advice in all the time you were here in Beijing, and after you left here you sent bitter replies to our letters, nevertheless, as the mission is on the very brink of ruin, the charity of Christ compels us once again to write these few lines, with due reverence and humility, and out of care for the preservation of the Chinese Church and of Your Lordship.

Your Excellency shows that you thought the practices of the Bishop of Conon could be introduced without bringing the mission into grave danger. Your own sad experience has now taught you that the truth is quite otherwise. If Your Excellency wanted to try out for yourself what the resources of such a man could achieve among the Chinese, that has now been more than sufficiently explored; our position has for the most part collapsed and been lost. For this reason in our letter of today, by the compassion of Christ, we adjure Your Excellency and beg you to agree as quickly as you can to come to the assistance of the Chinese Church as it rushes headlong to destruction.

Your Excellency must know better than anyone else that to furnish such assistance is in your power alone. Delay is impossible, as is shown by the danger, growing more serious every day, of our religion being lost in China. You will already have heard from your own people and from others what has been done and what has been decreed in Nanjing. The Emperor’s sense of outrage grows daily more powerful, to the extent that the more he once showed kindness to Your Excellency, the more his anger is now to be feared. If only it could be contained long enough to allow Your Excellency to depart for somewhere else safe and sound. That is of course what most causes us to be anxious and worried on your behalf, because of the sacred office which you hold, that it be not subjected to any further indignity.

We have just recently learned from letters sent to us here and written by the Emperor in his own hand, that, as in Beijing last year, so now he is once more complaining (if I may use his own words) that what Your Excellency says does not even agree with what you said on the last occasion, let alone with the facts. The courtiers who deal with these matters were very disturbed by this letter, and are forever confronting us with how often and in what terms

<!-- PDF page 473 | book page 463 | manuscript page 608 -->

Your Excellency protested that you had been sent purely on a | fact-finding mission regarding Chinese affairs, to learn about them and report what you had learned to Rome together with the Emperor’s documents and decrees. Moreover, you said that the mind of His Holiness in delegating Your Excellency to come here was simply to thank the Emperor for so many and such great favours which he has conferred on our religion, and to strengthen the increasing goodwill between such a great Prince and the missionaries and Christians.

When they compare such things and others of the same kind, noted by them and written down in the records, with what Your Reverence did in Nanjing as they have come to understand from His Majesty’s most recent decree, the result is that they cannot help producing insults and jokes which damage the very name of European. We are waiting to hear even worse insults than those from the Emperor himself when he gets back. Of course we can put up with them without being too upset, but only provided that we can make sure that his anger poured out against us all does not with one fatal blow cut off the whole China mission which has been built up by a hundred years of toil. This cannot happen without the loss of countless souls and without causing enormous sadness to His Holiness, who had but one aim in this legation of Your Excellency, to save the Chinese Church and to satisfy the Emperor, who more than any other of the kings of Asia has deserved so well of the Christian faith. We do not mention the enormous swell of discontent in the whole of Europe when they see Your Excellency expelled from China along with all the missionaries to whom you were assigned by the Pope, with your authority and your status, to be a protector and comforter.

### 4. They pray that he wait for the Pope’s reply

We also make no mention of the universal indignation of posterity, especially at the loss of the mission of the East, as well as in the other neighbouring countries which it will drag down with it, which will result from your legation unless Your Excellency agrees to apply the remedy which lies there in your hands, by suspending the obligation which you have published and imposed upon the missionaries until we have received a reply from His Holiness.

Since all this is for the good of the whole Chinese Church as well as being of the greatest importance for Your Excellency personally, we decided to write to you, most humbly requesting that you receive it in the same spirit as we are writing, for the general good and salvation of souls. But, in order not to cause difficulty for Your Excellency, or to appear as if we have little or no respect for your position, we shall in future make no further attempts to offer advice which by then may in any case come too late. We will simply be content with setting <!-- PDF page 474 | book page 464 | manuscript page 609 --> out the sad state of the Christian cause in this mission to the Supreme Shepherd, that he may take pity on his flock amid all the troubles which it is undergoing and give his succour to it when it is in danger.

We should indeed have written this letter in a more severe tone, because it was to be assumed that the Emperor would order a copy to be sent to him, and it was reasonable to believe that he would take offence if he found its tone too gentle when in his anger he had ordered that the letter should be at once reproachful and threatening.

May 13th. The Father Visitor[^p474-7] wrote a letter to Fr. Antoine Thomas and another to Fr. Kilian, deploring the iniquity of the mandarins who, once they had prevented the missionaries from setting out for the capital with the intention of seeking the permit, were now preventing them even from sending letters to Beijing. This is how he put it: “I am hanging on to the eight papers which Your Reverence entrusted to me at the beginning of March, until I have an opportunity to send them back to you safely. From here, other than sending them to Jiangxi by courier, there is no other way of getting a letter through, since the Mandarins intercept all the letters which are sent; they do however deliver those which come from Beijing. I am sending Your Reverence a copy of the letter which the Lord Patriarch wrote to the Father Provincial of Japan,[^p474-8] and the Father Provincial’s reply. He did not write to me because I don’t appear in his catalogue etc.”[^p474-9]

By these words the letter suggests firstly that his own courier was dispatched on May 13th or 14th, because it was necessary to do so just then if they were not to be lost forever in the postbag of the Canton mandarins. | This was the same courier who had been sent to deliver the appeal of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Macau to the Lord Patriarch as Father Visitor explained in the letter which was sent at the same time to Fr. Antoine Thomas; we shall have occasion to mention him again. Secondly, it tells us that the Lord Patriarch had addressed the decree not to him as Visitor but to the former Father Provincial of Japan, as <!-- PDF page 475 | book page 465 --> he did to the Superiors of the other Orders who were in Guangzhou. He sent the same decree to the aforementioned Bishop of Macau, who was beyond question their Ordinary, for it to be published and observed by his subjects. The letter of the Lord Patriarch to the Provincial of Japan, his reply, and the letter of the Visitor to Fr. Antoine mentioned here were originally sent to Rome, from where we are waiting for them to be returned, since we had such a mountain of work that there was no time to make copies.[^p475-10]

I can find nothing done or received in Beijing additional to what I have given above except a letter of Fr. Kilian sent on 29 May to Fr. Monteiro, who could reasonably have been expected to have already arrived in Guangzhou. The letter went as follows:

### 5. A letter from Father Kilian to Father Monteiro, deploring the demise of the mission and the loss of letters

Who can express how we in Beijing grieve for the position in which you, your colleagues, and the entire mission are placed? Other missionaries will indeed grieve with a straightforward grief at the sight of the vineyard of the Lord, which they have been cultivating with immense effort and a sincere intention, dying for no good reason. However, we are worn down by a threefold affliction: one is common to everyone, but all the more bitter to us, since we have nurtured the mission with an even more zealous and heartfelt effort; all the deeper then is our sorrow at its destruction, gratuitously hastened. In addition, a second and a third reason for desolation bear down upon us and disturb our soul: some people are shamelessly shouting that it was we who removed the first brick that brought the whole edifice crashing down; others insist that we did not ward off the crash though we should and could have done so, and so want to hold us guilty for such an evil wrong for which the Church of God will weep most bitterly. If I allow my pen to run free it will pour itself out in an unending lament.

But of what use are our tears, or our complaints, however justified? Here we sit in Beijing, not knowing what has happened or is still to happen. No letters have reached us here for a long time. The last one from Jesuits was sent from Nanjing on April 12th, bringing the sorrowful news of the lamentable expulsion of Frs. San Giorgio and Hervé. We believe that the Emperor has forbidden the Fathers in his party to write; it is at least certain that the Prince makes sure that their letters are delayed. The omens in that direction presage nothing but ill. Today we heard from the Master of Imperial Tours that on the 24th of the 4th <!-- PDF page 476 | book page 466 | manuscript page 610 --> moon the Emperor was in Jinhan near Yangzhou, and that he is making forced marches to get back to the capital; as a result, fear and trembling has set in among us, since to our worried hearts many questions occur which they might want to put, and we have not found any way to reply to His Majesty.

I ask Your Reverence to write to us very often; it doesn’t matter which of us you address the letters to, provided you give us a faithful, frequent, and brisk account of what is going on there. For many months now we have had only some brief words from Guangzhou, and, being totally ignorant of what is happening there, we are unable to look after the interests either of our fellow Jesuits or of others. But Your Reverence should be sure that we are ready to do anything at all that we can to be of service, | whether to the Lord Patriarch, or others, or Jesuits. But if we hear nothing, or hear about things only after the opportunity has passed, is it any surprise if all our expertise ends up being useless?

### 6. The great danger in delay

For these times are extremely difficult, and our affairs are in a very bad state. In order to make even a little progress we will have to think long and hard, and so we must be warned well in advance if our help is needed somewhere. We have never had the power, or, to be more precise, we have never even had one percent of the power which others attribute to us in order to criticise us. And if on any occasion there was some influence we might have had, it has certainly been killed by that shameful rabble: the Emperor would believe that he had been offended by us because he knows that he has been offended by those reverend gentlemen, and this does not allow us to intervene in any way, by prayers or intercessions.

It remains only for me to beg Your Reverence as earnestly as I possibly can to get news to us, to give us information in your letters about whatever might be usefully planned, said, or done to comfort and sustain the dying mission. I commend myself and the agony of the entire mission to Your Reverence’s most holy sacrifices. Beijing, 29 May 1707.

Your Servant in Christ. Kilian Stumpf.

That this agrees with the copy of the original: I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.

The reader is not to suppose that it is pointless to give this letter here; in two ways it may help towards a correct understanding of what really happened in the following months. For, firstly, from it along with others from people in Nanjing and Guangzhou it can clearly be shown that the exchange of letters really and truly was blocked. Secondly, along with others it also proves how genuine were the desires and efforts of the Beijing Jesuits to help the mission <!-- PDF page 477 | book page 467 | manuscript page 611 --> and the person of the Lord Patriarch; it refutes Duoluo,[^p477-11] if he wishes to allege that this or that was openly stated while there was no evidence to the contrary produced from Beijing. He will have to produce at least one example to establish the opposite, unless he wishes to be seen as an obvious liar.[^p477-12] |

Now to deal with what I undertook to set out here concerning the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch and the Most Reverend Bishop of Ascalon.

### 7. The origin of the Lord Patriarch’s sinister suspicions of the Jesuits

In the month of March it was said that on the 20th of that month the Lord Patriarch had weighed anchor and started on his slow journey from Nanjing to Guangzhou before he had received the reply from the Beijing Jesuits or the news of who had succeeded Fr. Monteiro, whom he had placed in an extremely dangerous position in the front rank of the battle. Meanwhile, the Patriarch’s mind was troubled by violent suspicion against the Beijing Jesuits, and Dr. Giovanni Borghese in various ways provided the Lord Patriarch with further grounds for suspicion based on false reports sent in a variety of ways. Since November 1706, everything which his deluded imagination suggested to his melancholy thoughts against the Jesuits he gathered and put down on a large sheet of paper, threatening us more than once that he would produce it in a Roman tribunal. He had already handed this farrago of fantasies to Fr. Sabino, who in turn, breathing threats and predicting the triumphs of skinning the Jesuits alive, left it out to be read not only by the Patriarch’s eyes, already bloodshot from illness, but also by all his staff. One of Fr. Guéty’s servants, John Wang, who had been dismissed by his master on the journey to Guangzhou, also had access to Dr. Borghese’s false statements. I do not know what he had in mind when he joined His Excellency’s ship.

### 8. He accepts the statements of a menial against the Jesuits, and puts them into his official records

There he was questioned about what had happened in Tartary, and is said to have made a statement that Fathers Pereira and Barros had set the whole thing up with the Prince: they had hidden themselves inside, drawing up a list of questions, and the Prince would then shuttle in and out, picking up the questions from the Jesuits’ own mouths and putting them to Fr. Guéty. When he had received some lashes at the Prince’s command, the two Jesuits applauded, laughing their heads off.

<!-- PDF page 478 | book page 468 | manuscript page 612 -->

The Lord Patriarch was given this record in the presence of his chancellor Fr. Andrea Candela, his Secretary Fr. Marcello Angelita, and his interpreter Fr. Buonaventura da Roma, a Franciscan who also had to sign the record since he was the interpreter brought in at the time. One is almost ashamed to refute the crazy invention of a Chinese man who is poor and looking for a reward and who had been dismissed by his own master, when it has been accepted into the tribunal of an apostolic nuncio as evidence against priests of God who have never even been suspected of such a thing. If this testimony proves anything about anything, at least it proves this, that just as in Beijing through Fr. Appiani His Most Excellent Lordship, hardly in a balanced state of mind, had searched out as witnesses disreputable people, servants, boys, debtors, and natives who were motivated by the hope of money, so now he had accepted that unsuitable man in a manner which was all the more disgraceful for being solemn.

### 9. The falsity of his testimony is demonstrated

To anyone acquainted with the Chinese court, it is not hard to see the untruth. Someone who wanted to witness truthfully to the things mentioned above would have to have been present in person, to have seen them in person, and to have heard them at first hand. But this servant was neither present, nor could he have been. For according to the confession of Fr. Guéty written in his own hand, the event took place inside the new palace in Karahoton and indeed in a place where the Firstborn Son, the First Prince stays; that is to say, beyond the second door of the Palatine Hall. None of the servants is allowed even to pass the first one, so how did this little man stretch his eyes and ears beyond the second one? On an earlier occasion, | in September 1706, Fr. Parrenin was blamed because when he arrived with the medical doctor,[^p478-13] the doctor was left at the door; on 22nd February 1707 the same doctor complained that though the Jesuits got in to the courtyards in the imperial palace, he was compelled to wait outside the door. If there was no open access for a doctor, how did it come about that the door was unlocked for the servant of the same man, who later was Fr. Guéty’s servant, in such a way that he could see the Jesuits hidden inside and the Prince coming and going, and even managed to hear what was said? Here in Beijing none of the servants ever saw with their eyes or heard with their ears anything that we did, said, or had to put up with inside. For although one person, if accompanied by us, is not prevented from passing through the outermost gate, he is forced to stop at the dragon gate, where entry is barred even for us by a military <!-- PDF page 479 | book page 469 --> guard unless we are taken in by specific eunuchs through two inner eunuch sentry-posts; each time we enter or leave, our names are recorded on a tablet. So the story is total nonsense to anyone who knows, and deserves no more credence than the hallucinations of a drunk. But because for our opponents there is nothing so absurd that they would not like it to be believed to defame the Jesuits, we hope, if God grants us the opportunity, to set out in legal form the evidence which contradicts his statement—just as under June we intend to produce more than one refutation of Dr. Borghese’s statements, which ought to be withdrawn.

### 10. The Lord Patriarch receives the appeal of the Bishop of Ascalon

His Excellency was only a short distance away from Nanchang in the last few days of April when the appeal of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon appeared, written on the 13th of that month and sent by an express courier who had to carry other letters from his master to Nanjing and thence, by the hand of Fr. Antonio da Silva, both to the Beijing Jesuits and to the Most Illustrious Bishop Bernardino. As His Excellency was reading the appeal, he began to turn many things over in his mind. He then summoned the servant back and asked him earnestly whether he was carrying letters addressed to anyone else. When he said he was, the Lord Patriarch debated with himself whether that appeal would cause disturbances and scandals elsewhere, and so he took the courier whom he had intercepted with the letters into his retinue, saying that he himself was taking the quickest route to the man’s master in order to satisfy him on all points.

On May 4th in Wan’an,[^p479-14] Fathers Monteiro, Pereira, Ferreira, de Mata, and Sousa caught up with the Patriarch. Not long afterwards, Fathers San Giorgio and Hervé joined the group as well. Father Vice-Provincial[^p479-15] José Monteiro was received not unkindly by His Excellency, who handed over to him the letter from Fr. Antoine Thomas in which, in the name of all the Beijing Jesuits, he was pleading that the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch would agree to temporarily suspend the regulation which had been published hastily, so as to prevent the whole mission from collapsing in ruins. To this His Excellency seemed little inclined, as will be seen in the letter from Fr. Monteiro of May 5th to Fr. Antoine, dated May 5th, a full transcript of which is given immediately below. For the moment it is sufficient to note that up until now (I write 16 March 1708) no reply to it from the Lord Patriarch has appeared in Beijing, or, so far as we <!-- PDF page 480 | book page 470 | manuscript page 613 --> have heard, was given elsewhere. There follows the letter of Fr. Monteiro to Fr. Antoine Thomas, the substitute Vice-Provincial:[^p480-16]

### 11. A letter from Father Monteiro to Father Antoine Thomas

I am writing this from the town of Wan’an, where I met the Lord Patriarch; Fr. Giovanni Buonaventura da Roma will bring it to you. The Lord Patriarch has ordered him to go to obtain the piao from the Emperor, as he will tell Your Paternity himself. This Reverend Father deserves any favours which Your Reverence and the other Fathers can do for him because he is dedicated to China. So I ask Your Reverence to do what you can in this matter. | Another point. The Bishop of Conon is indicating to me that he would prefer me to stay, if that accords with His Excellency’s decree. ### 12. The serious illness of the Lord Patriarch because of the loss of some houses and mission-stations belonging to the Sacred Congregation

When the Patriarch was faced with the fact that the two priests San Giorgio and Hervé, powerful punchers on his side and well-trained for the fight, had been forced to leave the ring, and understood that the downfall of the others would follow by virtue of the imperial decree promulgated in Nanjing, he was extremely disturbed. Fr. Buonaventura da Roma, a passenger on the same boat, said in Beijing that the Lord Patriarch’s declining spirits and his failing bodily powers had needed to be maintained by the application of medical poultices for the entire night.

### 13. He therefore sends Fr. Buonaventura to Beijing to receive the

When the Lord Patriarch had recovered his spirits, he explained his plan to his people. He decided first to send piao Fr. Buonaventura to Beijing to receive the imperial permit so that, protected by it, he would be able to preserve the three houses belonging to the Sacred Congregation, one of which had been owned by the Most Reverend Fr. Mezzafalce in Xiaoshan xian,[^p481-18] the second by the Lord Abbate di San Giorgio in the old capital of Nanjing, and the third by Fr. Montigny in Hangzhou. This readily gave public currency to the view that by that very action the Lord Patriarch had suspended his regulation. But people could not reconcile this with the fact that His Excellency, who had undoubtedly experienced the expulsion of those priests with death threats to them and himself for making the same protestation, could accept the permit just to keep the houses from which, to his sorrow, their predecessors had been expelled. Furthermore, some people took the argument further, saying that if, in order not to lose the houses belonging to the Sacred Congregation, his regulation had been suspended, it was even more possible, and indeed his duty, for him to have suspended it because of the loss of so many churches and souls, since these are no less dear to the Vicar of Christ and to Christ Jesus himself. Even Fr. Buonaventura made it clear when he was in Beijing to receive his permit that he had been completely convinced of that, and made a point of using the same form of application as the other Franciscans and Jesuits had presented, as will be clear under June.

### 14. Calumnies levelled against the Beijing Jesuits

The second thing he did was at the suggestion of Fr. Sabino Mariani and those who, with him, were close to the Lord Patriarch. They had no hesitation in saying that the promulgation of the regulation had and would continue to have the most <!-- PDF page 482 | book page 472 | manuscript page 614 --> dismal consequences for which the Jesuits were to be blamed—no longer just those in Beijing but others as well, | from whose number he did not exclude even Fr. Monteiro. He maintained that everything Fr. Monteiro had done he had planned to do, and had planned to suffer everything which he had had to undergo, so that he could damage the Lord Patriarch himself by his obsequious obedience. Nasty rumours of this kind started to make the rounds not just in the boat in which His Excellency was travelling, but in writing throughout the provinces as if they were facts. Indeed it is clear that such calumnies had even reached the Illustrious Bishop of Beijing by December 1707 and January 1708, when His Illustrious Lordship in a friendly way revealed to Fr. Girolamo Franchi, who happened to be travelling through Linqing, that some people were beginning to promote the suspicion that Fr. Monteiro had had been completely insincere and merely feigning blind obedience in taking down and ordering to be taken down the Jing Tian inscription, in order to stir up trouble rather than to follow instructions.

Hence, although the party opposed to us has in the course of twenty years been caught spreading many and evil rumours about the Jesuits among the missionaries, nevertheless, after such an authoritative calumny had been issued from the Lord Patriarch’s boat against Fr. Monteiro and his companions, it was as though the battle trumpet had sounded; there fell upon us a hail of malevolent humour, insults, and fabrications. In one’s worst nightmares one would not find a single obscenity which they, even those who had once said they were our friends, were not ashamed to hurl at the Jesuits in Beijing or throughout the provinces. I will give some examples in June and afterwards. But here, as it were in parentheses, I am compelled to show how strong a presumption there should be that such malevolent intrigue is utterly alien to a person like Fr. José Monteiro, the Vice-Provincial.

### 15. How unjustly Father Monteiro was accused before the Patriarch

In the past, the Lord Bishop of Conon, Charles Maigrot, the Lord Bishop of Rosalia, de Lionne, and their allies ensnared Fr. Monteiro in a most serious web of allegations. On many occasions he was brought before a court, yet both in the Roman Tribunal and the court of public opinion his innocence was everywhere established so far beyond doubt that not only did he avoid any Roman censure, but he was also named Vice-Provincial. His innocence was subjected to the Tribunal’s inquiry and emerged to shine all the more brightly, lending a still greater splendour to his other virtues. He won such favour in the eyes of His Majesty King Pedro of Portugal that he nominated him Bishop of Nanjing, as a successor to His Lordship Alessandro Ciceri, a man who by blood, by reputation, and by all the good that he did on behalf of the faith was quite outstanding.

### 21. The building of a magnificent house is begun in Guangzhou by order of the Patriarch

Note 1: By order of the Lord Patriarch and, it is said, using money belonging to the Sacred Congregation, Fr. Giampè29 had begun to build a magnificent house. He had already completed the first section of it, a splendid building on two floors, on the outside of which he had built broad balconies in which | one can stroll with pleasure and drink in the more serene atmosphere. Building in this style is completely unknown in this country, and was felt by <!-- PDF page 489 | book page 479 --> the neighbours to be an intolerable nuisance, in that the wide roaming eyes of Europeans would compel the female members of the neighbouring families to be confined indoors as if in a prison. The women were extremely upset by this, since the strictness of Chinese custom already prevented them from going outside the house, and now in addition they were losing their freedom to enjoy the cool peacefulness of the heavens in the early mornings and the evenings within the privacy of their own courtyards, lightly clad as the extremely hot climate demands, without being exposed to the gaze of the Europeans, not to mention the jealousy of their husbands. This, then, is the palace which Fr. Monteiro mentions in the letter given above, in which, as he also says, the Lord Patriarch at that point was still staying, unmolested. The citizens had long ago complained to the mandarins about those buildings, which violated the height restrictions of Chinese law. But they so far had got nowhere as long as the Prefects knew that the Lord Patriarch was being showered with favours by the Emperor. Even now they are still waiting for the end of the affair so that they can satisfy the people’s demands.

### 46. The Emperor orders that the method of preaching the Gospel previously adopted by Father Matteo Ricci should be observed

When I, the Emperor, arrived in Yangzhou, a number of Europeans who were present there did not come to me to receive our residence permit. They said that although they wanted to receive a permit they would not nevertheless affirm in writing that they would follow Li Madou (Father Ricci). When they were asked the reason for this, they replied that a decree to this effect had been promulgated among the Europeans by Duoluo (the Lord Patriarch) to the effect that if they followed the rule of Yan Dang (the Bishop of Conon) they could continue still to preach the Law, that is, to be missionaries. But if they were to follow the teaching of Li Madou, they would be able only to live as Religious and in addition they would be consigned to the earthly prison or Hell. So it would be inconsistent for them to apply for the permit. Bo Jin (Father Bouvet) also changed sides and speaks in favour of them. Bo Jin will perhaps write of these things, and if he should write, his letter is to be considered worthless.

In the light of these facts, I, the Emperor, have reached a decision which I put in the following decree:

If Europeans persevere in following the teaching of Li Madou, they will still be able, under my protection, to preach the Law; if however they follow the rule <!-- PDF page 510 | book page 500 | manuscript page 632 --> of Yan Dang, I will regard these my subjects of the Middle Kingdom as rebels, and I declare that I will absolutely prohibit the practice of their Law.

When they heard this, the Europeans who were present in Yangzhou went away to Guangzhou, saying they would wait there for Bo Xianshi (Father Beauvollier) and Long Anguo (Father Barros); they are to make ready for their journey without delay.[^p510-71]

The gist of it was as follows:

### 49. The Christians are deeply worried by the Lord Patriarch’s decree

It finally became obvious to us that, in the light of this decree of the Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lord Patriarch of Antioch, in various places in this province (about other provinces we who live here have not yet heard anything), and especially in the missions of the cities of Songjiang, Shanghai, etc., the Christians have been so upset that not a few of them have despaired completely, and seem for this reason to have now abandoned the Faith. ### 50. The reasons why the Beijing Jesuits are forced to lodge an appeal

On the basis of the evidence presented here: 1. 2. Indeed, on this point as on many others, he has opposed the missionaries of the Society, and in the meanwhile has imposed on some of our men appalling penalties quite incompatible with the dignity of Christian men.

3. It appears that, since it would already have been very dangerous for Christians to take no part in the Rites which the decree of the Most Illustrious and

### 54. Why and under what conditions the Fathers might swear to observe the Lord Patriarch’s decree

The reasons why we might not swear to observe the command of His Excellency without some reservations are the following, among others:

1. It was proposed to us as a Rule and Certain Law. But there appeared to be some doubt what kind of certainty was involved here. For there was doubt whether everything was being presented as a certain truth of faith, or just as something which had to be adhered to in practice. Was it to be adhered to in this way, so that nothing in its provisions could be departed from even when there might be danger to one’s own life or that of another? The reason for doubt on the one hand was that His Excellency seemed to be giving the command as his own, when he says we propose, lay down, and decree; on the other hand, it is prescribed under pain of excommunication that it is to be held, taught, and used in replies and that, irrespective of any serious danger.

2. We read that these provisions were decreed by His Excellency adhering to the mind and decision of the Holy and Apostolic See, etc. However, even if we believed that this was so, or that His Excellency in so decreeing was adhering to the mind and decision of the Supreme Pontiff, with such a faith as befits his clearly enormous authority, there nevertheless seemed to be a doubt whether that mind so corresponded to that of His Excellency that we were obliged to give our assent to it under oath.

3. Again, we read: If they are then asked what is there in Divine Law which is not in conformity with the teachings of the Chinese, they are to reply that there are several such things; if they are asked to cite instances, they may mention in reply anything which occurs to them: the drawing of lots, the sacrifices or Ji which are made to Heaven, the Earth, the Sun, etc. But it seemed to us firstly that if there were such an interrogation as laid down here, we would not be able to reply in the words in which the reply is expressed. For there is no mention in the Divine Law of lots, or of commemorative sacrifices. This observation may in itself be of little moment, but from this along with other conclusions which have already been drawn or could be drawn it seemed impossible that His Excellency’s mind could have been that we should subscribe to each and every one of the replies as laid down in his command, with an oath so absolute that any qualification was completely excluded, even the one we urged above: namely, to what extent and in what way are these in conformity with the decree of the Supreme Pontiff whose publication he mentions?[^p517-81] Secondly, there seemed some doubt as to <!-- PDF page 518 | book page 508 | manuscript page 638 --> what His Excellency intended by the words Chinese doctrine. If this were to be taken to include any teaching to be found in the Chinese Empire, who could possibly grasp what that might include? Or is it a question only of the teaching contained in the canonical books of the Chinese as it appears in them?

4. Moreover, we read: If they are further asked about the Ji or sacrifices for Confucius and the ancestors they are to reply in the negative: we cannot perform them because they are not permitted to those who worship the true God. Firstly, it seemed doubtful whether it was the mind of His Excellency that we had to make that reply as it stands. The reason for doubting was that we could quite freely say that we could never perform Ji in connection with Confucius, or in connection with the ancestors, since it is not our business to perform such rites. Secondly, this raised a further doubt about the connection being made between the Chinese word Ji and the Latin word sacrificia | by using the connective seu. Are we to understand that the intention was to condemn the ceremonies called Ji on the assumption that they are sacrifices? Was the intention to declare that the ceremonies in connection with Confucius and the ancestors are truly and formally sacrifices? The reason for doubt was among other things that the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch himself had said to credible witnesses that he did not have any strong objections to supposing that the Chinese in their cult of Confucius were considering him simply as a Master. Again, he said that in his opinion only those ceremonies described as Ji which go wrong by excess should be forbidden. Thirdly, even assuming that in using the words Ji or sacrifices for Confucius and the ancestors the intention was to declare that these ceremonies were truly and formally sacrifices, doubt remained whether it was being proposed as a matter of faith or only as a view which one was not allowed to dispute when teaching. On the assumption that the ceremonies called Ji in honour of Confucius and the ancestors were forbidden to Christians but were not declared to be idolatrous or superstitious, there still remained a doubt whether the prohibition was to be understood as stating that a Christian could never engage in them nor be present at them no matter what assurances he gave, or whether they could never be present even if there were any threat of imminent danger. Fourthly, it seemed doubtful whether among those so described were to be included every ceremony for the ancestors described as Ji; the reason for doubt being that we nowhere saw any prohibition on those ceremonies in honour of the ancestors described as Diao,[^p518-82] though such ceremonies not infrequently include elements of the ones called Ji.

<!-- PDF page 519 | book page 509 | manuscript page 639 -->

### 55. They continue to explain the reasons for their appeal, etc

5. Since we read also negatively with regard to the use of tablets in the Chinese fashion, firstly, there was a doubt about how the words in the Chinese fashion are to be understood. Was a Chinese person supposed to inform us to what extent something was or was not prohibited by law? Or did it refer to any custom in use in China? Was the word fashion intended to include just the exterior shape of the tablet, or also the inscription on it? Did it include only what was intended by the Chinese, or by all of them? Secondly, we did not see that there was any prohibition on the use of a tablet for Confucius, yet we knew its shape and inscription was similar to the others; nor did there seem to be any absolute prohibition on the use of the tablets for ancestors. We therefore were caught in two minds as to whether, and if so to what extent, it was prohibited to use the tablets of the ancestors which were required by the laws of the empire.

6. Because we read: Also they will respond in the negative if they are asked whether Shangdi or Tian is the true God of the Christians, there seemed to be a doubt whether all that was prohibited was the general and confused statement “Shangdi or Tian is the true God of the Christians,” or whether it also prohibited the missionaries and the Christians to ever refer to the true God by using the words Tian and Shangdi, even when they are suitably defined and explained? Also, is it prohibited to say or to take the view that there is a basis in Chinese books that it is the true God of the Christians who is referred to in various texts by the words Tian and Shangdi? And finally, is the requirement also that we should respond negatively to the Emperor or anyone else who asks us, using the ordinary definitions of the true God—which exclude from Him all imperfection—and also the words Tian and Shangdi, whether this is not the true God of the Christians? | There was a further doubt whether in the preceding words it was intended to condemn the inscription Jing Tian, of which we saw no direct mention. And if it is assumed that it is intended to prohibit its use, there remained a doubt as to whether it was being condemned on the grounds of atheism or of idolatry, as our accusers maintain.

### 56. Some obscurities and uncertainties and so on in the Lord Patriarch’s decree

7. Because we read: If they are asked why they hold these opinions about the matters just mentioned, they will reply that they are incompatible with the worship of the true God, and that it has been so determined by the Holy See which is the infallible rule in matters of faith for Christians. The doubt was firstly whether the meaning was that each and every statement contained in the earlier replies had been defined by the Holy See, and to each and every one of them one was required to assent on the grounds that it had been so defined by the Holy See; secondly, because there is an express reference to the infallibility <!-- PDF page 520 | book page 510 | manuscript page 640 --> of the Supreme Pontiff in matters of faith, the doubt was whether each and every one of these statements was being proposed so as to be believed as a matter of faith.

8. Because we read: And finally if someone were to say “How do you come to know that?” We know it from the declaration made by the Patriarch of Antioch, our Superior, who, by virtue of his faculties, has within himself the oracle of the Supreme Pontiff. The doubt was, firstly, whether we were being required to believe as a matter of faith under pain of excommunication that this declaration and command of His Excellency in respect of each and every detail adhered to the mind of the Holy Apostolic See and the decision of the Supreme Pontiff, which was said to have been published; secondly, there was also a doubt arising from the meaning of the words has within himself the oracle of the Supreme Pontiff by virtue of his faculties. Although the oracle of the Supreme Pontiff is an infallible oracle of the truth, we did not sufficiently understand in what sense His Excellency by virtue of his faculties, has within himself the oracle of the Supreme Pontiff.

9. Again, we read: Also withdrawn is any faculty to avoid or otherwise interpret this our command under the pretext of the decree of Alexander VII, of holy memory, Pope, given in 1656, or of any great danger. Furthermore, by special indult, the interpretation and declaration of Apostolic Constitutions is entrusted to us, and so we declare that despite that decree or any great danger, anyone … etc. We considered these statements seriously, and a serious doubt arose from each part: His Excellency seemed to arm his command against those who in virtue of the decree of Alexander VII of 1656 continue to maintain the use of the Chinese Rites. It seemed possible to argue that these rites were not condemned as superstitious or idolatrous by the decree of the Supreme Pontiff Clement XI, which His Excellency claims was issued regarding these rites on 20 November 1704. Had the Supreme Pontiff Clement XI defined that the rites were idolatrous or superstitious, there would have been no need for His Excellency | to have recourse to a special indult. But he did have such recourse; hence, we drew the conclusion that the rites were not condemned as superstitious. Secondly, there was also a reason for drawing that opposite conclusion, for the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch claims that his command applies to us. But unless, we say, the decree of the Supreme Pontiff does not define the rites as idolatrous or superstitious, the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch would not have to argue that his command was to be obeyed by everyone in all respects, even in the face of imminent danger, under pain of automatic excommunication; but apparently His Excellency does so argue, hence it appeared that we could conclude that the Rites are condemned as superstitious. We were stuck between the two sides of the doubt thus set out.

<!-- PDF page 521 | book page 511 -->

### 57. The hesitation felt by the Fathers about subscribing to the Lord Patriarch’s decree

These, then, are the most important points which seemed to us to be insufficiently clear in His Excellency’s command, and whose meaning we did not seem able to determine sufficiently for us to be able to subscribe to it under oath without restriction. Since we were not able to do so without any restriction, we did make one, which we judged to be more consonant with the dictate of our own conscience, with reverence to the Holy See, and with His Excellency’s mind. That is to say, His Excellency states that this command has been published by him in accordance with the mind of the Holy and Apostolic See and the Decision of the Supreme Pontiff, etc. We therefore could not be closer to the mind of the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch than by subscribing to His Excellency’s command even under oath, insofar and to the extent that it conforms to the decree which the same Most Excellent Lord Patriarch claims was published by the Pope on 20 November 1704.
