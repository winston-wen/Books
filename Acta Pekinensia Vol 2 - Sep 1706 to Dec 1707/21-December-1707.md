### 1. The various grades and names of Chinese credentials[^p772-1]

On December 1st Father Agostino Barelli of our Society writing from Hangzhou informed our people in Beijing of a series of accusations made against him and of their happy resolution, which God our Lord had in his kindly providence arranged for him even although he was away from home. The precise charge was that the Father, with his imperfect knowledge of Chinese characters, had allowed the words zhiwen[^p772-2] to be inserted by the Christians in a certain document[^p772-3] in order that by those words the authority of the credentials which had been granted to him might be enhanced. The governor of the city of Ningbo brought this charge against the Father as though it were one of criminal abuse.

For the basis of this charge to be properly understood in Europe one has to know that in China credentials are granted by authorities of different standing, by different individuals, and for different purposes; also, in accordance with the usual over-complicated customs of the empire, they are given different titles so that the standing of the document can be recognised from the name itself. Thus the warrant which is given to mandarins as they are about to set off through the provinces to take up their magistracy is called a zhiwen, which means that they have been created governors and are setting out to govern; those which are granted by the Ministry of Rites are properly called buwen;[^p772-4] and those granted <!-- PDF page 773 | book page 763 --> to ordinary people are called xinpiao[^p773-5] and zhang,[^p773-6] or permits from such and such a bureau.

Some European missionaries called their recently granted residence permit by the rather grand name long piao,[^p773-7] “a dragon (or imperial) permit,” which strictly speaking it was not since rather than bearing the Emperor’s seal it had the seal of the Marshal of the Royal Household. As a result, just as the Europeans were using the more honorific name for their permit to further their own interest and that of the Christian faith, in the same way the more jealous gentile mandarins were trying to play the permit down and, in Ningbo and many other places as well, wanted to insist that the only title by which it could be called was neiwufu de zhang,[^p773-8] “a sealed document from the Inner City” (i.e. from the Palace). But they were unable by these means to turn the fact that the Europeans were labelling their permit “dragon” into a damaging accusation. The term is rather broad, and in ordinary use is applied to anything which is obtained by favour of the Emperor; but this is not the case with the words zhiwen, which is used of a document or imperial credential for governors and is specifically used for the credentials of mandarins. What follows is the account Fr. Barelli gives of his case:

### 2. The charge and matters alleged against Father Agostino Barelli

Reverend Father in Christ, Father Kilian,

In the end what I feared from the Governor of the city of Ningbo happened while I was away. On the 29th of October he wrote his indictment and, on the following day it seems, sent it to my friend the mandarin, the buzheng shi.[^p773-9] On the 31st of October one of the buzheng shi’s servants arrived, saying that he had been sent by that mandarin to see what grounds I might have for remaining in China. On 1 November Fr. Contancin, who was looking after the house in my absence, went to the buzheng shi’s tribunal. Since the buzheng shi knew he was coming, he sent a servant to fetch the Governor of the city of Ningbo who, however, did not come. Since Fr. Contancin had been waiting at the door for three hours, a servant was sent to say that he should come back again in three or four days. Meanwhile on 4 November the buzheng shi sent an order to the mandarin Ren Hexian[^p773-10] to investigate <!-- PDF page 774 | book page 764 | manuscript page 808 --> the matter without delay. On November 5th two members of the mandarin Ren Hexian’s staff came to the church, bringing with them the following document:

“In accordance with an order of the mandarin, the buzheng shi, which has been communicated to the mandarin Ren Hexian, Ai[^p774-11] (Fr. Barelli) is cited on the grounds that he is alleged to have said in a petition that Guo[^p774-12] (Father Gollet) had a zhiwen or order of the Emperor. For his part, Guo admits that he has only a xinpiao testimonial. Ai therefore lied when he said that Guo had a zhiwen. Since this affair is | the Emperor’s business, I cannot treat it lightly or fail to get to the bottom of it. Accordingly Ai has been ordered to bring to us with all speed the object which he called a zhiwen and which Guo called a xinpiao, which Guo said that he had left in Ai’s church. It is to be thoroughly examined. There must be no delay. We cannot afford to put it off. Speed is of the essence. 10th month, 12th day.”[^p774-13]

### 3. The affair of Fr. Barelli has a happy ending

On that same day, Fr. Contancin went at once to see the buzheng shi, who received him most warmly. He showed him our testimonial, which the mandarin praised very highly and approved. He went on to tell the Father how he should reply to the mandarin Ren Hexian: he should say that the mandarin here had seen it, had examined our grounds for remaining in China, and had commanded that he not be troubled any further on the matter. On the 6th, Fr. Contancin sent a servant with his tiezi (visiting card) to the mandarin Ren Hexian who was to convey to him the buzheng shi’s precise words. And so it all ended happily, thank God, before I could get back home.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.[^p774-14]

The reader should know that in connection with this accusation the buzheng shi (the Imperial Commissioner) also must bear part of the responsibility in that he admitted Fr. Barelli’s petition as evidence in the case of Fr. Gollet. He sent it to the Governor of Ningbo, concealing the fact that in that petition the Europeans’ residence permit is described as a zhiwen. And so he prejudiced the finding by a Chinese stratagem in such a way as not merely to extricate the Father, but also himself, which he was able to do since he outranked the official <!-- PDF page 775 | book page 765 --> who had brought the accusation. The reader should please recall the case of the Most Rev. Fr. Mezzafalce, who in the letters patent concerning the death of the Most Rev. Fr. Alcala had used the character zhi, which is reserved for the Emperor’s use alone and abuse of which is an act of treason.[^p775-15] Had the magistrate not already been guilty of some negligence by covering up the Father’s use of the characters zhiwen in the petition, he would not have let him off so lightly, friend though he was. But what might some other enemy have done had the Reverend Mezzafalce been brought to his court charged with a much more serious offence?

On December 2nd the Emperor, giving us hardly any prior warning, ordered another Father, a mathematician by the name of Kong,[^p775-16] to be brought from Macau. He had heard about him, as he had about Father De,[^p775-17] on the recommendation of the Viceroy.[^p775-18] Since the question had been raised as to which of the two had made more progress in the science of mathematics, inquiry was made of Fr. Castner, but he simply sat on the fence. However, the Emperor suddenly decided that both should be summoned, saying that such a choice will be the least upsetting for us.

### 4. Two Fathers are summoned to Beijing by the Emperor

On December 5th, the above-mentioned order concerning the summoning of Fathers Kong (Gonzaga) and De (Romain Hinderer) along with Wei (Brother Miguel Vieira)[^p775-19] was given to a brother of the Viceroy who lived in the capital.

Meanwhile, as had been originally intended, more frequent reference was made to making arrangements for the examination of Father Castner.[^p775-20] It had <!-- PDF page 776 | book page 766 | manuscript page 809 --> been postponed on the grounds that the Emperor, after a long delay in Tartary, would now that he was home be occupied with many pieces of urgent business; during those days he had the Father kept busy by the Prince in setting up a lathe[^p776-21] properly, and other such tasks.

### 5. A searching examination of their learning is arranged[^p776-22]

On [December] 12th they proceeded to the examination, which really is far more searching than would be customary among European academics for a degree in the sciences. The questions and all the answers were shown in writing to His Majesty. He was not at first satisfied and did not accept the answers originally proposed. | On the subsequent days he examined more and more difficult matters one after another, even bringing in witnesses both European and Chinese to assure himself that there had been no collusion in the examination process. The examination lasted for three days and for three more every single calculation mentioned in the papers was rehearsed in the presence of the Prince, who was endeavouring by this means to understand everything, going right back to basic principles so that he might examine future candidates himself—not to mention getting one up on the Europeans.

### 6. The Bishop of Beijing raises matters of some importance with the Beijing Jesuits

On December 14th Fr. Girolamo Franchi, SJ, a missionary in the city of Ji’nan[^p776-23] in the province of Shandong wrote from Weixian[^p776-24] to say that on his way to the works of the mission in Weixian he had turned aside to meet the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing. From him he received some points to be communicated to the Jesuits in Beijing, which the Most Illustrious Lord himself had agreed should be passed on. These were: 1. The Most Reverend da Leonissa, Lord Bishop of Beirut, had written to His Illustrious Lordship to say that what he had done in Rome concerning the controversies he had done because he believed it to be right and would serve the Society. In fact, any more energetic effort and a multiplicity of testimonials would have harmed the Society.[^p777-25] <!-- PDF page 777 | book page 767 --> 2. After the decree of the Emperor against Rev. Fr. Hervé & co. a book had appeared in the South in which the use of the word Shangdi is defended. This book is attributed by others to Fr. Barelli or to some other member of the Society of Jesus. 3. Some people suspect that the prompt obedience of the Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial José Monteiro was given to the Lord Patriarch’s decree not with sincerity of intention but from a calculated and shrewd cunning. 4. Several people are of the opinion that the difficulties put in the way of missionaries from Guangdong Province coming to Beijing to receive their permits were secretly arranged by the Beijing Fathers themselves. 5. The Lord Patriarch has written some bitter words in reply to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing because of the promise he had made in accepting the diploma, and had gone on to threaten that he would be compelled by even more bitter words to withdraw the permission, etc.

### 7. The Jesuits reply to each point

The reader needs to take notice of the very short reply of the Jesuits in Beijing to these points. First and foremost, they are very grateful to the Most Illustrious Bishop for his kind friendliness in getting in touch with them. To 1. they reply that they continue to have confidence in the writer, in line with the books which he has published in Rome in recent years.[^p777-26] To 2., that a reply had already been made under July, and on this point Fr. Kilian later wrote to the Most Illustrious Reverend Bishop’s secretary in January 1708; as early as that, the Reverend Father Secretary revealed to the Lord Bishop that it had been established that the author of the said book was Fr.

<!-- PDF page 778 | book page 768 | manuscript page 810 -->

Barelli, according to a letter of Rev. Fr. Frossoloni himself. To 3., account must be taken of what was said during the months of April and May. Really, what are the Jesuits supposed to do? The Illustrious Bishop of Beijing himself together with the Patriarch’s party want the Jesuits to offer blind obedience; if the Jesuits point out the dangers, they consider them rebellious to His Holiness in Rome and suspect in faith. And if they obey? Then they condemn them as malicious on account of the bad consequences, as though knowing the dangers to the flock and the shepherd they had acted deliberately. What actually happened was this: some of the Jesuits took the oath to observe the regulation, and keep it they did, repudiating the residence permit at the cost of consequent harm to themselves and the mission. Others took the oath but appealed against it on the grounds of the serious consequences which would harm the mission. Others neither took the oath nor appealed against it. They simply made representations about what would happen if they took it, knowing that even if the Lord Patriarch had more clearly seen the dangers, he would still prefer the mission to perish rather than take a single syllable out of his published regulation. So they would indeed obey every syllable of it, and all of them along with him would either leave China or have themselves thrown out. If they would condemn every Jesuit who took any of these three courses of action, they must produce some fourth way—if indeed there is one—of which they would approve. | That will then be the one the Jesuits would decide to follow in future. To 4., it is enough to read the letters of Fr. de Britto of October 4th, of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon of October 24th and November 11th, and again that of Fr. Hortiz of the 12th of the same month. Fr. Kilian sent Rev. Fr. Castorano a complete transcript of these letters following the Bishop’s admonition, together with his letter to Fr. Soares mentioned above, expressly requesting him to bring them to the attention of the Most Illustrious Bishop as he had asked so that he could read them. In Shandong Province to harbour the worst suspicions of the Jesuits, especially of those in Beijing, is regarded as a God-given right! To 5., they should refer to what was said at length under June of this year about the reason why the Most Illustrious Bishop, wishing to satisfy the Lord Patriarch, interpreted the promise he had made to the Emperor in a broad sense and promised further satisfaction, and in addition had taken a detailed oath to observe the regulation, even those provisions in the regulation which no Catholic would seem in a position validly to swear to unless they had in advance been explained in a Catholic sense by the Lord Patriarch himself. In a letter to the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Ascalon dated June 1708, the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing explicitly claimed that doing this turned him from one who had fallen through fear into a man of principle. For the moment, that is enough on this topic.

### 8. Fr. Appiani is brought back to Beijing: various apprehensions as a result

On [December] 15th Fr. Domenge, SJ from Shaanxi Province, told us that he had left Xi’an fu[^p779-27] in company with our Very Rev. Fr. Pro-Vicar[^p779-28] to look after the mission there. On the way he came across Rev. Fr. Appiani, who was returning to Beijing from Sichuan at the order of the Emperor. He had a guard of five men, who were kind and caused him as little inconvenience as possible; they gave him permission to talk to both of them. He had questioned Fr. Castrocaro and learned a lot about the present position with regard to the Lord Patriarch and his decree and about the wretched condition of the missions as a result of the decree. The people from Beijing were stunned by this news, knowing nothing and worried about what would happen next. For, although they had previously known that reports about the aforementioned gentleman had been brought from Sichuan to the Emperor, there was absolutely no information about what His Majesty had said in reply. For the rest, the Very Rev. Pro-Vicar Castrocaro[^p779-29] wrote that Rev. Fr. Appiani was in good shape during his journey. It is quite clear that this letter was written in this month of December to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing, who refers to it in a letter of February 1708 which perhaps we will give in full later.[^p779-30] This is what he says to Fr. Bouvet: “I had news from Xi’an fu[^p779-31] in December. Fr. Pro-Vicar informs me that he met Signor Appiani 30 li outside Xi’an fu. He was being honourably conducted to the Court by order of the Emperor. He is well and happy, and is travelling with every convenience. I found this out on the last day of 1707. On the basis of that information I believed that he would be found innocent.”[^p779-32] Thus it is that human affairs never have just one face; according as one looks at them from different angles, they have one aspect after another, like the phases of the moon. In Linqing and elsewhere there were already omens that his return <!-- PDF page 780 | book page 770 | manuscript page 811 --> would be a happy one. But in Beijing it was not just a single shock that was bringing the Fathers out in a cold sweat; they had observed that there was still the same severity on the countenance of the Emperor whenever and wherever anything came up concerning the affairs of the Lord Patriarch and his companions.

On [December] 16th there was a strong rumour that the Emperor had indicated to the Prince that Fr. Appiani, whom he had summoned back, would be arriving at any moment.

### 9. He is committed to the prison for criminals

December 17th. In the morning while Frs. Parrenin and Kilian were standing at the gate of the Royal Garden waiting to be admitted, a young lad from the Prince’s household who was rushing past leapt from his horse and, taking Fr. Parrenin a little aside, inquired whether he had been informed where and when Fr. Appiani had arrived: | his master had sent him to go round making inquiries. Fr. Parrenin replied that he had absolutely no idea. However, Fr. Appiani did arrive at the capital on the evening of that day without the Jesuits in the city and the others who were a good distance away in the imperial gardens knowing anything about it. Fr. Appiani was immediately placed in the prison for common criminals. On the 18th preparations were being made for the Emperor’s return to the city, and into it on the 19th he did come for the fast day prior to the offering of sacrifice to the Lord of Heaven and Earth for the winter solstice. On that same day, the 19th, while we were having dinner in our house within the Saffron Wall,[^p780-33] one of the officials from the Ministry of Justice (or xingbu[^p780-34]) turned up out of the blue. He presented a sheet of paper torn from a breviary or some other book on which Fr. Appiani, without having addressed it to any individual in the community or to the community in general, had written as follows:

### 10. From there he writes to the Fathers asking them for urgent help

“I arrived on the 17th of this month and I am being held in the prison attached to this criminal court in very difficult conditions. Were I able to get some private place where I could spend some time in prayer, that would be a great comfort to me, but if not, may God’s will be done. I have nobody to bring me meals. If you were to send someone here who could make a deal with these fellows to <!-- PDF page 781 | book page 771 --> satisfy their greed for money, you would be doing a prisoner a great favour. I would like to know whether Fr. Müllener[^p781-35] along with Frs. Basset and la Baluère had sufficient funds to pay for their return to Sichuan Province. Above all I desire that God’s will be done, and I commend myself to your prayers. I promised three hundred copper pieces to the man who brought you this letter. Do not give money to anyone else. I have no more to say other than that I kiss your sacred hands. Farewell, Reverend Fathers! Your most humble servant, Appiani.

If someone could be sent here along with this messenger, that for me would be most welcome.”

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, of the Society of Jesus, Notary Apostolic.

The reader must excuse Fr. Appiani for writing somewhat peremptorily, demanding rather than requesting; the smallness of the page left no room for the less essential polite turns of phrase, and from his long stay with the Jesuits in 1706 he had become very familiar with them, with perhaps a certain sense of being above shades of meaning. He was asking for common necessities, apart from what he said about Rev. Frs. Müllener, Basset, and Baluère. He must have known that they had gone to Guangzhou to the Lord Patriarch and not to the capital and the Emperor, and indeed that Fr. Basset had gone with the intention of setting out for Europe and to that end had brought with him a group of boys from Sichuan to be educated either in Rome at the College of the Sacred Congregation or in Paris at the Rue du Bac seminary.[^p781-36]

### 11. The Fathers assist the struggling man

To help the poor imprisoned man, after handing over the 300 copper pieces we sent a domestic servant who found the man in question without chains but in a group of sixty prisoners. He gave him what he could and asked what more he would like. In reply he asked whether the Fathers might obtain for him, in the many enclosures of the prison, somewhere private for himself. This was settled on December 20th. The Head <!-- PDF page 782 | book page 772 | manuscript page 812 --> of the Bureau of Prisons decreed that it was not the custom to rent out the little apartments in the court for some days or even for a month but to hire them—or more accurately offer them for sale—for an entire year, each cell being ten feet long and the cost being 25 Chinese ounces of silver (the equivalent of Roman scudi); | none of the payment was to be returnable in the case that the lessor or the lessee was released within the year, but neither could the person be evicted if his case had been quickly concluded but he wished to stay for the remaining months of the year. It did not seem sensible to hire a squalid hut for so much money on the spot, both because there was no way of telling for what reason Fr. Appiani had been summoned back nor whether he would have to stay there for some time, and because it could readily be foreseen that however the affair turned out in the end this reverend gentleman would have many needs to meet for which a rather short-term act of benevolence on our part would be insufficient. We therefore focussed our concern on making sure that food and the help of a servant would be provided; meanwhile with fear and trembling we awaited whatever the Emperor might assault our ears with.

### 12. The Fathers are afraid that the decision about Fr. Appiani might be something harsher

That same day, the 20th, was the day of the general punishment; the practice was that once a year hordes of convicted persons were slaughtered outside the gate. On that count too our fear was increased, for although we did not think that death would be inflicted on Fr. Appiani, given that he was allowed to walk among the chained up prisoners without being chained himself, our minds could not be entirely at peace knowing how inscrutable is the heart of kings. So when we presented ourselves in the audience hall and noticed the complete silence, and that still nobody had mentioned Fr. Appiani although four days had passed since he had been imprisoned, it seemed wiser that we should start talking about him with a view to finding out whether anything worse was going to happen to him that day and whether there was any way left by which we could help him. First we tried an approach to Zhao Chang, who was in charge of operations in the Yangxin dian. He said that so far he had not been informed of the man’s arrival either by the Prince or the Emperor. This astonished us, but the same thing was confirmed back home shortly afterwards by the servant who returned from the prison and stated that he had been told by a Christian mandarin, a captive in the same place, that the prefects of the Ministry had not yet warned the Emperor about Fr. Appiani since during the three days of the fast it was contrary to the custom of the country and of the courts to make any announcement about any new criminal cases or the arrival of old criminals at the Court. Nevertheless we thought we ought to say something and

<!-- PDF page 783 | book page 773 | manuscript page 813 -->

Fr. Parrenin informed the Prince, to whom ten of our Jesuits in a long line on their knees began to make their petition as he was going up to the Emperor. The Prince stopped for a moment, but as soon as he heard Fr. Appiani’s name he said with a smile, Nage nimen buguan,[^p783-37] “That is none of your business,” and took off out of their sight.

### 13. He is transferred to the Palace Prison for Offenders

When the Emperor found out from the Prince that the Fr. Appiani was there he gave orders for him to be transferred from the xingbu to the shenjin si,[^p783-38] which is the palace court for offenders. From there Fr. Appiani himself wrote to us on December 22nd as follows:

### 14. The letter of Fr. Appiani to the Fathers

Reverend Fathers,

December 22nd.

Towards evening yesterday it was ordered by the Prince that I should be taken at dawn to the tixing jingli tribunal[^p783-39] and so in fact I was taken there, loaded in a cart. I was held close to the gate outside the tribunal until the afternoon waiting for the Prince to come. Meanwhile I saw Fr. Parrenin, Br. Rodes, and Fr. Jartoux going past again and again. In the afternoon the people in charge of the xingbu Tribunal brought an order whereby I am commanded to be taken at the third watch tomorrow night to Changchun yuan[^p783-40] where it is said that the Emperor in person is going to see me. I simply cannot fathom this enigma. Since I see that nobody is coming here, I am writing to advise you that I have paid 200 copper pieces to hire today’s cart and that I will need still more help tomorrow: the attendants want to be fed. However, it really is intended | for me to be taken by cart right from the tribunal itself to where I am supposed to go. If you would like to give me any advice about the manner in which I should conduct myself in my role in this comedy—or rather, tragedy—insofar as it depends on me, I will try to take your advice, following it in every appropriate <!-- PDF page 784 | book page 774 --> way. I asked Charles (he is our domestic servant)[^p784-41] for a lined tunic, since the one I have on at the moment is badly torn. I most humbly kiss your hands, and pray that you will give the messenger two hundred copper pieces. Appiani.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Notary Apostolic.

### 15. Some comments on that note

Before I go any further, I offer the reader the following comments: 1. While Fr. Appiani was indeed commanded by an earlier order to be taken to the shenjin si Tribunal he was not in fact transferred there, since in virtue of a second order on the 23rd he was to be taken away to the Villa. 2. It was simply a mistake for Fr. Appiani to think that had heard that on the following day he would be admitted to the presence of His Majesty. Nobody who is in chains is permitted even to go up to the Emperor’s outermost door, let alone to enter his sight. 3. When the Jesuits read this note, on the one hand they took some comfort from the fact that Fr. Appiani was displaying courage and looking upon his plight as a comedy, but on the other, they were afraid that just as previously the Patriarch’s people were in the habit of describing the wretched succession of events as Jesuit-inspired drama, so too Fr. Appiani, failing to swallow his anger against the Jesuits sufficiently, would show himself unfairly resentful, not so much to explain himself as to score points against the Jesuits. 4. Since up to that point we were completely in the dark about the charge to be brought against him the next day, it was difficult to give him any advice on what it was best to do. Fr. Kilian decided that Charles should tell Fr. Appiani that we had no idea why he had been called to Beijing, nor why he was to be taken on the next day to the Villa, and that Fr. Kilian asked only that he should say little to the Prince and should answer with humility.

### 16. The value of Chinese copper coins

5. The reader should know that 100 copper coins, as they are in common circulation, are worth one tenth of a Chinese silver ounce, and 1,000 are worth one entire Chinese ounce, equivalent to one Roman scudo. I am not saying this somehow with the intention of at some point exaggerating our generosity to Fr. Appiani, but so that the Father Procurator should know his expenses already incurred and still to be incurred in the service of this Reverend Father, so they might be accurately recorded in published reports.

### 17. The charity and generosity of the Jesuits towards Fr. Navarrete

There is no other reason for this than that the Jesuits in Beijing have learned from the printed books of Rev. Fr. Navarrete, who by their charity was maintained like one of us for several years and who then wrote in such a disgraceful way, to believe that the benefits given to him became grounds for complaint against us, as something he was ashamed of and about which we should be embarrassed.[^p785-42]

On December 23rd Rev. Fr. Appiani was taken to the Villa but I do not know where he was first housed. Later however he was seen under the portico of the Emperor’s father-in-law’s country house, through which the Prince was to pass. He was bound with chains, which were removed before he was taken into the Prince’s garden.

Some of our Fathers were also commanded to be present at the Villa today. They went in the afternoon into the orchard, as is customary, but heard nothing worth noting about Rev. Fr. Appiani. Fr. Kilian was also in the same Villa assisting Brother Franz Stadlin, who was constructing clocks inside the Prince’s palace.[^p785-43] A summons with Fr. Kilian’s name on it was sent to the city by mistake but eventually reached him in the Villa after the fourth hour of the evening. The wording of the summons sounded more severe than usual, ordering him to come at once, since the Prince had a message from the Emperor addressed to him. The Father hastened there, making a detour to Fathers Parrenin and Jartoux, from whom he learnt that Fr. Appiani had been led into the Prince’s garden. | Fr. Kilian’s hair stood on end since he perceived that he alone had received a summons, and was afraid that he would be forced to be present at the interrogation of Rev. Fr. Appiani which was known to be scheduled for that day.

### 18. Fr. Kilian is summoned to the palace and grudgingly admitted …

Without much choice in the matter, he went to the Prince’s doors at almost five o’clock in the evening, and for the reason suggested above decided to stop there instead of asking to be announced to the Prince. This was both to show that he had obeyed the order in that he had come and to avoid causing inconvenience, which would certainly have been dangerous. Night was <!-- PDF page 786 | book page 776 --> falling and everyone was beginning to make for home. Suddenly there was a great commotion inside the garden: clubs were hastily brought in, strongarmed doorkeepers and kitchen and stable staff were summoned, and soon there was the sound of shattering blows being struck in pairs one after the other. Fr. Kilian, separated from what was going on only by a wall, could hardly help in such circumstances being afraid that the Prince had perhaps, carried away by one of his usual rages, ordered Fr. Appiani beaten. He regretted not having gone in earlier in the hope of rendering the poor man assistance in any way he could, so he tried to get in even unannounced. The guards barred his way; he showed them his summons; they said they couldn’t read; and as he was arguing with the guards about getting in, the storm of hundreds of blows stopped. There was not the slightest sound from the one beaten, since their mouth is usually gagged, and the very silence increased the Father’s terror. Eventually he was permitted to enter. Wandering alone along the winding paths of the garden in the last of the fading evening light, he reached a small house. When he got there, he knew from the sound of their voices close at hand that Zhao was there along with Charki, both of them mandarins; finding the door open, he then went in suddenly, intending to sit down with them on the ground even though in the dark nobody might recognize his face. As he went to sit down, Zhao Laoye stopped him, telling him to find somewhere else, and to be careful not to discuss the business of the Prince and meddle in other people’s affairs. These remarks did not sound good.

The Father at once made his way towards the Prince’s lodgings and announced his arrival by greeting the eunuchs. In the event, he was standing there until the Prince had dinner served at the seventh hour, and continued to stand there until the eighth. When the tables had been cleared away, the Prince admitted female singers through an interior door, and only the servants and eunuchs remained outside the room. As a result, the Father was not supposed to remain in the area any longer, but neither was it a good idea for him to leave, since the Prince in the summons had expressly told him that the word came from the Emperor; the anxiety of the waiting Father had been not a little heightened by hearing the hail of hundreds of blows, though whether or not they had fallen on Fr. Appiani he still did not know. What he did know was that the said Father had not yet left the Prince’s garden.

The eunuchs were whispering among themselves, wondering why a foreigner was wandering around at that hour rather than leaving. One of them, a young man of affable disposition, spoke to the father in the dark asking, if he could hear him, what the master’s intentions for him were now. The Father replied that he could hear and that he would prefer to leave were it not for <!-- PDF page 787 | book page 777 | manuscript page 815 --> the fact that he was constrained by the weight of the royal word; he thereupon showed him the summons. When the young man along with the others had read this in the light of a lantern which they brought up, they were afraid to send the Father away, and hid him in a small hall until they found the right moment to announce his presence.

### 19. … and is ordered to come back the next day

Half an hour later Zhao and Charki passed through the same place on their way to give the Prince the questions put to Fr. Appiani and his replies to them. These were taken both from the report of the Governor of Sichuan and from his confession today, which was written down and presented by the Prince to His Majesty on the following morning. The Father learnt all this while hiding in the dark, and was pleased to discover that Fr. Appiani was not the person who four hours earlier had suffered a severe fracture of the pelvis. Not long after this, the Father was told to go away and come back first thing the next morning. However, he made every effort to gain entrance to the house in which he now knew for certain that Fr. Appiani | had been interrogated by the above-mentioned mandarins. The night-guards however refused to allow this, so he left the garden and after him Fr. Appiani was dismissed. On Fr. Parrenin’s orders some of our servants were waiting at the gates of the garden to be ready for any eventuality, in case they discovered that Fr. Appiani needed anything for that night.

### 20. Why the Emperor was angry with Reverend Fr. Appiani

The Father had to go on ahead since he has already been prohibited from speaking to Fr. Appiani, who was handed back to the court officials without delay. These, however, had been well wined by our servants, and so allowed them a brief opportunity for conversation. Fr. Appiani said to our servants that there was nothing he needed; the mandarins were at that moment looking for somewhere where he could spend the night with them. The Fathers should stop being worried, as his case was not at all difficult: the Emperor had been offended on the grounds that a year earlier he had left with the Lord Patriarch without first asking the Emperor for permission to leave. The said Reverend Father had alleged the same explanation for his captivity to the same servants or ours in the year before, when he was being held in chains in the xingbu Tribunal. Whether this was the real reason he would soon find out.

### 21. Father Appiani is taken into our house

On December 24th, having seen the written account of the questions and the replies of Fr. Appiani, the Emperor to some extent listened to us as we begged that he be released from the public prison and held somewhere else. He ruled that he should be transferred to <!-- PDF page 788 | book page 778 | manuscript page 816 --> some one of our churches to be held under military guard, with the express warning that his trial was not to be considered over yet. To implement the sentence the Prince assigned for the Reverend Father the Church within the Saffron Wall, and there was no questioning this decision. However, the Fathers there did all they could to avoid the ignominy of a military guard. Fr. Parrenin suggested that soldiers were unnecessary: we already had a sergeant permanently guarding the glassblowing workshop, whose door was inside our own premises. This plan was accepted. We considered it not a bad compromise since in fact Father Appiani was being handed over by the Prefect of the shenjin si, who had presided over the previous day’s hearing, to the mandarins attached to the glassblower’s workshop inside our house, to be kept under observation.[^p788-44] He would be at the end of our south hall where he had a comfortable lodging, a warm room, and a chapel equipped with everything necessary. It happened, however, that we were still busy in the Villa around midday, when Fr. Appiani arrived at our house; at that very moment Signor Borghese, the doctor, was there too. He attended Fr. Appiani because of some long-standing complaint, since nobody there as yet knew about the Emperor’s prohibition against anyone visiting him; because of a similar ignorance of the royal edict Fr. Bouvet, as best he could on a fast-day and the Vigil of Christmas, refreshed Fr. Appiani in our community refectory.

On December 25th Fr. Kilian, coming in to wish him a happy feast, found Fr. Appiani setting things up at the altar. At the same time the soldiers asked to be allowed in, but the Father kept them out so that Fr. Appiani would have enough room to celebrate Mass comfortably. When Mass was over, there was a happy celebration of the birthday of Christ Our Lord, and the soldiers were given permission to do their own thing.

### 22. His surprising confidence

The Reverend Father’s confidence seemed extraordinary. He behaved in all circumstances as if he was oblivious to everything and was living in the middle of his own country. Despite the military guard and the interdict he behaved as if he were a free man, wishing to receive the visits of the Christians etc.; on the 27th of December he went so far as to invite the Father Superior to come and see him. When the Superior said | he was not free, he left his place of detention and went to the Superior!

<!-- PDF page 789 | book page 779 -->

### 23. Danger of a fire because of his negligence

Meanwhile, his bedroom caught fire, endangering our whole house. The Reverend Father excused his lapse by saying that he had been heating his Chinese bed[^p789-45] more than usual, and in addition he had asked for plenty of burning coals to be placed in his room since he had it in mind to prepare a bath to relieve his body, stiff with cold. Maybe he meant a sweat-room, for he had not got a drop of water ready—or perhaps he thought he could be cleaned by a dry bath! The Reverend Father had asked that through us he might get permission to return to his church, where he could live as a recluse at His Majesty’s pleasure. We were extremely eager to help him—and the mission—and thought it worth trying to see whether something of this kind might be arranged.

On December 28th therefore, the Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial José Soares, Fr. Parrenin the Superior of the house, and Father Kilian had an audience with the Prince in the Emperor’s garden requesting that Father Appiani’s desire might be met.

### 24. He is placed under closer guard

The Prince said, with a bitter laugh, “You have no idea what a reasonable request is. Didn’t my father make it clear that the case was not yet over? Yet here you are, making further requests! Well, it will get you nowhere. Do you want me to treat a man who has been accused and not yet acquitted as if he were owed a favour? Why don’t you ask me to invite him into my palace, give him a comfortable room, assign servants to be at his beck and call, and send my major-domo every day to ask respectfully what his good pleasure might be?” With these sarcasms he went off to the Emperor, and when he came back, he brought with him an order that Fr. Appiani should be transferred from our house to the adjoining premises of the glassblowing workshop, to be guarded by soldiers in such a manner that none of our Jesuits should be allowed to speak to him, nor should he have permission to write or to receive letters; the order was put into execution that very day. At this the Fathers and Fr. Appiani were distraught.

The year may be brought to a close by a letter from the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing written on December 27th, which I did not mention earlier because I did not want too quickly to lose the thread of the Appiani case.[^p789-46] This letter, addressed to Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial, went as follows:

<!-- PDF page 790 | book page 780 | manuscript page 817 -->

### 25. A letter from the Bishop of Beijing to Fr. Vice-Provincial

Very Reverend and dear Father in Christ, 27th. A few days ago I received two bundles of letters along with one from Your Reverence. I have already dispatched the letters which were addressed to individual Fathers, so if they are sent to them by the two silver flotillas, they can be delivered. So far as I myself am concerned, I would ask you to do me the favour of keeping my silver until there is some safe opportunity through your Fathers who are already coming to the Court by order of the Emperor,[^p790-47] since on their return journey they could pass along this canal.[^p790-48]

Very many thanks to the Fathers Castner and the others[^p790-49] for taking the trouble to carry my silver; if there is any way in which I can help them, let them write to me. As for the letters from Rome, I have received the ones of 1705 and 1706 from the Sacred Congregation, but they don’t say a word to me about the decree on the Chinese rites. The Bishop of Beirut[^p790-50] writes to say that the case is already over, but still the decision remains withheld from publication.[^p790-51] The following words came to me in writing from Guangzhou: “The absolution given by the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Goa was declared null in Rome and was censured[^p790-52] by the Lord Patriarch.” And from Pondicherry a person whom I trust wrote to me on the 30th of April this year to the contrary: “The Lord Primate also sent the decree of the Sacred Congregation wherein it approves the Pastoral with | which he annulled what the Very Reverend Lord Patriarch did on that coast.” So, I do not know how to reconcile these contradictions. Your Very Reverend Paternity who has had letters from Goa will have clearer information. If it is true that the Most Reverend Lord Primate had a decree from Rome confirming what he had done, that new letter would be very favourable for our <!-- PDF page 791 | book page 781 --> work. I have already told you in my previous letter[^p791-53] that I have replied to what Their Majesties[^p791-54] commanded me to do in their letters. Since I am not able to write in time this year, if we survive, I shall reply next year to the two previous letters as well as this most recent one sent to me.

I am relieved that His Majesty is pleased at the arrival of the three priests who are to serve him and I pray to God that his heart will be touched in favour of the others who are still under arrest. I have no news of Father Buonaventura da Roma; moreover, I hear it said that the Lord Patriarch is very angry with him. If you know for certain whether this is so, I would appreciate being told and also whether Fr. Simões[^p791-55] has incurred the same wrath. Here too we have had quite a few troubles since the Emperor passed by on his way to Beijing: first in registering our piaos and also, very often we have been visited by shuban[^p791-56] and chairen who have come to investigate Fr. Frossolone and two other missionaries. What is worse, the other day on December 17th we got a command from that zhizhou[^p791-57] that we had to appear in person at his audience and to bring our piaos so as to register them and have them examined, by orders of the libu. My companion went with them and they were registered very carefully. Further, the mandarin is beginning to suspect that there is an important disagreement between us Europeans and the Emperor. I don’t know what to do or to say. Now the matter is going through the tribunals and the chairen as they travel around demand to see our chops.[^p791-58] Fr. Francisco Palencia has many problems in Yizhou despite his piao. Two Fathers, Miguel Fernández and Martin Aleman have already left for Guangzhou at the order of their superior, to take care of their order’s houses in case they have to leave China. I can’t think of anything else.

That this agrees with the original, I so certify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Notary Apostolic.

<!-- PDF page 792 | book page 782 | manuscript page 818 -->

### 26. Services rendered by the Fathers of the Society towards the Franciscan Fathers

From the above letter it is clear that the Fathers of the Society, in order to respond in the way Christ taught us to the many wrongs perpetrated against them in this last year by the Franciscan Fathers, in no way relaxed their usual efforts to be of service to them. They put themselves in danger by transporting silver at their own expense, both for the Lord Bishop and for individual missionaries, for over four hundred French leagues. This favour was all the greater since the total sum is not small even though the individual shares in it were small. This is easily seen if one reckons each carried one hundred scudi; no small effort is required to transport them, and no little anxiety in trying to safeguard such an amount on an overland journey. I do not put this in terms of the weight of silver, and heartily wish that it could have been increased tenfold. However that may be, my pen cannot but rejoice to discover that, to crown the end of the year after these difficult months, this small favour done for the Reverend Franciscan Fathers is beyond question, despite the fact that others performed with equal charity in previous months were either | denied altogether by some or misrepresented as injuries by others.

Secondly, it is also worth remarking that the Most Illustrious Lord here sends his best wishes to Fr. Castner, to whom on November 3rd he had denied the faculty of administering the sacraments he had asked for.

### 27. The instructions of vicars apostolic and of other officials of the Holy See are sometimes to be corrected and rendered void

Thirdly, it is confirmed that there were varying reports on the censures of the Lord Patriarch on the Indian Coast both being approved and retracted, and the Most Illustrious Bishop here correctly judges that their retraction will prove in the future to be very advantageous in the present calamities of the Chinese mission. In this his views are poles apart from those in China who consider the important thing to be reinforcing the authority of the Commissar, just as previously it was maintaining that of the Vicar Apostolic, Bishop Maigrot. In so doing they think it more bearable to see the mission torn down than to criticise the thoughtless actions of the Commissar, a view which is contrary to the Spirit of the Church of God and the examples of the holy pontiffs, who in what they did aimed at nothing but the truth. If their informants fell short of the truth, these pontiffs believed that it was to their own great credit, necessary for the flock of the faithful, and pleasing to God to undo what they had mistakenly done once they saw that undoing it was necessary.

Fourthly, the Most Illustrious Bishop had good grounds for suspecting that Father Simões has, along with Fr. Buonaventura, incurred the wrath of the Lord Patriarch, because both of them—in the same place, in the same manner, <!-- PDF page 793 | book page 783 --> on the same day, and at the same hour, and having handed in identical documents—under the same condition sought and obtained a permit. That he should have subjected one of them to a censure and sent the other away requires some further explanation, which we shall perhaps produce in all good time, in some documents written in Fr. Buonaventura’s own hand and by others.

### 28. The missionaries harassed by the mandarins even after a has been received

Fifthly, His Most Illustrious Lordship the Bishop also bears witness to the harassments to which the missionaries have been subjected by mandarins and tribunals piao notwithstanding the piao. This will provide important material for writing under the period from the start of the year 1708 until June and beyond.

### 29. Fr. Miguel Fernández removed from the city of Ji’nan

Finally, the Lord Bishop suggests the reasons for the removal of Fr. Miguel Fernández from Ji’nan fu, about fu which we shall have to write at the proper time, because this was a heavy blow to the aforementioned Reverend Father and, even though he knew full well the reasons for his removal, he made sure that the nastier features of this affair were attributed to the Jesuits.

I testify that nothing is missing.[^p793-59]

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, Procurator and Notary Apostolic.
