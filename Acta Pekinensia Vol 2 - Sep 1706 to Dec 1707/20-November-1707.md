### 1. The Beijing archival records are sent on to Fr. Provana

I shall first give a fairly detailed account of affairs in Beijing and then discuss the events which were reported to Beijing this month from elsewhere. That is not to say that every single item arrived in this same month, but it is so that the reader may be informed about what has been done elsewhere in connection with the Jesuits in Beijing or against them, and about anything else which providentially has worked out in their favour somewhere else. However the reader should bear in mind once and for all that, whenever mention is made of a letter from Guangzhou, it cannot have arrived in Beijing in less than fifty days. Normally postal relays from the capital have taken about thirty days to get to Guangzhou; from Guangzhou to the capital about fifty, and often sixty or seventy.

November 1st. The records extracted from the Palace archives both for last year and this current year were handed over to the brother of the Governor of Guangdong to be sent post-haste and delivered into the hands of Fr. Provana. The Governor of Guangdong and Fr. Provana together certified in writing for the Imperial Court that they had been delivered.

November 3rd or 4th. The daren Pagara and Foiorò returned to Beijing from Macau. Reader, do not be surprised that I have put down “the 3rd or the 4th” as the date of their arrival, as if I could or should have pinned it down more definitely, for they wanted to keep their arrival secret. We heard that one of them had been seen in the square, and at once inquired at his house whether he had arrived. They replied that they did not know what we were talking about. And, just as they arrived in secret, so they slipped silently out of Beijing and went to Tartary to report to His Majesty.

November 8th. Some of us were unexpectedly summoned to the palace where letters from Guangzhou, Macau, and Europe were handed over to them, having been sent from Tartary. The daren had taken them there and given them to the Prince because, as they later said, it had long been in their instructions that they should first deliver all letters for the Europeans to the Prince. At the same time, the Fathers were ordered by an unknown messenger from the palace to translate all letters into Manchu so that whatever was written to the Europeans could be read out to the Emperor. The ill will of the Prince, authoritarian and full of distrust, worried us very much but we quickly did the translation in case the delay itself should serve only to fan his suspicions.

November 10th. Still more letters came from Tartary to be translated, most of them concerned with the journey and qualifications of our brethren Fathers

<!-- PDF page 743 | book page 733 | manuscript page 788 -->

Castner and Liebstain and Brother Stadlin; the Court was surprised that they had not yet arrived.

### 2. The — why should they speak disparagingly of Europeans?

In any case, those daren were somewhat badly received daren for no other reason than because throughout their expedition they had never, either in Macau or in Guangzhou, communicated with the Court whether about the business entrusted to them or other things that occurred on the way.[^p743-1] But when they discovered how many reasons for distrusting Europeans were being noised abroad, they trimmed their sails to the wind and said nothing which was polite about them, and nothing which was true. Instead they spread gossip everywhere, repeating anything discreditable or odious which they had heard— from Chinese who were envious of the dealings between the people of Macau and the Europeans, or from the rabble of bastard dregs of humanity. They considered that the only stuff for which the Lord Prince had any taste and which would do themselves any good was whatever was offensive to European ears and harmful to their reputation. Of the two, the more shameless was Zhang, otherwise known as Fopao or Foiorò, despite the fact that His Lordship the Legate had made great efforts to win him over both personally and especially through his people in Guangzhou, and through Fr. Bénard with gifts of considerable value. This man is a Tartar, a barbarian. He certainly ill repaid their kindness despite the fact that when he was with them and accepting gifts he made many promises, some of which he kept, but once he got to | Tartary he undid it all, repeating in detail to the Prince exactly what had in fact been said so that it could be passed on to the Emperor.

### 3. The Lord Patriarch, after being reported as trying to escape, is more narrowly confined in Macau

He reported that the Patriarch had come to an agreement with the Auditor of the Governor and through him with the Prefect in Xiangshan; that through these two the Captain General of Macau would be forced to remove the military guard; and to cap it all, that he had sent a servant to Fujian to arrange an escape route for himself. This was untrue although a Chinese was sent to an English ship in Ningbo, perhaps to pick up from it letters from Europe. In making these statements, I am not claiming that they are true, but that truly, in fact and to some effect, they were reported <!-- PDF page 744 | book page 734 --> by Foiorò in Tartary. On November 14th, in the palace in Beijing, he himself voluntarily admitted to me that he had himself told these stories in Tartary, and finally he repeated them all again from the beginning with many people present. Whether true or false, the consequence was that the Emperor ordered the Prince to write to the mandarins that the Lord Patriarch should be more carefully guarded.

November 13th. The Emperor returned to the capital from Tartary and remained there until the 18th. Oh dear, the number and length of the interrogations to which we were then subjected! His Majesty’s annoyance was increased by the unexplained slowness of the travellers Fathers Castner & co. Eventually from their letters it was learnt that one of their two guides, from the family of the Supreme or General Prefect of Canton,[^p744-2] had fallen ill two days into the journey, and his death had caused lengthy delays. Nonetheless, two men were sent by the Prince to meet them and, if they found them, to urge them strongly to hurry along.

November 14th. Fathers Pereira and Kilian were summoned to the Court and by order of the Prince each was handed a letter addressed to him, which they were made to translate on the spot. These two letters, along with a third one, had been lying among Foiorò’s baggage, which had been brought to Beijing while he himself went off to Tartary. In the two letters there was hardly anything to be found which might make the Prince’s mouth water apart from domestic news, but there was a third which from the address belonged to Fr. Antoine Thomas and which was kept in the Palace, since because of his infirmity he had not been able to come for it.

### 4. The Prince orders a letter sent to Fr. Antoine Thomas, which he thought to be from the Lord Patriarch, burned

November 15th. Since Fr. Antoine Thomas was not coming to the Court today either, the Prince required that the letter should be opened by the others and translated. But the other Jesuits, who had not even seen the letter, said that it was not right for them to interfere with it. They asked that it should be sent to the Father, since it could be readily assumed to have been sent by a friend and contained nothing apart from friendly chit-chat sent across the enormous oceans. The Prince stubbornly refused. He summoned Foiorò and inquired who it was that had given him the letter to take to Beijing. The fellow turned it over in his hand and looking at it more carefully asserted that it had been entrusted to him by the Lord Patriarch; he did not say whether the Patriarch had personally handed it to <!-- PDF page 745 | book page 735 | manuscript page 789 --> him or whether he had delivered it through someone else, and the Prince did not inquire any further either. He just ordered it to be burned in his presence.

[November] 16th & 17th. The days were passed amid various anxieties: we were not allowed to leave the palace, nor was it helpful to be subjected at every moment to the extremely impertinent questions maliciously brought up again and again by the daren.

[November] 18th. The Emperor left Beijing, intending to stay in the Villa from now on until the winter hunting season had started.

[November] 20th. The man who had gone to meet them came back bringing a letter from Fr. Castner to Fr. Kilian. But he told the Emperor about it, with the result that Fr. Kilian was at once summoned to the Villa and had to translate it as soon as he had opened it. In so doing, however, he did win their trust. On the same day Foiorò was sent to meet and take in charge the new priests, and to bring them in, forbidding any European to contact them either in conversation or in writing. |

[November] 21st. We sent servants to meet them, carrying winter clothes which were suitable for the place, but they were refused admission. When this news got back, one of the Jesuits hurried out to do all he could to make sure that they should not suddenly and by accident be brought within sight of His Majesty wearing Southern clothes, frayed by the journey, but the Prince’s cunning, stimulated by suspicion, had cut off all means of access.

### 5. The Jesuits who had travelled from Europe are received unbendingly by the Prince, but kindly by the Emperor

[November] 22nd. We were all summoned to the Villa and all alike forbidden to speak with the Jesuits, who were soon to arrive, until we had obtained permission from the Prince. At last the Fathers came and were admitted to the grounds of the Royal Villa. As soon as they arrived they were examined by the Prince. Since Fr. Castner spoke fairly good Chinese, he was compelled to reply to all the points which the Prince might be holding in his cunning heart. At length, when the Prince had reported to His Majesty and explained it all in front of the hunting party, he presented the Fathers themselves, who were belatedly treated with greater kindness. Then all their chests were opened in the most incredibly inquisitive manner, and—this is what defies belief—the Prince himself opened everything, examined it, fingered through every single item, and wasn’t even ashamed to lay out the European shoes on a separate table.

I take no pleasure in describing these events, because neither I nor Fr. Parrenin, who were present at the whole performance, could without embarrassment <!-- PDF page 746 | book page 736 | manuscript page 790 --> look on while such a great Prince, the firstborn of the greatest ruler of the entire Orient, should so sully his hands, and all because of an unfair suspicion.

What in the end was it, the reader will ask, that could have occasioned such suspicion? My answer is that those things will have occasioned suspicion which were the subject of the accusations made against the Jesuits by the Patriarch’s party, together with the accusations made in writing against them by the stupidity of Fr. Guéty in Tartary a year earlier. The Prince may have laughed at the time, but he kept at the back of his mind these accusations with which the Patriarch had come to rebuke the Jesuits for this and that. But leaving aside the other points made in the accusations, the one he will have seized upon was that the Pope had given orders that the Jesuits should be punished for engaging in trade in China and so on. So, the Prince had promised his father and the whole Court that he would uncover the more widespread dishonesties of the Europeans, and that he would bring to light the sources from which they secretly sought to derive their income. He did not find his father unwilling to listen; after all he had in this case given him permission to search the Europeans’ luggage. I myself give witness that on that very day I heard from the mouth of the Prince himself that he had laid things out so carefully that it would be impossible for a single needle to be removed from the belongings of the new guests without his noticing.

### 6. Their luggage is searched by the Prince

This almost military search of the bags was heating up, yet not a single piece was found which was worth bringing to the notice of His Majesty. In the end, some mechanical clockmaking tools which had not previously been seen were presented, and the Prince’s exhausted curiosity began to give way to a sense of shame that the outcome so lacked credibility. Even the Crown Prince indicated that enough was enough, and when the eunuchs reported this, the Prince eventually had to have the poor remnants taken away, among which he had not found the booty he had longed for before he started.

Now another surprise awaits the reader. On that same night the Prince once again sent word and on behalf of the Emperor advised the Jesuits themselves to make a search; if there was anything worthy of the Emperor’s eyes, they should bring it on the following day. He was not so thick-skinned that he could face his father with his nets and hands empty when he had promised rich spoils. | In case the guests had absolutely nothing to offer except the lathe they had brought, they should borrow something from their brethren who lived in Beijing, so that they could at least in this way save their poverty-stricken blushes.

### 7. Small gifts to the Prince from the Beijing Jesuits

And so, with the help of the Prince’s own secretary at a very late hour letters were sent to the three churches, asking each of them to bring on the following day whatever they had to make up for the inadequacies of our Religious poverty. In the end, two small globes were found, one celestial and one terrestrial made of silver (engraved at some time past by the hand of the Most Serene Duke of Bavaria[^p747-3]); an instrument for observing the inclination of a magnetic needle; a few small mathematical instruments; and a few trifles glued together that same night from among the bits and pieces broken by the journey. These were presented the next morning (the 24th) and in part accepted. The Prince’s acquisitiveness calmed down and questions on scientific topics began to be asked with such intensity that the new guests were not allowed to go home to wash their feet after such a long journey and so many hardships.

### 8. A comet observed

[November] 27th. First, a new comet was identified and

28th. the same one was observed in the constellation of Aquarius, 6 degrees 20 of longitude and 18 degrees north latitude.

29th. The same was observed again in the constellation, 6 degrees 20 longitude, north latitude 17 degrees 11. The Emperor was not notified of this because from the first day to the second and the second to the third it became notably smaller, and now was hardly different from an ordinary star.[^p747-4]

### 9. The death of the Franciscan Fr. Antonino Ripa in Guangzhou

I turn now to events which happened in other places yet which in their own way affected the Beijing Jesuits.

November 2nd. In Guangzhou, Rev. Fr. Antonino da Ripa Bottoni of the Order of St. Francis[^p747-5] died piously; Fr. José Pereira of the Society of Jesus first announced his death to the Beijing Jesuits on November 5th, asking for the usual prayers to be offered, and Rev. Fr. Fray Buonaventura da Roma did the same on the 6th. He wrote in the following terms: “Fr. Antonino died on November 2nd at 7 in the morning. Please, would Your Reverence for <!-- PDF page 748 | book page 738 | manuscript page 791 --> the love of God ask all the Jesuits to be so kind as to add some prayers for the soul of this father, in addition to the normal three Masses.”

### 10. As he was dying, he approves the practice of the Society in adopting the Chinese Rites

The latter added the following: “The Franciscan Fr. Antonino died on the 2nd of this month. On the day he received the Holy Viaticum[^p748-6] he said, in the presence of Fr. Nuñez[^p748-7] and Fr. Langasco,[^p748-8] that he was not sorry that he had allowed the practices of the Society to the Christians in China, for these were in accordance with natural law, which those who take a different view contravene. This was his conscientious judgement. This good Father had a happy death, for which he had waited for 15 days, in great conformity with the will of God and much consoled by his Franciscan brethren.”[^p748-9]

Fr. Monteiro had sent the news about this in his letter to Fr. Kilian, on October 29th, writing as follows: “Fr. Antonino Ripa, a Religious and learned man, has finally died. When he received the Holy Viaticum he professed, in the presence of the Bishop of Ascalon, Fr. Nuñez, and his Religious brethren that he continued to take the view favourable to the Chinese rites because in conscience he believed that they were in accord with natural law.”

November 6th. Reverend Fr. Buonaventura wrote to Fr. Kilian to thank him for not asking for permission for him to go to Macau, and also to let him know how he had been treated as if he had been excommunicated etc. His letter is as follows:

### 11. The letter of Fr. Buonaventura da Roma to Fr. Kilian

Most Reverend Father, | After my arrival here in the city of Guangzhou, I received two very gracious letters from Your Reverence to which I reply by saying that I am ready for anything in the interests of justice. I am not, nor will I be, ashamed to assert what is just, even to the Pope himself. Your Reverence (and all the Jesuits as well) can be sure that I shall stand up for the truth. I have already said to Rev. Fr. Visitor and to the Vice-Provincial that I will willingly do anything I can to be of assistance, and gladly, even going to Rome if they think it would be appropriate. There is nothing to be said about <!-- PDF page 749 | book page 739 --> the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing. He has let me down as well. I thought he was writing to the Lord Patriarch asking him to reconstruct the crumbling mission and care for the sheep entrusted to him, but he did not say a word about any of these things. On the Day of Judgement he will see what good it has done him to have remained so silent on matters in which he was in justice bound to try to do some good, at least for his own flock. So Your Reverences did well not to obtain my passage to Macau, which would have been pointless. I humbly beg Your Reverences, whenever I ask you to get something for me, if it does not seem to Your Reverences to be possible, or not convenient, by all means write to me, for I am well aware that Your Reverences will do everything in your power for the missionaries if you think it would help. So I thank you, just as if I had got permission to go to Macau.

As for myself, the French priests in the city of Shaozhou refused to have any contact with me; they said I had received the piao in the form used by the Jesuit Fathers and that the Patriarch had declared me excommunicated and a perjurer. I have written to the Patriarch; that was two weeks ago, and he still has not replied. So far there is no confirmation of this declaration.

Fr. de Maij is staying in the city of Qie’an,[^p749-10] and in the city of Gaozhou[^p749-11] there is a Religious of my Order, Diego de Santa Rosa. They are intent upon losing all the Christians: Fr. de Maij publicly burned the tablets of the dead and refused to hear the confessions of those who would not hand them over. For sure, if the Emperor knows of this we shall all be expelled from this mission. The decree of the Emperor ordering the expulsion of those Europeans who do not have a piao has not been published there.

### 12. The nature of the Patriarch’s decree

We have no news from Europe, but of one thing I am convinced: that a decree of the Holy Father has not been sighted. From what the Lord Patriarch said in Nan’an to Fr. Fernández (Serrano) it can be inferred that the decree was fabricated by the Patriarch and does not come from the Pope. He said something to the following effect: I deliberately produced this decree in such a way that if there is anything the Pope wishes to add or remove from it he will be able to do as he sees fit.

This much from Fr. Buonaventura.[^p749-12]

That this conforms to the original text, I so testify,

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, acting as Apostolic Notary.

### 13. Fr. De Maij publicly burns the tablets of the dead, but not with impunity

3. It is true that Fr. de Maij had some tablets brought out to him, and in public—that is not to say in the square or the street, but in the forecourt of the church inside his compound—burnt them with others looking on. But then, even without the decree being promulgated there, he was expelled by the mandarins and went into hiding in Guangzhou | with his beard shaved off in the house of a certain Chinese until he was able to board a ship bound for Manila dressed as a sailor, bidding farewell to the mission and the Sacred Congregation. When Fr. Kilian in Beijing was talking about this with the medical doctor Giovanni Borghese, he replied, astonishingly, that the aforementioned Father had denied that he had set sail from Europe or left the Philippines to come to China in order to work in the mission, but rather to paint all the sights of this empire, especially the Great Wall, and would then with the people from Manila go to Mexico, where he would bring a lawsuit about the inheritance of the notorious General Papacino,[^p750-13] which involved him. However that may be, he certainly did a lot of paintings, and left some to the Chinese unfinished, with the result that while he was laughing at the Chinese among his friends, the Chinese in their houses were laughing at these heralds of the Gospel coming from Europe.

### 14. A letter from the Bishop of Ascalon to Fr. Antoine Thomas

On November 11th, the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon was kind enough to write to Fr. Antoine Thomas as follows:

Last month and at the beginning of this month, I wrote more than once to Your Paternity and to Rev. Fr. Stumpf. But since I think that letters from Guangzhou get delivered to Beijing only with difficulty, in the end I am sending this one via Ganzhou. I wrote to Your Paternity saying how displeased I was that an application was presented to the Emperor in my name, saying I have followed the practices of Fr. Ricci and at the same time promising that when I was strong enough I would travel to Beijing, when it was clear from both my letters to Your Reverence that this was not my intention. Since the matter was still not settled, I wrote that no matter what, a different song would have to be sung on my behalf. I can see that what is done is done, with good intention and as a kindness to me, for which I have thanked and now again thank Your Reverence and the other Fathers. But in fact I cannot think of anything which could have done me and perhaps the entire mission more damage. I admit that I had asked that, in the event that the missionaries are expelled, a request might be made through you that I might remain in Guangzhou, like Fathers Monteiro and Muñoz, and that I expressly added that no undertaking was to be given on my behalf. If this proved impossible, it would have been preferable to ask my advice in the crisis than to destroy me altogether. Just recently an order came from the Viceroy, which said simply that Bai Wanle was coming to receive the piao, had fallen ill during the journey, and gone back to Guangzhou. He is not being required to come until he is better. No further elaboration was given. Consequently, I remain prevented from leaving China, nothing is settled about my being free to do so, and in the end I will be forced to go to Beijing. And for what? So that, at risk to myself and to others, I can protest to the Emperor that those words I have followed the practices of Ricci are not mine, and that I obey the Patriarch’s decree to the letter? For that is exactly the declaration I shall make even if the Emperor’s anger is aroused against me, since that is for the best. It therefore seems preferable for me to try to look to my own safety and escape from China by whatever route I can. For on the other hand the Patriarch is making ever tougher threats, and the worst thing that could be alleged by my enemies is to accuse me of violating the edict after my appeal has been explicitly turned down.

### 15. He unwillingly obeys the Lord Patriarch’s decree

The remedy which could solve the difficulties involved in my flight is simply that through Your Paternity it should be arranged that a letter should be written to the Viceroy stating that he should not prevent me leaving for Europe. For, in default of that command, Your Paternity must be in no doubt that he will force me to <!-- PDF page 752 | book page 742 | manuscript page 793 --> go to Beijing as soon as it is clear that I am sufficiently recovered. So that is what I most earnestly ask Your Paternity to do, and that Your Paternity with the other Jesuits should try to erase every memory of me from the Emperor’s mind; I shall speak strongly in your favour in Rome, either in person or by letter, but equally strongly I shall speak against you in China as long as there is an obligation to obey the decree. And even though I was asking you for a favour, since it was through a mistake that I was pushed into such straits, I am now asking for a remedy as a matter of justice. Your Paternity and the other Jesuits are ignorant of how things are in Guangzhou, and so you make many impracticable arrangements. First, you wish the missionaries to go to Beijing to receive the piao; but the Viceroy makes difficulties and in the sharpest terms Fr. Porquet was forbidden to go. Second, you take the view that everyone will go, but apart from Fr. Porquet almost nobody will go because they are unwilling to oppose the Lord Patriarch, who wishes everyone to go but to do so with his decree intact so that the Emperor will be clear how much greater is the Patriarch’s authority | in China than the Emperor’s. Not many people, myself included, will find this display of ill-feeling anything to laugh at.

Thus the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon.

### 16. The Lord Patriarch and his party gloatingly strengthen their position

Note that, from their very first arrival until now, the sole aim of the Patriarch’s party has been to flaunt the sacred and wide powers of the Lord Patriarch. They do not regard it as having been granted by His Holiness Pope Clement XI for edification, rather they presume that they have a share in it. So, they lord it over the priests, who are very few in China, issuing arbitrary orders without hearing any contrary arguments or having any preliminary inquiry into cases. Thus, when in the month of April 1705 the Lord Patriarch had at first, in accordance with the advice of the old missionaries of various orders, decided to pay a visit to Beijing incognito, his party insisted that he should decide to force the Fathers in Beijing, without any discussion, to tell the Emperor that he, the apostolic visitor, had arrived; that he was the superior of them all; and that he wished to be received with public honour. And so in Beijing, in December, the first month of his stay, nothing was more frequently heard than the boast by the auditor Fr. Sabino Mariani that the Patriarch was the Pontiff of the missionaries, that he could release all the Religious from their vows, abolish their privileges, define <!-- PDF page 753 | book page 743 --> the meaning of their institute, and interpret the decisions of previous Popes; that what was to be left to the Religious missionaries was whatever the Lord Patriarch left, and that whatever he in the fullness of his power should remove was justly to be removed. So, when the Beijing Jesuits brought a petition on March 1st begging the Bishop of Beijing to tell them what was to be done and what reply was to be made to the Christians, who were upset by the Patriarch’s denunciation stating in effect that the practices of their traditional Rites were to be abandoned, he replied: We should be careful, in case the Patriarch leaps to issue a decree and chooses to deploy all the power which swells his chest, for then, because of his superior authority, there would be no way out. So the Patriarch himself, not just once but especially on 3 March 1706, threatened Fr. Pereira by saying that he had been given in Rome the power to pass judgment without hearing the other side and to carry out sentence, a power which he intended to use, and in fact he did use it on more than one occasion.

### 17. The Lord Patriarch says that no appeal is possible from his judgement &c

So he himself with some heat told Fr. J. François Gerbillon that there was no appeal from him against the violent measures with which he had threatened him. So also when at the end of August the Patriarch had been ordered by the Emperor not to postpone the day which had been decided upon for his departure, our whole house resounded with patriarchal power which they said was going to move into action once the Lord Patriarch had left, so that the Emperor would be dumbfounded that such power had been given to mortals. Again, while the Bishop of Beijing was accompanying him on the journey as far as Linqing, His Excellency the Lord Patriarch quite often repeated that if the decree against the Rites came, he would publish it at once, heedless of any consequences, so that his and the papal power should shine out in these lands to combat the leniency which the Bishop of Beijing was himself advocating. And then again in January and February in Nanjing the Patriarchal party urged him to use his wide powers at last and set out a decree on his own authority, stemming of course from that of the Pope. He should oblige all the missionaries to obey it under pain of the most severe censures, heedless of the danger which was rightly seen to threaten both himself and the missionaries; heedless, too, of the eternal ruin of the neophytes and of the exclusion of the Holy Gospel from this empire which they could and should have seen as the natural consequence of their actions. And so, once the decree was passed, the Patriarch’s party spread everywhere the idea that a new article of faith had been laid down by the Patriarch’s authority, as a consequence of which the Pope himself has no power to change it in any way. The same is now happening in Guangzhou, as the Bishop of Ascalon notes in his letter of November 11th, and His Excellency <!-- PDF page 754 | book page 744 | manuscript page 794 --> himself on November 18th says just the same in a reply to Fr. de Goville in which he protests that he is using his own authority and will admit of no appeal, as will be evident from the entire text of the response on the 18th of this month. Let the reader take note | of these things and pray along with us that these patriarchal Boanerges[^p754-15] might come to see of what spirit they are.

November 12th. The Rev. Fr. Tomás Hortiz, Superior of the Augustinian fathers from Shaojing fu in the province of Guangdong, sent Fr. Antoine Thomas the following letter, from which it can readily be seen how things stood in that province and how the missionaries felt about it. Anyone who reads it even quickly will hardly doubt that this man has written not just in accordance with his own judgment but expressing the feelings shared by all the missionary Religious.

### 18. A letter from Fr. Tomás Hortiz, an Augustinian, to Fr. Antoine Thomas

To Your Reverence, Pax Christi. I received Your Reverence’s letter with the news of your improved health, which I was delighted to hear. I ask God that he may keep it so for many years to come, that with your great zeal for the needs of our time you may come to the aid of this mission. Given the present state of affairs we have every reason to be alarmed that it will be endangered by the waves of the savage tempest which is tossing it about. Since the force of this storm is so great that there is no hope to be placed in our human efforts, we must have recourse to the seat of the divine mercy, humbly begging it not to desert us in our hour of need but to guard us and this mission from all adversity. I am surprised when Your Paternity says that there is no hope of saving it by any human means unless the Most Illustrious Lord Legate suspends the regulation he has published. As I say, I am surprised that Your Paternity says this as if there were in some way grounds for hope that the Patriarch would suspend his decree, especially in the light of his sharp reply to the Bishop of Ascalon’s petition on the subject, seriously taking him to task as though by his appeal he were going to instigate a riot. I have always thought this was a mere theoretical possibility with no basis in fact: it would have been easier previously not to have published it at all than to suspend it now. There was greater danger then than threatens now, and yet he refused to refrain from its publication; so how is he going to be willing to suspend it now? I am very sad to hear that we have now been given leave to go to the Emperor in Beijing in order to get the diploma allowing us to stay in China; for if so long as the

Patriarch’s decree remains in force there is no hope of saving the mission, what hope is there of obtaining that permit if we answer questions in accordance with the decree?

### 19. The difficulty in accepting the permit and not obeying the Patriarch’s decree

Besides, the aforementioned permit could more accurately be described as a way of destroying the mission than as a permission to remain in China, for in the end it leads to the destruction of this mission and the loss of the missionaries. It is a kind of disguised persecution, both carried out without a sound and, one might say, corrosive rather than explosive, not violent yet slowly but inevitably producing its effect. This mission, therefore, cannot survive if the law about obtaining the diploma is not repealed as soon as may be. Your Paternity must be aware once and for all that, reckoning nothing of any danger, we shall steadfastly observe the regulation which has been laid down, as the Lord Patriarch says, by the Church and the Supreme Pontiff unless the Supreme Pontiff goes back to the beginning and lays down another regulation: firstly because our conscience requires us to do so. Secondly because otherwise the result will be that the Church and the whole world will believe that the difficulty about abolishing the rites is caused by the missionaries alone and not by the Chinese. Thirdly because the duty of obedience to the Church and her ministers has been given to us by Christ our Lord but this Chinese mission has not. If we refuse obedience, we shall deserve judgment and will suffer the punishment of the unfaithful servants. If by our offering obedience the mission comes to ruin, it cannot be laid at our door provided we did our duty and left no duty undone. Fourthly, although rumour has it that the aforementioned decree flowed from the will of the Patriarch himself and not from the Church, no basis for this rumour has been provided. Why, therefore, do we put so much trust in a rumour spread by this or that individual as to turn away | from an obedience to a decree which is based on such a strict and serious witness, thus greatly burdening our conscience?

### 20. The reasons which led Fr. Hortiz, the Augustinian, to follow the Lord Patriarch’s line

In addition to the testimony of the Legate, I have seen a letter written in Rome by Rev. Fr. Castner, who says that the Holy Father told him to write to the Jesuits in China and say that so far as the Rites are concerned they should obey everything which the Lord Patriarch laid down for them. So how will it help to do otherwise! This is quite apart from the fact that it can hardly be doubted that the decree will by now have been published, for some people begged His Holiness to do this for the good and benefit of this mission. Now, therefore, that the decree has been published, for better or for worse that’s <!-- PDF page 756 | book page 746 --> how it will remain, whether we now obey, or whether we do not. To seek to resist and withdraw from our obedience will be no more than whistling in the wind, besides being a violation of our conscience and other evils as well. Again and again we should congratulate ourselves that we followed this path from the beginning, and we offer due thanks to some of the priests of the Society who opened such a way for us and who by removing the Jing Tian tablet and other such things, which they arranged to have done in obedience to the decree, led us in this way by their example. What we and this mission will lose in the future because of these actions our Father in Heaven knows. We have sought first obedience and his justice, and whatever we and this mission are lacking will surely be added unto us. The dealings of the Archbishop of Goa have been declared null by the Supreme Pontiff. In contrast, the actions of the Lord Patriarch have been confirmed as valid. A similar sentence has been confirmed against the Capuchin Fathers.[^p756-16] I have seen and read a transcript of the decree. No doubt in the future there will be a similar outcome to the question of the subjection of the regulars, the business of Macau, and every single other piece of business in China.

### 21. In the Philippines, the regulars were compelled to be subject to the Bishop and follow his line

The affair between the Archbishop of Manila and the regulars was settled in favour of the Archbishop, and His Holiness together with the King of Spain ordered the regulars to be subject to their ordinaries, leaving them no freedom to withdraw from their ministries. Whether they liked it or not, they were bound by both kinds of justice, including the secular, to remain in their ministries and to obey their ordinaries. So what path are they to follow now? None other than to bear their adversities patiently and to weep for their sufferings and afflictions. The actions taken against us by the mandarins here have just the same cause as the one which explains the sufferings of the other missionaries, but with this difference: that the mandarins in other districts have shown themselves less severe than the mandarin of this city who has little regard for the law of God, which he criticises in many ways.

### 22. A mandarin in the province of Guangdong demolishes a sacred building

So, after we were confined to this church, it happened that a single apostate, as people say he is, attempted a rebellion. The result of this was that a local graduate from a certain district called Meiyan zhou who had long nourished an extreme hatred for a church which we had built some years ago <!-- PDF page 757 | book page 747 | manuscript page 796 --> got some of his supporters together and put them up to making accusations to the mandarin of the city against the church, asking him for an edict allowing them to demolish it, as though the sin of a single apostate could fall upon it. The mandarin by chance was in the neighbourhood and without giving the matter any consideration at all came in person, burnt the piao containing our permission to preach the law of God, and overturned the altar and the images, which he later sent to us in this church here. Three days later he arrived in person and told us that it was not convenient for that altar to be in that church, and so he had sent it to us. Then, in our presence, | he ordered the baozhang and baojia[^p757-17] not in any way to make it difficult for us to leave the house. But this had no effect, since it was merely an oral rather than a written command. So today we have been locked in again as we were at the start with the exception of Fr. Juan Barruelo, who managed to get permission from the mandarin of the town to return to his own church. Next that same graduate along with nine others brought an accusation with a request that the said church should be demolished. We do not know whether it will turn out well or badly. There are those who say that the mandarin would like to correct his earlier mistakes, but others say that he is absolutely set upon destroying the church. May God show us his favour in such a great affliction.

### 23. Some observations on Fr. Hortiz’s letter

It must be said however that there was no lack of material for anyone who did want to answer, provided that when he was offering reasons, either his own or other people’s, he could control himself so that no opportunity would be given for some premeditated calumny to be hurled against the Jesuits. For example, the first reason taken from the document seemed strange because of its subject-matter: for the self-same Rev. Fr. Hortiz had already composed a paper which he had shown to others arguing against the subjection [of the Religious] and against the oath which the Lord Patriarch extracted from the Religious to give to the Sacred Congregation;[^p758-19] on that occasion the dictate of his own conscience did not stop him from fighting against the same Lord Patriarch to safeguard the privileges of his Order, yet now it does prevent him from appealing or from putting the Gospel and the salvation of souls before obedience.

### 24. The difficulty in forbidding the Chinese Rites derives from the Emperor and the Chinese themselves, not from the missionaries

The second reason and the conclusion drawn from it is also manifestly false because a year ago in November and then definitively in December, experience made it clear that the difficulty in forbidding the Chinese Rites came from the side of the Chinese and the Emperor: the Bishop of Conon and the Fathers Mezzafalce and Guéty were expelled because of their prohibition on the Rites and their unchangeable determination that they would not be permitted, and then nine Jesuits were sent into exile in Guangzhou, soon to be followed by the Abbate di San Giorgio and Fathers Hervé, Montigny, Carvalho, and Le Breton, along with nine or ten Dominican fathers. In addition, a decree of the Emperor was published enjoining the execution of anyone, European or Chinese, who attacked the traditional rites. I simply cannot see where the mind or the pen could have come from which does not recognise the difficulties made by the Chinese at that time, when the author of the letter remembers that he along with his companions were then kept locked up, the frontal inscription of the church burned, the altar destroyed, the images removed, and on the following day the church itself demolished beyond any hope of repair. Yet from this letter the Church especially and the whole world will be given to understand that the difficulty in forbidding the Chinese Rites came only from the missionaries and not the

<!-- PDF page 759 | book page 749 | manuscript page 797 -->

Chinese. The author does indeed mention the envy and hatred of some graduate or other but makes no mention at all of the fact that long before that he had been locked up with his companions; says nothing of the fact that some Augustinian fathers promulgated the decree immediately; and does not mention that one of them wished to carry it out in respect for the tablets of the dead, but so many men and women gathered around to stop him that he was forced to take flight to a suburban church in Shaojing. Had he kept these events in mind, perhaps he might not have written this second reason, or perhaps he had borrowed it from someone else | and was unwilling to record his own experience and that of his companions in case the falsity of his explanation should be obvious.

### 25. Fr. Hortiz’s third point is refuted

His third reason is such that in the year 1705 it could have prevented the author from writing against presenting the oath to the Sacred Congregation and its vicars. For it would not have been his fault if the privilege of his Order had been lessened, since neither the Church, nor the Pope, nor his provincial in Manila had issued an order or reason for obeying it. Anyway, this explanation presupposes what is not the case, for it assumes that it would have been necessary to have positively rejected the authority, power, or decree of the Patriarch, which nobody so far has suggested. I do not know why the author did not bethink himself of the Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon, who gave assurance that he would obey and was prepared to profess such obedience before the Emperor at the risk of his life, yet would have lodged an appeal. He did not yet promulgate the decree, nor did he as yet teach what the decree ordered, but pursued his appeal while publicly explaining that he would go to the City and there defend the declarations of the Emperor against the decree of the Lord Patriarch.

As for the further point that were the mission to perish because the regulation was promulgated and observed it would not be the fault of the Religious, this proves nothing more than that once the mission had been lost the Religious would go back to his monastery and his cowl and would have as much afterwards as he had had before, and could quietly live for himself. Nowhere in the world can a Religious expelled in such circumstances be arraigned in positive law because the mission and so many souls have perished, but it does not thereby follow that such a Religious will be blameless before God. The canon law and the civil magistrate punish many things to which no blame attaches in the divine tribunal, and the public forum passes over many things which involve an obligation in the eternal law. If, as the author maintains, it is not to be considered a fault to have allowed the mission to be destroyed by leaving <!-- PDF page 760 | book page 750 | manuscript page 798 --> undone those things which one had no obligation to do, might it not have been considered praiseworthy to have saved the mission by doing what one was not obliged to do?

Who had put St. Paul in charge of the ship and the souls who sailed in her? Yet when shipwreck seemed about to engulf them all, it was he who made the danger clear to the centurion together with a way of avoiding it, contrary to the view of the helmsman and the captain; it was he who pointed out to the soldiers that the sailors were tricking them, despite the fact that he was not in charge of the ship, nor did the soldiers have any power over it.[^p760-20] Just as the laws are silent in war, so to people who are trapped on a precipitous slope the laws give way to individuals who, if they are endowed with bravery and divine zeal together with great charity, may be praised—even superlatively—for coming to their aid.

### 26. His fourth argument is demolished

The fourth argument comes from someone who is ignorant of the law. The common law is that someone who is asking to be trusted must behave in a trustworthy manner; a representative promulgating a law must show the law and the words of the legislator, and should not be believed if he does not do so. If a transcript of the pontifical decree against the actions of the Archbishop of Goa was shown to the author of the letter even when it had nothing to do with him, why did he not ask to be shown a copy of the pontifical decree against the Chinese Rites, which had everything to do with him? Requests to be shown at least an authenticated copy of it had come from the Bishop of Conon, the Bishop of Beijing, the Bishop of Ascalon, the Vicar Apostolic of Nanjing, the Jesuit Vice-Provincial, and others, yet that favour was never granted to a single soul; indeed nobody’s legal right was respected, though it was a matter of the greatest gravity concerning the souls both of the missionaries and of the Chinese. Yet while the Lord Patriarch was denying this, he was quick enough to circulate in China a nonauthenticated transcript | concerning a foreign case about the censures against the good Capuchin Fathers. From this fact alone it is no mere conjecture to suggest that he just did not possess what was being asked for, since the only support he offered for his authority was the copy of a pontifical letter which simply did not deal with the Chinese question at all. However weighty the testimony of the Lord Patriarch might be, nobody acts as a legate in virtue of his personal character, but effectively to place someone under an obligation. The canons cannot be ignored: either a particular law must lay it down that it is within his competence, or else he is bound by the common law concerning legates.

### 39. Fr. Castner’s letter

Reverend Father in Christ,

From your last letter which I received two days ago, I see that Your Reverence wishes me to reply to the calumny which I hear some people are spreading, that when I was on my way back from Europe, I opened letters addressed to the Most Illustrious Patriarch of Antioch which had been entrusted to me by the Sacred Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith, and subsequently sealed them in a different envelope which I then addressed in my own hand and in that condition had them delivered to the Most Illustrious Patriarch.

<!-- PDF page 768 | book page 758 -->

In the first place, those who are spreading such stories, if they were true, should have brought me before my legitimate superiors, where I could have replied to the calumny. Otherwise, they automatically demonstrate clearly enough that they are looking neither for the truth nor for justice, but seek merely to damage my reputation and to slander me contrary to all natural law and still more to Christian charity.

It is completely untrue to say that any seal was violated by me or that any letters of the Sacred Congregation to the Illustrious Patriarch were opened by me. If the Patriarch himself is questioned about this, I trust in his conscience that he will not refuse to admit that the package I brought to Macau (and it was the only one with which I was entrusted, together with a small chest) had an unbroken seal. God is my witness that I have brought it with unblemished honour and with great care, and delivered it safely without any serious problems.

### 40. He explains what happened

The sequence of events was as follows. Since I had to go through considerable parts of Italy, Germany, Holland, and England by land, and in addition the sea voyage was longer than normal, as everyone agrees, I noticed that as a result of the many hardships of the journeys the package in question suffered a small amount of damage to the tips of the corners of its wrapping. To prevent any further damage I added an additional layer of wrapping and in my own hand wrote the same address as was on the inside envelope. The inner envelope remained with its address and seal absolutely intact. It was only the tips of the corners of the package which had been somewhat worn away by the uncertainties of the journeys.

But while I was in Macau, I arranged with the assistance of the Rev. Fr. Provincial that the package in question together with the small chest should be delivered to the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch in Macau unopened, and an authenticated affidavit to this effect was completed by the Public Notary of Macau.

This is the exact truth. If anyone denies it, let him bring me before my legitimate superiors and I shall answer not only as to a malicious liar spreading calumny to others or, worse, defamation and detraction against me, rather than showing himself to be someone who is seeking the truth.

Again, people who spread around such allegations by that alone show their ill-will as well. If they had at least sincerely believed that the package in question had been opened (which however is false), and had been rewrapped again by me, they should rather have supposed that it had happened in crossing so many provinces and kingdoms where, especially in those times when wars are raging, it is agreed that almost all letters do get opened. At least they ought to have inquired about this before spreading such a calumny around. Surely the <!-- PDF page 769 | book page 759 | manuscript page 803 --> law of nature and above all the Christian Law requires as much. | Indeed, if I am to write the truth, it required no little worry and indeed money to make sure that they were carried unharmed from Italy to Germany, and thence to Holland, and especially to England (where everything is done extremely carefully so that even one’s clothes are routinely searched), and from there to Portugal to be taken to India and finally safely delivered. On that account, in return for such carefulness and unrecompensed expenditure thanks rather than calumny might have been expected, especially from people who wish to be disciples of Christ.

### 41. He confirms the truth through the testimony of the Bishop of Ascalon and of the Lord Patriarch

Moreover, as soon as this kind of rumour reached the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon, he was kind enough at once to swear the opposite: that from the same packet brought by me he had received one letter addressed to him by the Sacred Congregation and forwarded by the Patriarch, completely safe and inviolate. What the Most Illustrious Lord was kind enough to testify, unasked, he will, I hope, continue to be willing to testify if he is legitimately questioned. But the people in whom not truth but calumny is close to their heart are quite uncaring about such things. These, then, are the facts which I have had transcribed, and at the same time I offer myself as ready to obey any commands.

From the Court of the Emperor in Tartary, 24 September 1708.

PS. If there had been any honestly motivated preliminary interrogation about this calumny, it would have been easy for me to provide witnesses who saw that package with its seal unbroken. But even this would not be necessary, for I have confidence in the conscience of the Most Illustrious Patriarch that he would not refuse to confess the truth.

Your Reverence’s least servant in Christ, and your unworthy subject, Kaspar Castner.

That this corresponds with the original, I hereby testify.

Giampaolo Gozani, SJ, Visitor for China.

### 42. Fr. José Simões also lodges an appeal

November 24th. Fr. José Simões, a missionary in Nanchang who last July together with Rev. Fr. Buonaventura da Roma had accepted a permit, informed Rev. Fr. José Soares, the Vice- Provincial who was in Beijing, that he too had on his own initiative thought of an appeal against the decree or rather regulation of the Lord Patriarch, and that he had sent it by three routes.[^p769-37] But this appeal, so far as we have been able to find out, could not have come into His Excellency’s hands.

On the same day, the 24th, Fr. Monteiro wrote to the same Rev. Fr. Vice- Provincial making these points among others:[^p770-38]

Regarding Fr. Müllener, these mandarins have not up until now passed on his request to delay the time appointed for his return to Sichuan and neither have they passed on Fr. Fontanilla’s request to return to his church of Nanxiong. On the 20th of this month Fathers Lirot and Henry[^p770-39] arrived here from Shaozhou fu. The former was accompanied by four chairen, the reason being that some time ago he was ordered to leave a town in Zhixian[^p770-40] to come here. He wants to settle in Shaozhou but is waiting for a decision from the mandarins. It is said that Fr. Basset has boarded an English ship.[^p770-41] Fr. Le Blanc has not made an appearance and is thought to be in hiding in Huguang.[^p770-42]

The latter was a mistake, however. Although the Very Rev. Fr. Le Blanc separated from Fr. Henri his companion in the province of Yunnan on the journey, he did not remain in Huguang but rather went across to the province of Fujian where he laid low, as will be seen under the month of May 1708.

November 30th. The Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing sent the following reply to Fr. Vice-Provincial, whose letter sent on the 15th I do not record here since its import can be understood from the reply. The principal point was that the Provincial had sensibly requested permission | for administration[^p770-43] for Fathers Castner and Liebstain, who were then hourly expected. This permission the Lord Bishop refused, as can be seen in what follows.

### 43. The letter of the Bishop of Beijing to Fr. Vice-Provincial

The letter of Your Reverence the Vice-Provincial dated 15 November arrived in my hands on the 26th of the same month, together with another enclosed from the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon. I have put off replying until today because I was waiting for the young man whom you already know, but since he has not yet returned I am writing at length to Monsignor Ascalon; his letter is <!-- PDF page 771 | book page 761 --> included with that for Fr. Antoine and I hope he will try to send it on as soon as he possibly can.

I am very pleased that the two fathers are coming, together with a brother, to be in the service of the Emperor. That perhaps is already saying something, but I am very disappointed that the priests from Guangzhou are not coming nor are all the other recent arrivals, since I do not know the reason why they are all delayed for such a long time.[^p771-44]

### 44. He denies permission for administration sought for Frs. Castner and Liebstain

Concerning the powers of administration which you ask from me for the Fathers mentioned above, I reply that I very much regret that I cannot grant them until they send me their oath that they will observe the decree of the Lord Patriarch. In addition, since you are the Vice-Provincial, I ask you to send me yours too and those of all your subjects in my diocese, since the Lord Patriarch has ordered me to do this. On this point, when I wrote some time ago to Very Rev. Fr. Antoine Thomas, who at that time was Provincial Superior, he replied that he had discussed the matter with the same Lord Patriarch. He has written nothing more to me. Seeing the censures which are flying around in Macau and in China, I am very much afraid in case that gentleman might move against you too and against my person. He would do much worse things if he knew that I gave permission to administer to those who do not submit to his orders. Very Rev. Vice-Provincial, be patient, for I am doing as much as I possibly can.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Procurator and Apostolic Notary.[^p771-45]

There is nothing missing here; I the above signatory so testify.
