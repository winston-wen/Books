Very few things happened during this month, and most of them were touched on in September in so far as they fell within that time.

On October 7th Purgama or Pursama arrived and when we wanted to ask after the health of the Emperor (by whom he was sent) he would not allow this unless the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon was sent away. This Manchu had done the same around about the 8th and 9th of the previous month.[^p193-1]

### 1. The Emperor’s gifts which had been dispatched to the Supreme Pontiff, Father Bouvet, and Father Sabino are brought back to Beijing

Moreover he said that he had been sent as an envoy to Guangzhou, and that he had been ordered to be there within 25 days, to bring back to the palace the gifts which earlier had been intended for His Holiness, together with Father Bouvet and Father Sabino. For this he was carrying a decree written in Manchu and by order of the Emperor translated into Latin before he left the hunting lodge. In addition, he said that Father Barros was following him on the road and that the Emperor had given him all those records which His Majesty had caused to be taken from the archives and translated into a European language so that he together with Father Beauvollier could take them, by order of His Majesty, to the Supreme Pontiff.[^p193-2] This Manchu said farewell to us and prepared himself for the journey which he was to begin next day. This very same evening, Father Barros arrived from Manchuria.

October 8th. [Father Barros] on the 8th communicated to us verbally a version of the imperial decree by which Pursama was sent to Guangzhou. However he explained at greater length how much he had done with the other Jesuits to arrange that the gifts at least might be able to be sent by ship in the best way possible, to avoid any offence to His Holiness; but he did not have any success in that. He also showed us a fairly accurate account of his interrogation which had been written in Latin by Father Guéty which was to be taken to Europe. For as I related in the month of September this had been drawn up entirely by his <!-- PDF page 194 | book page 184 | manuscript page 447 --> own effort and written out by hand by Father Guéty. But it had not yet been corrected, although even the other one was not without mistakes and grammatical errors. And the reason was that it had been written entirely in the presence of the Prince who did not allow the Jesuits to speak to Father Guéty in a European language. The result was that they could do no more than suggest to him a few corrections to be made. To be perfectly accurate he said that three versions had been made because Fr. Guéty, while writing out the first in a fair copy had purposely concealed in the second one some matters which he had before allowed to flow from his pen in response to the grave threats which were levelled at him.

### 2. Fr. Guéty is severely punished for his deceitfulness and lies, with the Jesuits interceding for him without success

Then when the prince noticed that there was a disparity between the old and the new copy he flared up to the point of such impetuous anger that he ordered Fr. Guéty to be beaten with a stick, and when a young courtier declined to carry out the beating he beat the courtier himself, even though he was the son of a Gelao.[^p194-3] The Jesuits who were present casting themselves down on the ground, and striking their heads to the point of drawing blood begged for Fr. Guéty to be pardoned. But they could not prevent him from receiving slaps and kicks because the Prince complained that this was the basest deception of himself and his father.

Here, once again, very serious blame was imputed to the Jesuits, namely because their intercession was not sufficient to excuse his deceit, which Father Guéty imprudently persuaded himself would succeed, without its being noticed by such an astute prince.

But it behoves us sooner or later to turn the page and not just complain about Father Guéty, because it was himself that he let down, and made himself the laughing stock of the whole court, and also exposed all the Europeans who were most undeserving of it to the reproaches of all the Chinese and Manchus. There is no doubt that if even one of the Jesuits had acted so stupidly against any one of the Europeans in sight of the court in the way Father Guéty acted against all the Jesuits, then the missionaries of all the Orders would have broken out into complaints against him | and the whole of our Society. Why then do they demand that the Jesuits not only remain silent in the face of so many calumnies uttered by Father Guéty, but even undergo further punishment, because they were able only to protect Father Guéty from being beaten but not from being slapped?

On October 10th the mandarin Henkama with a crowd of scribes arrived at our northern house[^p195-4] and there in the room of the Father Superior, in the presence of Fathers Beauvollier and Barros, compared every one of the records with the translations; then after summoning to the same the brother of the Viceroy and the sons of the Governor of Guangdong, he communicated the Emperor’s decree. With the greatest diligence he recommended that in accordance with the Emperor’s wishes they should conduct the said Fathers with all care to Guangzhou, keeping in mind that they were sent by the Emperor and that without doubt they would report back whether they had been well or badly treated. Each of them, by means of their household servants, politely conducted the Fathers with such speed as the floods caused by the rain allowed. However, afterwards, the Governor with his ingrained disdain for foreigners showed them none of the kindness he was accustomed to show to other guests of the same status.

Before Father Barros returned to the College, he paid a formal visit to the Bishop of Conon and when the latter inquired about Father Guéty he answered that he lacked nothing that was needed in that time and place; that he was well fed and clothed by the person to whom he had been entrusted by the Emperor to care for him. There was no need now to send him fur-clothing for there he had received free of charge better clothing than the Jesuits normally wore. When he questioned further what was happening with regard to Father Guéty, he said: “He has deliberately blurted out many things about which he should have been silent, to the considerable embarrassment of himself and the Jesuits as well.” Moreover he himself and all the Europeans had been forbidden more than once by the Emperor, from the third of September on, to speak to or to have anything to do with the said priest.

This news caused the Bishop of Conon not a little concern, and sending Father Barros away briefly, he hurried to the Father Superior, begging him at great length to tell him what had been done by Father Guéty. The Father gave him no other answer except to say it would be better to find all that out from Father Guéty when he returned with the Emperor.

On October 14th the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon strongly urged Father Jartoux[^p195-5] to tell him about the condition and circumstances of Father Guéty. But it was not the intention of that Father to relate such matters to any other man as could not be heard without blushing and could not be spoken without offending one’s sense of modesty. And so the Father excused himself from being <!-- PDF page 196 | book page 186 | manuscript page 448 --> forced to offend the ears of the Most Illustrious Lord, adding that what had been done could not be undone. And he asked him to restrain his curiosity until Father Guéty’s return.

The 16th day of October was appointed by the Emperor for the beginning of the journey of Fathers Barros and Beauvollier. But since a doubt arose as to whether the Latin translations of the records should be authenticated by a seal, just as the rest written in Manchu and Chinese had been authenticated by order of the Emperor, it was necessary to wait for a courier so that this doubt could be resolved.

### 3. Fathers Beauvollier and Barros are sent to Europe by the Emperor

On October 17th the said two Jesuits began their journey to Rome between 9 and 10 in the morning, bearing with them the archival records and a translation of the records. About these records, one thing must be noted, that some have three seals and others two. The reason is that in those cases where the two mandarins, Zhao and Henkama, had been involved, they both imprinted their seals underneath the seal of the prince. Where only one of them had been involved in the business, they judged that it was to be signed by the seal of that one only. The records which had been translated into Latin and Italian were authenticated by both the Superior, Father Jean-François Gerbillon, and | Antoine Thomas[^p196-6] with their seals impressed on each page, and signed by the two notaries apostolic, Fathers Kilian Stumpf and Pierre Jartoux of the Society of Jesus.[^p196-7]

### 4. A letter relating to the Lord Patriarch’s journey

Today we received the first news of the Lord Patriarch’s journey. A letter came for Father Superior, Father Gerbillon, from the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing and also one from Father Appiani. Both of them were very brief. The former said that he together with the

<!-- PDF page 197 | book page 187 -->

Lord Patriarch had reached his house in Linqing on October 4th, after spending 37 days on such a short journey etc.

The other one mentioned something vaguely about wanting to commend himself without giving any reason. For this is what he wrote in French: that he does not know how to travel by two roads, and if others had not meddled in a certain matter, he would have brought it to a successful conclusion. It seems that he was speaking of that unfortunate business of the contracts. Then he added that the Abbate di San Giorgio had caught up with them on the 6th or 7th day after the Lord Patriarch left Beijing, because he wanted to consult the doctor about some ailment he had. But when he found that the doctor had gone to Manchuria he went back with the Lord Patriarch with the intention of consulting the doctor by means of letters from a distance. Also through the Lord Bishop of Beijing, we learned that the aforementioned Abbate had reached as far as Dezhou, as we recorded under the beginning of September.

### 5. Rumours and a letter from Guangzhou

At the same time, a letter came from Guangzhou, sent through Father Bouvet, stating that the Jesuits of Beijing in that place were being regarded as excommunicated because, as people were saying there, it was through their machinations that the Bishop of Conon was reduced to that state of misfortune into which his associates in the South thought he had been plunged. The letter also mentioned that the rumour was being spread abroad there that the Lord Patriarch would have been recalled to the Court by the Emperor if the Most Excellent Lord himself had not interposed his authority. From reports such as these the only conclusion is that rumours grow by being spread, and that when news is accepted as true without foundation, the fancies of others are joined to them with the freedom which seems to always accumulate new additions in Guangzhou.

With regard to the recall of the Lord Patriarch to Beijing, whether for better or worse, neither he nor the Jesuits ever heard anything either from Manchuria or from the Court. However there were some who were afraid he might be recalled, and perhaps they wanted His Excellency to use his authority to ask the Jesuits to prevent his recall. But the Lord Patriarch himself was above such ideas because he knew the extent he could rely on our sincere concern for his own person and never wrote anything of the kind.

Regarding the Bishop of Conon, his friends at Guangzhou had earlier spread the rumour that he was to be detained in Beijing for three years until all his writings had been examined by the Tribunal of the Rites, and so, but too late, they were trying to influence the Lord Patriarch not to leave the Capital and to delay his departure as long as he could. Whether any advice of this sort had reached the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, no one dares to say; although <!-- PDF page 198 | book page 188 | manuscript page 449 --> at about this time the said bishop had begun, little by little, in the silent hours of the night, to burn his more controversial writings, as he himself said at the time, and his servants noticed when they went to investigate a fire burning in his room in the darkness.

About this time that notorious trouble-maker Wang Baiduoluo[^p198-8] in discussion with a catechist belonging to our Northern Church asked if the Very Reverend Father Mezzafalce and two scholars who were formerly tutors of the Bishop of Conon had been cited. When the catechist said he knew nothing about this, he said: “This is certainly true. But there is nothing to fear. The two scholars are quite distinguished and one | of them is so talented that the Emperor will easily be influenced to favour him.” Wang Baiduoluo also predicted good fortune for himself, saying: “The Emperor already knows where I live in Beijing, (he was living with a eunuch belonging to the Palace, to whom he had attached himself on account of his name,[^p198-9] as if he were a blood brother) and he knows other things about me, and when he returns to the city he will make me a mandarin.” It is possible that this young man, by some trickery on the part of the prince, had been inspired with this hope, to prevent him from leaving Beijing before he himself returned. Certainly it did not occur to him that his hope had no foundation; after a few days he went out to meet the Emperor who was returning as far as Miyun,[^p198-10] which was a town a fairly short journey of three days from the Capital.

### 6. Calumnies against the Jesuits are spread by one who had been suborned

On October 20th we heard that there was a rumour being spread widely among our debtors who had been recently incited without good reason by Father Appiani’s agents, the chief one of whom was Gu Duomei.[^p198-11] He had been rejected in all the Churches and was well-known to the Lord Bishop of Beijing who found him obnoxious. Among the Christians he spoke openly, saying that the mandarins had been corrupted by the Jesuits with gifts etc. and in this way the Lord Patriarch was prevented from learning from the mandarins the actual words of the Emperor and from being able to have his words in their true sense conveyed to the Emperor. This, taking into consideration the speaker, appeared <!-- PDF page 199 | book page 189 --> to be complete nonsense, but it then eventually produced a similar idea in the mind of the Lord Patriarch. Father Buonaventura da Roma came to Beijing with almost exactly the same words after seeing His Excellency in the month of June 1707. This will be recorded in that place.

Secondly, he said that Father Sabino was the only ambassador and that it was not true that the Emperor had called Father Bouvet his yuxian,[^p199-12] since that Father was no less a European and no more a Chinese than Father Sabino himself.

Thirdly, he said that he had been commissioned by Father Bouvet to transact this business personally, namely that in the tribunal called the bingbu[^p199-13] he was to arrange for the name of Father Bouvet to be written before the name of Father Sabino, with the promise that whatever he spent in securing the cooperation of the brushes of the mandarins would be paid back generously to him by the Father, and that he had carried round the document from the Tribunal inside his gown[^p199-14] for several days.[^p199-15]

Fourthly, that he had been advised that according to the wishes of the Emperor and the Prince, there would be an investigation of him personally, and that he had no fears for he is conscious of his own rectitude and will answer freely if it comes to an interrogation.

Fifthly, that he is despised by the Fathers, even by the one at whose hands he received baptism.

Sixthly, he said that Father Gerbillon never wanted to avail himself of his services.

One would be hard put to know whether to marvel more at the effrontery of the speaker or at the audacity of the person who invented these lies. In the meantime, take note of one thing only, how he claims it was a business involving several days and says that he carried the document for the tribunal inside his gown for some time, that document from the Tribunal which he had secured by his own effort and for money. However, the matter was decided by the Emperor on January 1st when before dinner he nominated Father Bouvet to take the gifts along with Father Sabino, while appointing January 3rd as the <!-- PDF page 200 | book page 190 | manuscript page 450 --> day of departure. It was deferred to that day only because the Lord Patriarch had handed over for translation a letter of his to the Supreme Pontiff just when night was coming down and the palace gates were being closed; as has already been related more at length in its own proper place.[^p200-16]

Also this Gu Duomei is an insignificant little man, poor and destitute, as everyone knows. So he concocted a very improbable story, that the Father wanted to rely on his purse to procure by a bribe that an imperial edict which may be sealed only by the highest officials of the tribunal should be altered.

It is certainly a matter of regret that the Lord Patriarch chose Father Appiani as his assistant and was not horrified at the idea of transacting the business of his lord and of a papal legate by means of the worst dregs of the common people, and well-known and notorious sycophants. |

On October 29th there was definite news of the Emperor’s imminent arrival. The Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon showed that he wished to go out with the Jesuits to meet the Emperor as he returned from his hunting expedition. The Father Superior, J.F. Gerbillon, dissuaded him because he knew that a meeting with this Most Reverend Lord would not be acceptable, since the Emperor still felt aggrieved because he had been offended by him, and this person had made no effort so far to give him any satisfaction whatsoever.

### 7. The Jesuits meet the Emperor as he is returning from Manchuria

On October 30th we went out about three leagues to meet him. The Prince arrived first, bringing with him the young sons and grandsons of his father. When he saw us waiting in a field he turned aside from the road in our direction, hurrying his horse. The Superior J[ean] F[rançois] G[erbillon][^p200-17] and Father Kilian hurried to meet him. He said to them that we should give hospitality in our Eastern Church to Doctor Borghese, on his return from Manchuria, treating him with respect; that he had done nothing wrong but that this was what his father wanted.

A quarter of an hour passed then almost thirty minutes when the Emperor arrived with the Crown Prince riding by his side. When he looked at us on our knees beside the road about half a javelin-throw away, he enquired kindly whether all were well. At length in the throng of men Father Guéty also appeared, and behind him Doctor Borghese with Father Parrenin. But we hurried on and passed through the outer wall of the Imperial Villa so that we would be ready if His Majesty gave any commands. There Father Parrenin told us that yesterday <!-- PDF page 201 | book page 191 | manuscript page 451 --> an order had been given that when Father Mezzafalce came to Beijing, he was to be accommodated in the Eastern Church, as we had already been informed on October 15th. It became obvious then that the prince was being devious when he instructed us that Doctor Borghese was to live there.

Secondly we were told that Father Guéty was allotted by the Emperor to the house of the mandarin Henkama with the prohibition of any communication either with the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, or with any other European. It seemed to us that we needed to ask the Prince what he wanted done by us in regard to his unclear instructions regarding Doctor Borghese and Father Mezzafalce. But in fact he caused us to give up any desire to ask about this, when in the presence of the eunuchs and a crowd of courtiers, he made us a laughing stock. He mimicked Father Guéty, now imitating his way of speaking, now his mannerisms; how he made accusations against the Jesuits, how he stuck to his claims, how he denied what he had already said, how he took an oath on his breviary which he drew from his boots, how he dodged the blows or begged for mercy.

And so, covered with confusion, we retired to our house and Father Parrenin, late in the night, as ordered, conducted the doctor to the Eastern Church. He was grieving inconsolably, saying that he was confined in a prison, that he was suffering violence he did not deserve and was being treated like a slave. He was so distressed that he refused the supper he was offered, so that he had to pass the night with an empty stomach and a heart weighed down with grief.

This night and during the following day, that is on 31st October, Father Parrenin provided an account of events in Manchuria which we had either never heard about, or perhaps only vaguely. However it is not possible to include them here except in summary.

### 8. The Emperor is induced to fear the Europeans

[Father Parrenin told us:]

1. That the prince told him to his face, how earnestly he had warned his father to be on the alert against Europeans, who certainly intend at some time to occupy China just as they have taken over other kingdoms outside Europe. And when he replied that they had already lived peacefully in Macau for two hundred years, and also inside China, he replied that they would be satisfied if even after one thousand years they should achieve their purpose. He said there could be no doubt that the Lord Patriarch had come to explore whether the time was ripe. He also said that the prince boasted that | the gifts were prevented from being sent to the Supreme Pontiff through his efforts. The prince said the same to Father Barros and at about the beginning of August he spoke in a similar vein with Father Pereira, and so I shall confirm this point from a letter from each of them.

<!-- PDF page 202 | book page 192 -->

Father Pereira, on August 26th, among other things wrote in a letter to Father Kilian as follows:

The eldest son of the Emperor, as is his wont, in a very measured and serious tone of voice, asked me if His Holiness was looking to take over China. I replied equally seriously what I thought appropriate to remove these erroneous suspicions which the Emperor had also raised with me, but with much greater shrewdness than his son. The Lord Patriarch could have prevented all this if he wished, but each time he made things worse by his harsh responses to the Emperor.

In another letter of August 20th he wrote:

To change the subject, there is something else I should tell Your Reverence, that the Da Age (the Prince)[^p202-18] boasted that he knew all about our affairs through sending three men, one of whom was a Christian, to investigate us carefully, and lest we doubt that his agents had had done a good job, he mentioned various points which would be embarrassing to us, such as saying that he knew about the discord in the household of Wu Xiao Laoye arising from love of the Holy Law.[^p202-19]

Then the prince said to him that for five years now he had been telling his Father to beware of the Europeans (this agrees with the year when a mandarin of the Prince’s household was sent as a spy to Japan). He said that up to this he had not succeeded in convincing him, but that now his warnings were beginning to have some weight in the mind of the Emperor.

### 9. He threatens that he will send all the missionaries back home

2. That in Manchuria the Emperor said publicly that the Europeans had outstayed their welcome; but after that, he took care to inform the Jesuits who were there that the evil wrought by the Lord Patriarch had become deeply entrenched and required a desperate remedy so that it might be converted back to a spirit worthy of a man. He would send all the missionaries back to Guangzhou and leave them there for a year or two, where together with one another they could learn the error of their ways. Perhaps he would recall a few of them, while he would expel all the rest from China. In response to this draconian threat all the Jesuits, with their foreheads pressed to the ground, pleaded that His Majesty would not use such a harsh expedient. For otherwise it would come about that the Chinese Christians without exception would all desert the sacred teaching <!-- PDF page 203 | book page 193 | manuscript page 452 --> of Jesus Christ which they had once adopted. It is not known whether the petitions of the Jesuits were reported or not. However the Emperor, with the Holy Guardian Angel of the Chinese Empire guiding him, did not take up this subject again.

3. That the Emperor when he according to his custom divided up the spoils of his hunting did not designate a portion for the Europeans. But when a eunuch called this to his attention, he allotted one deer to be divided among them. The eunuch divided it into four portions among Fathers Pereira, Parrenin, and Barros and Brother Paramino. He left Father Guéty out. Shortly afterwards he explained to His Majesty how he had divided it and the omission which he had made. [The Emperor] replied: “You acted correctly; for I wanted him to be passed over, and I want the same done in the future if it happens that I give something to the Europeans.”

4. That Zhao Laoye who loves our holy religion and who arranged for his own brother and for members of his household to receive Baptism, told him with a groan that our mission is ruined.

5. That Father Guéty can scarcely be regarded as having written the Latin version of his interrogation. He merely spoke it in French, and he [Father Parrenin] supplied the Latin words, but in this he was so | ignorant that fairly frequently it was necessary to take the pen and to write the word, because even after it had been spoken by a native French speaker he was unable to write it correctly.

6. That he had warned Father Guéty not to put down things that were obviously false, for example, that Father Fontaney had sold cloth. And he answered that that was what he had heard. Therefore, argued the Jesuit, you should have put down that that was what you had heard.

7. That after the transcript had been written by Father Guéty, neither he nor the others were allowed to see Father Guéty and he was ordered to follow with the baggage, but was not admitted to the hunting-field.

8. That the Emperor arranged one day for the Jesuits to be told that in the future Europeans would be hated by the Chinese. They replied that they were absolutely sure that this would happen, but that they trusted in the protection of His Majesty who is the wisest of men. But he replied that even he would have no means of diverting the hostility of the Chinese.

9. That finally, on October 30th, at about 10 o’clock at night, he had greeted the Bishop of Conon and spoken a few words with him about Father Guéty, above all that the meaning of words had been changed, namely: they will move every stone. And that the Bishop of Conon had criticized what Father Guéty had said and done and was surprised that those words had been said publicly. However, in the meantime, he made excuses for himself and said he <!-- PDF page 204 | book page 194 --> did not remember that he had written anything to that priest which would harm and injure others.

### 10. The Bishop of Conon condemns Fr. Guéty, and his replies

On this last day of October, the Father Superior, J.F. Gerbillon, repeated and more clearly explained to the Most Rev Bishop of Conon, at his request, what had been communicated to him summarily by Father Parrenin the day before. The Most Illustrious Lord was visibly trembling, and in a state of great anxiety he made the following statements:

1. that he condemns Father Guéty;

2. that he would rather die than speak badly of anyone;

3. that he had already burned his writings which he brought with him from Fujian;

4. that he is looking for advice, and for the remainder of his manuscripts to be handed over for safe-keeping, with the request that they be preserved.

Next he was told by Father Superior how the Very Rev. Fr. Mezzafalce and the two Chinese scholars had been summoned, and of the great danger that threatened the mission on account of the imprudence of Father Guéty, the Lord Patriarch’s conduct, and opposition to the Emperor on the part of the Bishop of Conon himself. The Most Illustrious Lord replied to this by protesting strongly that an injustice was being done to those two Chinese scholars, formerly his teachers, saying that they had tried, using every effort to persuade him that the contrary meaning should be taken in the Chinese texts, but he would not abandon his own opinion.[^p204-20]

7. He said that because he had been accused by the Jesuits, he had performed the diao[^p204-21] at the funeral of the mandarin, but had been compelled to say much that would displease the Jesuits.[^p204-22]

8. He said he was waiting for someone to give a reply to this syllogism of his: The teaching of the Chinese is that which is taught in all the schools of the Chinese, and pure atheism is taught in all the schools; therefore …

Proof of the minor premise:

In all the schools there is taught the doctrine of Zhu Wengong;[^p204-23] and this is pure atheism; therefore …

<!-- PDF page 205 | book page 195 | manuscript page 453 -->

After being given this opportunity the Father Superior continued the discussion for two hours, and replied as follows to what had been said:

1. In the first place he advised the Bishop of Conon to answer the Emperor with gentleness and modesty, by which His Majesty will be conciliated, not irritated. To this end it is necessary for him to say as much as his conscience allows in favour of the teaching of the Chinese; in this way the final blow to the mission now in its death throes could be averted.

### 11. What the Emperor has demanded from the Lord Patriarch

Then he told him further, that all the Emperor required from the Lord Patriarch was not the approval of the Chinese rites, but that he should act in the capacity of a commissioner, by keeping an open mind and not declaring that he belonged to the opposing party, as he had openly done before the pagans and faithful alike. And also that he would listen to the Emperor, and | show he was ready to communicate to His Holiness the meaning which the Emperor declared to be in the Chinese terms and rites.

### 12. The principal ways in which the Bishop Maigrot had offended

Nor did the Father Superior fail to tell the Bishop of Conon that he had given offence to the Emperor precisely in this, that he, the Most Illustrious Lord, admitted he was ignorant of things Chinese, and yet rejecting any instruction would stand firm and force everyone to accept his opinion, even though it was the opinion of an ignorant man and one who admitted his ignorance. In this way, he said, the Emperor criticized him to his household: “It is absolutely amazing! A man from the experience of others, and by his own admission convicted of ignorance of our affairs, presents himself to dispute them. I try to instruct the man with kindness and explain to him the real meaning of our affairs; notwithstanding, he rejects my teaching as being false and chooses his own which he knows is the result of ignorance.” This is clearly intolerable for His Chinese Majesty.

### 13. What especially offended the Emperor

2. He should know that the Emperor is scandalized if missionaries speak ill of one another, and from those comments which the Lord Patriarch has frequently uttered, and which Fr. Guéty has now got off his chest, he has certainly come to the conclusion that some have conspired together to destroy others (the Jesuits) by any means that are possible.

3. It was the right thing to do to burn those papers which were useless except for kindling a fire by means of which the mission would go up in flames. He said he gives thanks to God that he did not permit it to enter the mind of the Prince <!-- PDF page 206 | book page 196 --> to intercept in the post the letters of the Lord Patriarch. If it had occurred to him, it is absolutely certain he would have done it, and certainly to the great harm of very many people.

4. He has no plan in mind, unless, as he has already said, to beg the Bishop of Conon, in so far as his conscience allows, to speak in favour of the Chinese rites.

5. He would definitely not take the manuscripts into his care, because in case there was an investigation he would be the first to be asked about them.

6. It seems absolutely in accordance with Christian charity that the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon should free the catechist Jin Xiu from blame, if in fact he did nothing to offend the Emperor.

7. It was not a suitable time for the missionaries to be forced to give time and attention to defending themselves when they should be working with their whole heart and all their strength for the salvation of the Chinese.

8. Finally, that Gordian knot can easily be undone by conceding the major premise and denying the minor; and negating the minor, by denying both premises.

### 14. The serious mistake made by the Bishop of Conon is proved by an excellent comparison

The Bishop of Conon was amazed at the liberty taken in this refutation. The Jesuit explained his reasons in this way: The major is false because it is not a doctrine which is being taught, but the style of Zhu Wengong. This is what the Emperor and the scholars of the Hanlin yuan[^p206-24] and others openly state. For example, if the works of M.T. Cicero[^p206-25] are being studied in Europe, e.g. On the Nature of the Gods, it is not considered that it is Cicero’s beliefs about the gods of the Gentiles that are being taught, but his mode of expression etc. In this regard, it is lawful in China to oppose both orally and in writing the views of Zhu Wengong, and there are in fact many books in China written by Chinese non-Christians which refute the views of Zhu Wengong.

With regard to the minor premise, it stated correctly that famous scholars, and above all the Emperor, say that Zhu Wengong explains much about Heaven in a purely material way, dealing with its physical and material origin and the interaction of things. If however it comes to a question of one Supreme Lord of all, he could not have failed to know about him, even though he may seem to <!-- PDF page 207 | book page 197 --> feign ignorance of God, so that he can display the keenness of his understanding in explaining the nature of things.

Against this the Bishop of Conon objected that the books called the Rijiang[^p207-26] printed under the Emperor’s name followed the atheism of Zhu Wengong. To this the Father replied with confidence: “Most Reverend Lord, I have read all of them which have been printed in the Manchu language in which ambiguity is avoided, and which show that the Chinese text is fairly often equivocal. I assure you that I have found in them clear knowledge of the true God, and in fact beyond all doubt. Why do you fail to quote the passages from the Rijiang which you say support your case?”

This was the end of the discussion of that insoluble problem.

Only the Most Illustrious Bishop added that he was afraid that the pontifical decree had not yet reached the Lord Patriarch, and that it prohibited more of the Chinese rites than he ever claimed to be prohibited.
