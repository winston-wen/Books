### 1. Fr. Francisco Pinto, Provincial of Japan, is excommunicated by the Lord Patriarch, who was under house-arrest in Macau

On the first day of this month, the Rev. Fr. Provincial of Japan, Francisco Pinto, told Fr. Kilian that he, more than any other Jesuit, had been harshly treated by the Lord Patriarch, since an order excommunicating him had been issued and publicly displayed. The Lord Patriarch from the moment he arrived in China had held this Father in esteem, and never used to mention him without some word of praise. However, from the moment that His Excellency began to be kept under housearrest in Macau, he turned over more bitter thoughts, looking for a chance of striking at the Jesuits collectively. He thought he had found such an opportunity in Fr. Pinto, on the grounds that when he had inquired whether the Father recognised that he personally exercised the authority of an apostolic visitor here in the Orient, etc., the Father was unwilling to answer the question; for he was aware that that a pastoral letter from the Most Illustrious and Reverend Primate of the Indies and Archbishop of Goa forbade under penalty of censure those under his jurisdiction to recognise the Most Illustrious Patriarch until he had produced the appropriate documents required by both the new and the old canons to establish his authority legitimately. I do not wish to pursue this matter at length; it is a long way from Beijing, and must be dealt with by the people in Macau.[^p669-1]

On the 2nd, a letter arrived from Rev. Fr. Buonaventura in Nanjing, as mentioned under September 7th. He was asking us to obtain permission for him to go to Macau and later to be able to return to China. Here is Fr. Kilian’s reply:

### 2. A letter from Father Kilian to Father Buonaventura da Roma

October 3rd

Very Reverend Father, the Peace of Christ.

The request Your Very Reverend Paternity sent from Nanjing on September 11th 1707 is right and good. We all wish the same thing, knowing that it is in the interests of Our Most Illustrious Ordinary,[^p669-2] of the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch, and of the mission as a whole that Your Reverence should openly carry out whatever either of the two aforementioned Lordships has recommended. That there is business to be discussed, there is no doubt;

<!-- PDF page 670 | book page 660 | manuscript page 738 -->

that anything can be done in practice is less certain, since there is no way of getting permission from the Emperor to travel to Macau and return to Guangzhou. If the ships should have brought us some reasonably good news, please write to us without delay so that we can gain access to the Emperor no matter how late the news may have been in getting to Your Reverence. It is not easy to explain why we do not dare ask for this permission, but that we do not dare to do so can be seen from the fact that we do not presume to make the request for any of our own people, nor for Father Muñoz, a Dominican. He is the one who is looking for a licence to go back to Fujian and Zhejiang. Some of them want to get back to their missions, others to help the Bishop of Macau in his recourse to Rome. But what they would like cannot, in the present state of affairs, be brought up with His Majesty. Before I explain why this is so, it may help to offer the shared judgement of the Fathers. We are of the view that there is a strong possibility that the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon may go to Macau and come back again, for he has already obtained permission to do so from His Majesty. It might be that on this occasion he could take Your Reverend Paternity as his companion, or if he himself was not going might possibly send Your Very Reverend Paternity on his business—to this the Viceroy would easily agree. But we cannot think of any other way of doing it. As for what might be done there, | please do not hesitate to consult with Fr. Monteiro and his companions, above all however with the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon, to whose prudence the business as a whole might be entrusted. Being as far away as we are, we are not sufficiently in a position to know what approach to take. Vague but rather disturbing news has reached us that Father Bénard might have overstepped the mark and with the help of the mandarins tried to intervene on behalf of the Lord Patriarch. If that is what happened, it was imprudent and excusable only by his ignorance of Chinese affairs. What reply can we make to the Emperor if he has been informed by the da ren[^p670-3] of the Fr. Bénard’s efforts, and questions us about it, and how are we ever going to get permission for anyone else to go to Macau to make contact with the Lord Patriarch and come back when they choose?

### 3. The Fathers are afraid that in their efforts to help the Lord Patriarch they might get in his way

I ask that what I am writing be correctly understood. It is not our wish to refuse all comfort or assistance to the Lord Patriarch; indeed day and night we try to work out how we can be of use to him. But we are afraid that our well-meaning efforts might in fact get in the way in the <!-- PDF page 671 | book page 661 | manuscript page 739 --> very turbulent circumstances in which business has to be conducted. In addition, we do not know what Rome might send us this year; we have no doubt that the recent English ships brought letters for the Lord Patriarch. We assume that they will have brought the pontifical decree against the Chinese Rites, and that the Lord Patriarch will not merely persist in what he has started but will rashly order the missionaries to face down the Emperor. And if this in turn is publicly known, whether sooner or later, what benefit can there be for us or for anyone else if permission to confer with the Lord Patriarch and then return to China has been obtained at the wrong time. To conclude briefly: we wish more than anything that Your Very Reverend Paternity could confer with the Lord Patriarch, but we are much more worried at the idea of asking the Emperor for permission to do such a thing. In our judgment another way has to be tried, as the many people in Guangzhou who are more acquainted with the situation will think best. I ask Your Reverence in the name of God not to think we are slighting him. Here and now it seems to us completely impossible to present to the Emperor, to good effect and with hope of success, such a proposition as requesting permission to go to Macau and return to Guangzhou. However, if tomorrow or in the next few days we get any clear and distinct information about the person of His Excellency the Lord Patriarch and which is of a kind which is likely to open the way, we will not hesitate to strive manfully to meet Your Very Reverend Paternity’s fair and holy demands.

I commend myself to Your Reverence’s holy sacrifices.

Beijing, on the Vigil of the Seraphic Saint Francis and the day after receiving Your Reverence’s letter.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify,

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.

### 4. Comments on the above letter of Fr. Kilian

Note 1. The reason why the Reverend Father looked for permission to go to Macau was that, in accepting the residence permit, he had promised not to leave China, and so would have been prevented from going to Macau by the mandarins.

Note 2. In the letter, it is asserted that the Reverend Father Muñoz and others asked the people in Beijing, one and all, to seek permission to return to their churches. Their letters clearly witness to this and there is no need for them to be transcribed here. But it was astonishing that men who had spent years in China and had experienced so many trials should seek to be restored to their churches less than three months after they had been expelled from China individually | and by name, and knowing that the cause of the mission had been appealed to the Supreme Pontiff.

### 5. Some of our men who were on the ship from Goa are called to the presence of the Emperor in Beijing

Note 3. Da ren refers to two Tartars who had been sent by the Emperor to take the Lord Patriarch to Macau, and had stayed there for quite some time; they were to carry out the Emperor’s order and wait for the arrival of the ship from Goa, to see whether there were any Europeans aboard who might be of use in the Emperor’s service and whom they were to transport immediately to the Court as they had done with Fathers Castner and Liebstain and Brother Stadlin. They left Guangzhou on September 7th, though nobody in Beijing knew that when Fr. Kilian wrote his letter.

### 6. The military guard stationed at the Lord Patriarch’s residence in Macau

Note 4. In fact, the rumour had already flown to Beijing that some Europeans had arranged with the Governor’s auditor and with the mandarin of Xiangshan that on their authority the military guard should be removed from the Lord Patriarch’s residence. One of the da ren, Foiorò by name, solemnly confirmed this when he got back from travelling with the Emperor, who was still engaged in his hunting. This action we neither approved of, thinking it dangerous, nor disapproved of because of the respect we would most willingly have shown him had we been allowed to do so. This fact was mentioned to show to the Rev. Fr. Buonaventura how careful one must be in asking for a permit at this time, when this rumour could easily have been prejudicial to the Lord Patriarch and anyone else whom he admitted to do business with him face to face.

I shall now add the other letter on the same subject which Fr. Kilian wrote to the Lord Bishop of Ascalon on October 3rd.

### 7. A letter from Fr. Kilian to the Bishop of Ascalon

It is almost impossible in Beijing to write anything at this time and in these circumstances which require exchanging either immediate or frequent answers. Divine Providence has so arranged that although sometimes it does impede our human efforts, in the meantime it is arranging for more-than-human help to be given, whereby ruins which are beyond human remedy can be repaired with a solidity which is beyond nature. Besides the eagerly awaited letters from Your Most Illustrious Lordship, no letters have so far come from either Goa or Europe; as of today we have heard not a single word. All we know is that some which were dispatched to Beijing from Guangzhou on August 5th by October 11th had not yet turned up in Nanjing, so who can foretell when they might get here? Rev. Fr. Buonaventura da Roma writes from Nanjing to say that he is on his way to Guangzhou, and that he would like to go on from there to Macau to finish his business with the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch and the Most Illustrious Bishop of Macau, and then <!-- PDF page 673 | book page 663 | manuscript page 740 --> come back into China. With this in mind he asked me and the other Fathers to apply for a special permit for him, in case the mandarins either refuse to let him leave or will not let him back in when he wishes.

### 8. The Emperor’s anger is still smouldering

Our Fathers can be in no doubt that his application is no less timely than necessary by reason of the affairs which that Father is dealing with and the interests of His Lordship the Bishop of Beijing, the Lord Patriarch, and the mission itself. But the Emperor has been absent for a long time, and even if he were to be back in Beijing soon, it would hardly if at all be possible to ask for such a permission. Some are of the opinion that the difficulties in dealing with the Emperor are somewhat less, and that his anger has all but subsided. They are deceiving themselves: for it is a solid and unalterable fact that His Majesty | has untrammelled power to govern in his own empire even regarding Europeans, just as he can issue orders to the Chinese and the Tartars. Nothing will enrage him more than learning from his staff that even three or four Europeans, contrary to imperial law, are not acting in accordance with his wishes, and there is no charge more difficult for us to rebut than were the Emperor to say that the Europeans are stony-hearted and pig-headed. His failure to take action against them would become a scandal to the Chinese and his officials would correctly adjudge that they should be expelled from China.

### 9. The difficulty of obtaining from the Emperor any favour for Europeans

None of the things I have just mentioned worries Rev. Fr. Buonaventura in the slightest. He is pleased to state that from the midst of a shared difficulty, the solution to an individual problem can shine out all the more clearly. But in all honesty, it seems to us impracticable to seek for such a permission here and now, besides which we do not know with any certainty what has happened so far as the Lord Patriarch is concerned, or what ought to be done once he has received the letters from Rome. Nor can we make any announcement or give any reply concerning the other missionaries in Guangzhou, not just because we do not know what the da ren might secretly have sent to the Emperor, but also because not a single missionary—Jesuit, secular priest, Franciscan, or Augustinian—has written to us to say that he is willing to defer to His Majesty and come to Beijing to accept the piao, or to say that he will have nothing to do with the permit and prefers to get out of China. Since after so many months we are still ignorant of such matters, what approach could we make to the Emperor to ask favours from him? The anger of kings is easy to inflame, and, once in flames, very hard to extinguish. If I may say what I think, it is almost a miracle for a gentile monarch who has been provoked so often and so sharply <!-- PDF page 674 | book page 664 | manuscript page 741 --> not yet to have vented his fury. It will do the mission no good if we either make objections or proposals which will arouse the man’s rage.

### 10. The Bishop of Beijing shuts his eyes and pretends nothing is happening

There is another point which, although it does not involve Rev. Fr. Buonaventura personally, gives us reason to be afraid that in addition to the other obstacles we would not be able to seek the desired permission from the Emperor. This arises because of the Reverend Franciscan Fathers in the province of Shandong who quite openly say, and write, that they have received the royal piao but have not accepted the condition that in the meanwhile they should not preach against the Chinese Rites—and their actions match their words. Fr. Fernández does not accept our Fr. Franchi’s admission; he says that Fr. Franchi has incurred censures because he did not promulgate the Patriarch’s regulation, and so has Your Lordship for daring to lodge an appeal against it. Others carry on as best they can, trying to bind the Christians to the regulation, boasting that so far no harm has come of it. Your Lordship will not be unaware how many anxieties things of this kind cause us, especially because the Lord Bishop of Beijing is at least turning a blind eye to it. I wrote to the Reverend Fathers Fernández and Castorano; Fathers Régis, Bouvet, and Jartoux wrote to the Lord Bishop, and in addition swore an oath that what those Fathers gratuitously alleged as a cloak for their own freedom was simply false from start to finish. However, the Bishop gave them a cold answer—that he himself would make sure that a too-fervent zeal did not stand in the way of the conditions which had been offered but not accepted. Nobody doubts that the Emperor wishes to place them under an obligation—an obligation in law, not merely an agreement. We indeed do not wish to raise the issue whether the obligation is in law or not; nonetheless they should not fail to understand that they are bound also in charity to avoid putting themselves or the Christians in danger, at least in the short term. However it turns out, we cannot be without apprehension in case the Prince through his spies gets to know what is going on in the next province, and then, in the Chinese manner, involves everyone from the same Order in the disaster. That being said, Fr. Buonaventura’s petition is fair, and we shall make a wholehearted effort to have it implemented | if we can find any grounds on which we might succeed. One thing occurs to the Fathers to ask Your Lordship: if by any chance you do get to Macau, could you please take the Reverend Father as your companion? We think the Viceroy will agree without difficulty if Our Fathers who are held there by order of the Emperor support him, on which point I am writing to Fr. Monteiro.

### 11. Fr. Buonaventura da Roma: how much he has done for the mission and the Society of Jesus

We know that the Rev. Fr. Buonaventura has done as much as anyone could ever do for the mission and the Society, and we shall not forget how we simply cannot repay the debt we owe him on this account. I have exhausted Your Lordship’s patience in writing such a long letter pleading someone else’s cause. Please forgive me. I have not however raised anything foreign to Your Illustrious Lordship’s interest in anything which contributes to the good of the mission. I know that the Angel of the Church in the province of Jiangxi is a pillar of our Holy Church in this kingdom. ### 12. Father Domingos de Britto’s letter to Fr. Stumpf

To Father Kilian Stumpf,

Today, October 4th, I got your letter of September 3rd. I am pleased to hear from you that the transcripts that I sent you from here have arrived safely. The rest of Your Reverence’s letter consists of a vehement invective against the Jesuits in Guangzhou because we have not as yet left here to go to Beijing and seek the piao. That kind of invective deserves praise in that it comes from the immense zeal which Your Reverence <!-- PDF page 676 | book page 666 | manuscript page 742 --> has for the good of these missions. Nonetheless it is based on two mistaken assumptions: the first is that Your Reverence supposes that we Jesuits in Guangzhou have persuaded ourselves that there is no need to go to Beijing to apply for the piao: this was only the belief or fantasy of the Reverend Friars, not of the Jesuits. Secondly, Your Reverence supposes that the order from the mandarins permitting us to go to Beijing arrived in April. If the order reached the mandarins then, why have they taken so long to inform us? And for what reason did they prevent Father Porquet from going to apply for the piao at the beginning of September, saying that there was no such order from the Emperor, and being unwilling to accept what Your Reverences said in your letters? After the arrival of your letter dated December, we set out at once for Nanjing, and were stopped in Shaozhou in the month of February. We gathered that letters of that kind were not enough | to enable us to continue on our way. We waited and waited for the Emperor’s zhi but in the end it never arrived. Without it, what would have happened to us all would be what happened in Guangzhou to Fr. Porquet and to those who followed us to Shaozhou the first time. It follows from all this that Your Reverence should not be surprised that we have not been able, nor have we dared, to set off for Beijing. It also follows that if the Jesuits in Guangzhou were to be expelled from China with a great crowd of clerics and friars, it would be no fault of ours nor because we wished to leave the mission, but through mo aihe,[^p676-5] that is to say by force or compulsion.

That this agrees with the original,[^p676-6] I so testify,

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary

### 14. The position of the Lord Patriarch and his staff in the negotiations about the Chinese Rites

I believe I should warn the reader somewhat more at length at this point about the chain of argument which the Patriarch’s people constructed. They had fixed that they should introduce the decree which Clement XI refused to sign, and, keeping quiet on that point, they once more took up the Chinese question.[^p678-8] To achieve their end, they did not want the Lord Patriarch in China to formally[^p678-9] accept any of the various things which the Jesuits had been able to bring forward. This policy was carefully carried out, as can be quite clearly seen in these Acta from December 170510 through August 1706. In addition, when he had himself come to the Kingdom, where the crucial point of fact concerning the controversies could and should have been settled, the Patriarch’s people managed to get the Lord Patriarch to give strict orders that the discussions should not be held in the presence of the Emperor nor any of the controversial points treated. He also refused to allow a fresh request to be made to the Emperor for a new declaration, yet his entourage said that the earlier one was not at all adequate. This is what took place almost until the end of July, although one might also include August 1706—during that month the Patriarch’s people made great efforts to make sure that the Patriarch did not accept, nor write to the Supreme Pontiff about, the declarations on each specific point made by the Emperor and given to the Patriarch, who had not asked for them, did not want them, and even repudiated them unread. But that is what the Emperor was ordering him to do.

Then, both while the Lord Patriarch was at court and while he was travelling through the province of Shandong, it was being said here, there, and everywhere that the Lord Patriarch had produced a decree against the Rites. His problem was whether, and if so when, it was to be promulgated. More than once in September and October the Lord Bishop of Conon told the Jesuits in Beijing that he had said to the Lord Patriarch that it should not be promulgated, not even if a decree from the Supreme Pontiff were to arrive, because of the danger presently arising from the Emperor, who is offended, and the Patriarch <!-- PDF page 679 | book page 669 | manuscript page 744 --> who has offended him. The Lord Bishop judged that the matter should be referred to two or three missionaries of really good judgment, to consider the matter and work out some way in which it could be validly introduced without being published. Similarly, the Lord Bishop of Beijing who from January 1705 until that November had sat at the Lord Patriarch’s side, and both in writing and speech has frequently expressly stated that the Patriarch had said, both in writing and conversation, that he would publish fearlessly—a course of action from which he tried to dissuade him.

### 15. The steps leading to a heightening of the danger to the mission

However, after the expulsion from China in December 1706 of the Bishop of Conon, Mezzafalce, and Guéty, and the threat that this would happen to everyone who did not possess a piao nor was prepared to accept one because they were opposed to the Chinese Rites, the Patriarch’s staff tightened the noose: either they would be arrested with the others or they would themselves take the others out of the mission with them. They urged the promulgation of a decree, not that of the Pope which had not arrived, but that of the Patriarch, investing it with authority by saying that it kept to the decision recently made by the Holy See and confirmed by Clement XI, though no such decision was ever produced. In order to inject into the missionaries a reasonable fear of the censures under which such a decree was promulgated, they intimated in writing and in conversation | that it was defined as a matter of religion, and as such constituted a new article of faith, being based on an oracle of the Supreme Pontiff which the Lord Patriarch possessed in himself, and was therefore to be obeyed by anyone who did not want to separate himself from the bosom of Holy Church.

### 16. Some missionaries, with the assent of the Lord Patriarch, accept the imperial permit

This ploy still did not prevent some missionaries, relying on solid foundations in canon law and theology, from paying due respect to the patriarchal decree while accepting the piao and staying in China. At the same time some of the Patriarch’s supporters, professing their allegiance to the decree, refused the imperial permit and so were expelled by the Emperor. It was obvious that the chain was losing its hold and that the Patriarch’s supporters, having many burdens including exile to bear, now sought means to enable some of them to stay on in the mission. To this end the Lord Patriarch either allowed or even ordered two of them to accept the imperial permit—Reverend Fathers Castrocaro and Serravalle. Fr. Buonaventura himself was dispatched from the Patriarch’s side with the express command given in person to go to Beijing and ask for the permit.

### 17. Why the missionaries in Guangzhou hesitated to come to Beijing and receive the

By now many Jesuits had accepted the permit which allowed them to remain in China; seven Franciscans along with the Lord Bishop remained undisturbed in piao the province of Shandong; and with the knowledge and approval of the Lord Patriarch three others, Fathers Castrocaro, Serravalle, and Buonaventura, were taken into the protection of the Court. Now several more appealed against the patriarchal decree, and among those in Guangzhou there naturally grew the desire that they too should accept the diploma, so as to safeguard both themselves and their churches. Since, however, the Patriarch’s people did not dare to appear in Beijing, they gently persuaded others that there was no real reason to undertake that journey: now that the imperial anger had been laid aside and he had recovered his usual clemency he would call the Lord Patriarch back to the Court. Fathers Pereira and Antoine Thomas had been sent by him and were already on the way to make peace, and there were nothing but sounds of simple kindness and cooperation, so it was reasonable for those in Guangzhou to wait without worrying for a happy ending. Such considerations were willingly listened to, since everyone was delighted not to have to incur the expense and to avoid the hardships which they alleged would be encountered on a journey of nine hundred French leagues in each direction. Hence the delay of the missionaries. But, after hearing Fr. Frossoloni’s account along with the stories of the people from Shandong, when the rumour went around that the permit had been granted there without any condition being attached, the Patriarch’s party greatly inflated the Emperor’s acquiescence; yet even so they could not tempt others to apply for the permit. So a new kind of leverage was found: on the assumption of the Emperor’s tolerance, the Patriarch’s people wished to show to all and sundry how right the decree had been whereby much danger had been avoided, what a bad mistake it was to have appealed against it, and what equivocation was involved in accepting the permit with its conditions added. These fresh threats cried out for its reiteration and for a wider publication of the censures and redoubled attacks which would be imposed on those who criticised it.

### 18. The reasons why Fr. Buonaventura da Roma was excommunicated etc

The extent to which the Patriarch’s people spread the idea of the Emperor’s clemency is obvious from the many instances mentioned already and will also be seen in the signed reply of the Lord Patriarch to the Commissary of the Franciscan Friars of 11 January 1708. How frightening the flashes from the new thunderbolts were can be seen in the letter from Fr. Monteiro | about Fr. de Goville already cited, but also in the case of Fathers Castner and Liebstain <!-- PDF page 681 | book page 671 --> in Shaozhou, and again, clearest of all, in the case of Father Buonaventura. He was never accused, summoned, or given a hearing, but was declared to have lied under oath, excommunicated, and deprived of his upkeep as a member of the Patriarch’s staff before any living soul in Guangzhou had heard a word about his case. Fr. Monteiro is quite right to say that this presented a fresh disincentive to the missionaries in Guangzhou against daring to take a single step towards accepting the permit. They could see that Fr. Buonaventura had been condemned contrary to every legal procedure and punished—the same man whom the Lord Patriarch had, from his own ship, from his very side and in his own words, sent to the Emperor to receive the piao so that he could thereby save the three patriarchal houses in Nanjing, Hangzhou, and Xiaoshan. Who would not be afraid of so precipitous a judgment and of a power like a thunderbolt such as God has never given to any man?

### 19. The Lord Patriarch’s supporters praise the Emperor’s clemency but with what in mind?

The reader will be looking for some further reason why the Patriarch’s supporters, in word and deed, should have tried to laud the Emperor’s clemency, given that from what has so far been said this behaviour would indeed succeed in furthering the end they had in view, but that the end itself seems not worth the trouble. I reply that there was indeed a hidden agenda, which was the principal one: the Patriarch’s people, overconfident in their wisdom, had tricked the Bishop of Conon into coming to Beijing, and there they had him presented as a most learned man, capable of replying to any of the points in dispute. In so doing they created a breach in their wall, which opened the way to all the events which ensued: the humiliations, the detention, the ignominious questioning, and the exile of the Bishop of Conon and his companions. Yet, by brandishing the decree in a manner which was inopportune and hostile before everybody, they brought about their own expulsion as well as making the other missionaries abandon the vineyard, leaving the mission defenceless against the final imminent threat. The result was they no longer had an excuse, nor did they believe that any excuse would stand unless they accused others as the sources of all these wrongs. So they tried to attribute the sad state of the mission and the harm done to souls not to the anger of the Emperor but to the malicious accusations of other missionaries. ### 29. The favour of mandarins should be invoked cautiously

It was just like that when, a few years ago, the scholars in Shaanxi twice closed the gate of their city in the face of Fr. Provana and got ready to lay violent hands on him. ### 38. How to deal with the Emperor

From his own point of view, a gentile Emperor, lord of his empire, does not care at all what might be laid down in the canons and councils of the Europeans, but relies upon what he regards as his natural right. He does so modestly, and if he is not granted a hearing, he will assert his rights to the Europeans at the same time as he invokes it against those whom we have seen he has expelled. If we are not going to try to compel the Supreme Pontiff to follow his laws—and we cannot do that—then all we can do is to entreat him not to turn away a gentile monarch who wishes to foster the Church of God, but to generously allow the Supreme Pontiff to look again at the case which his commissary cavalierly dismissed; we make this appeal not for our own sakes, but for the sake of souls and the Church of God. And besides, the law of nations recognises the standing of anyone, even barbarians, insofar that they can both claim an exemption against a legate who has acted wrongfully and bring an action against him. It is all too obvious that neither the Most Christian nor the Catholic King[^p694-38] would have decreed that there would be no recourse (to say the least) against the Lord Patriarch had he done in their countries what he has done here. If, however, it seems necessary for the case to be treated under this formality of an appeal if it is to be admitted, as if the Emperor had lodged an appeal, and, in virtue of the appeal, the power of the Legate had been suspended, then let those who are sticklers for legal form examine it. It is one thing to require due process so that an action may be brought and responded to in a Roman court; it is quite another to grant the right and obligation to do so before the learned world and before God. I believe there was enough to create an obligation before God and the whole heavenly court for the Lord Patriarch to desist from exposing the mission to a danger of which by his own admission he is well aware since the gentile monarch, in full control of his empire, has provided him orally with an authoritative interpretation of the rites and of the words they contain. In addition he ordered that the explanation be sent through the Patriarch to the Supreme Pontiff with an authoritative statement that European missionaries who object to the Rites are not to be tolerated in his empire. Furthermore, he threatened that he would send his explanation through other people when that would be more effective. Far more effective indeed, when he expelled those who were unwilling to wait for the Supreme Pontiff’s replies to that explanation, decreeing that all of them to a man were to be expelled unless they agreed that for the time being they would wait, and when he stated that his vassals would be considered as rebels if they had unfaithful to their native rites.

### 41. A letter from the Lord Patriarch to the Bishop of Macau

Seeing that your Most Illustrious Lordship has forgotten the obedience you owe to the Holy See and the obligations of your episcopal ministry, I find myself constrained to ask you to consider not so much the harm that you have caused to the Holy Father in the person of myself, but rather the deplorable state in which your flock is enmeshed in censures. Is it possible that you can be at peace in your conscience? ### 54. A letter from Fr. Antoine Thomas to the Lord Patriarch

Most Excellent Lord, After I received Your Excellency’s letter dated January 18th of this year to the Fathers of the Society of Jesus living in Beijing,[^p708-57] and some further later ones, I chose to reply at some length, as both the situation and the protection of the endangered mission required of me. This letter is the fruit of a long and difficult struggle against feelings of exhaustion; I dare to offer it to you out of love for the mission which is on the point of perishing. Your Excellency must indulge my prolixity as being a sincere and necessary liberty when writing on a matter of such consequence: without freedom in setting the matter out, or if I had to be more concise, I could not persuade you of the remedy for so many enormous ills. ### 58. The Bishop of Conon rightly proscribed by the Emperor

In truth, it was too much to expect of His Illustrious Lordship that he would make such progress in his own understanding as to accept the Emperor’s teaching about the meaning of the words and rites of his empire, as the outcome showed; nor can it be concluded from what actually happened that Your Excellency in making that proposal intended anything of the kind. ### 59. Demonstrably ignorant, he refuses to be taught the sense of Chinese words by the Emperor

When the Emperor in many discussions had shown up his lack of expertise in Chinese literature, and had taught him with great kindness, in our presence, the meaning of the Chinese rites and customs, and that the word Tian meant the true living God whom we Christians adore and for whom we use the term Tianzhu, the Most Illustrious Lord persisted in his view, from which he could not be shifted throughout two hours of the Emperor’s explanations. ### 73. Fr. Joachim Bouvet’s book: its argument

This book was only in manuscript. It made just one assertion: that both in the ancient and in contemporary Chinese texts the one true God is known by the words Tian and Shangdi. ### 75. The Patriarch prohibits it

### 78. He avoids an inquiry into the Chinese controversy, despite being invited to do so by the Emperor

### 82. The Patriarch orders the Christians’ petitions to be torn up

Some years ago the Christians in Beijing sent an oath to the Holy See concerning the Emperor’s intentions in the matter of the rites to be conducted in honour of Confucius and of ancestors. I do not think that Your Excellency knew at the time that the seal of confession had been broken with the penitent neither consulted nor having given his consent. So while Europeans, Chinese, and Tartars—the highest and the lowest in the land—were expecting some proposal to be made to the Emperor regarding the rites, as the Emperor had wanted up until now, Your Excellency wrote to him in Italian falsely accusing three French priests of a capital offence and denounced the entire Portuguese nation as an enemy of His Majesty seeking to do him harm. To be sure, this point had nothing to do with the controversies, but I thought it should not be left out because I have undertaken to put right in front of your eyes the way in which, with what I take to be your good intention to do your best for the mission, you are in fact driving it to certain ruin. Your Excellency believed that the mission could rise again from the ground, safe and sound, if the three Fathers had been condemned for a capital offence, and all the Portuguese had been expelled and the city of Macau destroyed.

### 86. The Emperor’s good judgment and fairness

During it, he heard the Emperor saying that he was afraid that the Europeans, misunderstanding the Chinese books and rites, were putting others in danger, and at last hearing it from the Emperor’s own mouth he recognised that His Majesty had done no more than explain to him the meaning of the words, and the intention and the performance of the rites. ### 88. How the Bishop of Conon behaved towards the Emperor

### 90. The Emperor’s testimony about the meaning of the word

Because of the events I have just described, Your Excellency could not have been in any doubt that the Emperor’s anger had been aroused both by the Tian Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon and by yourself. Nothing could be clearer than this statement of the Emperor’s or of greater strength so far as refuting the account given by the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon. ### 92. The Emperor’s wisdom and sound judgment

Your Excellency wants the discussions to be ended by yourself, not by the Emperor; that is what the Jesuits too want. They protested that all that should be asked of the Emperor is what the word Tian means when it occurs in Chinese books and in daily use, and, do they venerate their ancestors with civic respect or with sacred worship? For the rest they await the decision of the Holy See as to whether it <!-- PDF page 736 | book page 726 --> is permitted to use this or that word, and this or that rite. The Emperor himself has insisted that his statement sets out nothing more than the correct meanings of the Chinese words and rites, established by true learning and proclaimed by his own indisputable authority; he leaves to the Pope the right of deciding if the meanings, thus clearly established and authoritatively stated, are truly in harmony with the Christian Law. Despite all this, we have been forbidden to have such a conversation in the presence of the Emperor with the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, who for all these years has been warring with us to the detriment of the Holy Law and misrepresenting the Emperor’s authority. While Your Excellency had appointed him as judge in the matter of the words, writings, and rites of the Chinese, nonetheless, addressing yourself to the Emperor in person at the end of June, you announced that the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon had been invited by you to explain himself to His Majesty; apparently he was to give an explanation in public before the entire Court. If it was no fault in Your Excellency giving His Majesty to understand that there would be a future conversation, there certainly was no fault if the Jesuits endorsed the opinion given in the Emperor’s judgment about the correct interpretation of their Chinese affairs. It was not us who had asked for that conference.

### 93. The Bishop of Conon’s lack of knowledge is demonstrated

Finally, as I have related above, when the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon had been brought to Tartary, the Emperor tested him in spoken Chinese, the written language, and books; in all of them he found him deficient. He attempted to get him to present the reasons for his disapproval of the Rites and got nothing but silence. He taught him the meaning of the terms and rites and found him to be stubborn, to the extent that he would say nothing other than that he did not understand much about such matters, but that the little he did understand was quite sufficient for him to have little regard for the Emperor’s learning, authority, and majesty. He did not actually say the last phrase but his actions were enough to make his view clear. He stuck to his point that the rites are religious and therefore wrong because Tian and Shangdi for the Chinese do not mean “God,” and it was quite extraordinary for the Emperor to say that Fr. Ricci had proved, on conclusive evidence taken from the classical books, that contrary to some interpreters Tian does refer to the true God. The Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon told the Emperor that he himself had never read Ricci’s book, though he had pretended this to be the case when he flourished it in Rome and before the whole European world.

<!-- PDF page 737 | book page 727 | manuscript page 782 -->

### 94. The Emperor’s view and testimony about the word , the honour paid to Confucius, etc., is treated with contempt by the Lord Patriarch

The Emperor refused to be made to appear foolish by the Illustrious Bishop of Conon’s claim of ignorance and to Tian allow the subject to be avoided. With his own brush he wrote that Tian had the same meaning as Tianzhu; that the rites paid to | Confucius and the dead were merely civil; that no petitions were made in front of the tablets of the dead; etc. He also presented his decree to confound the Most Illustrious Bishop and, as a formal legal document, to help both Your Excellency and the Holy See to understand. In so doing, not only had he opened the way to ending the controversies which have so far been concerned with matters of fact about the Chinese but he also presented Your Excellency with an opportunity for lessening any ill will and expunging all past offences, if only you had said that that you had been taken in by the reputation for learning attributed to the Bishop of Conon. This was a point which His Lordship himself had openly conceded, for when the Emperor asked him why he had been introduced by Your Excellency with such praise and yet was found to know nothing, both on his admission and in the view of others, he said it was because Your Excellency knew him only by repute. However Your Excellency preferred stubbornly to defend your earlier mistake of praising the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon rather than to make any concession which might help the Chinese Church.

### 95. The Lord Patriarch’s irremediable stubbornness

After the decree I have described, the Emperor left no stone unturned in trying to deal with another especially abrasive facet of Your Excellency’s personality, persisting over a period of several days through the mandarins in the hope of obtaining a more flexible reply from Your Excellency, who might be made to admit, at least hypothetically, that if someone had made a mistake in introducing the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, such a person could also well find it was only right that it should cause offense. But the Emperor was laying aside any such right so that he could explain the rites and language of his empire in the sense in which the whole empire accepted them from him. This was to no avail. Your Excellency could have defused the Emperor’s anger with one soft word without any loss of dignity on your part. Instead you chose to speak to him harshly and to arouse his fury; you refused to listen to the mandarins trying to persuade you to be more sensible or to your staff pointing out the dangers. When the interpreters refused to carry out their part in offering such resistance, you resorted to sign language since you could not speak your mind. To demonstrate to the mandarins your inflexibility you threw yourself out of your seat onto the ground, with your eyes shut and your ears stopped. You made it clear that you would not consider any more accommodating reply. What would the mandarins have <!-- PDF page 738 | book page 728 | manuscript page 783 --> said if perhaps the interpreters had covered up for you? But Your Excellency’s views could be concealed no longer. They said that Your Excellency’s business was completely at an end and threw the reply you had given them back in your face as if to say such insane mental rigidity just has to be left alone; there was no point in looking for any further cure for it.

### 96. The Emperor sends the record of events to the Supreme Pontiff and recalls his envoys, etc

It happened, though, that the Emperor’s patience made him delay; later, in a calmer frame of mind, Your Excellency was able to reply in a somewhat more moderate manner. But moderation expressed with such an effort could not be other than suspect to the Emperor. So, to ensure that the Emperor of the World, the greatest ruler outside Europe, would not be regarded as appealing to the Supreme Pontiff from a position of inferiority, he sent the records of all his actions to the Holy See, carried by two Fathers. And, punishments had to be meted out to ensure that His Majesty was not further dishonoured in his own empire, looked upon as a laughing-stock by a few Europeans who were rejecting his proffered explanation of the Chinese Rites when the most learned men in China accepted them as being in accord with the books, and that every living soul in the empire would honour him as an authority. So, firstly he dissolved the legation to the Supreme Pontiff.[^p738-71] Secondly, he forbade Your Excellency to delay the day of your departure from the kingdom. | Thirdly, he restricted your movements more narrowly. Fourthly, he ordered the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon to be placed under detention.

### 97. The Emperor summons the Fujian scholars to be interrogated

Fifthly, he imprisoned Your Excellency’s catechist along with two Christians well-known for their dishonesty; their services, with Your Excellency’s knowledge, were used by Fr. Appiani to stir up trouble. Finally, when Fr. Guéty’s imprudence gave him an opening, he summoned for examination the scholars of Fujian, the teachers of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon; the Reverend Fr. Mezzafalce; and Fr. Appiani himself. Under Your Excellency’s own eyes, he then had them brought back in chains to the Criminal Tribunal in Beijing. These beginnings of the ills soon to afflict the Chinese Church, which I have touched upon above, did not move Your Excellency at all, so far as giving any satisfaction which might have averted the events which could bring disgrace down upon your own person and upon the Supreme Pontiff, along with the ultimate destruction of the mission.

<!-- PDF page 739 | book page 729 | manuscript page 784 -->

### 98. The Lord Patriarch throws all the blame and the destruction of the mission on the Jesuits

But what do these things matter? Your Excellency predicted the destruction of the mission and put the blame on the Beijing Jesuits. You defended the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon and wished to crown him twice over. But who deserves this harsh judgment? Blame cannot be distributed as loads are divided among baggage animals; those loads are lessened by being distributed onto other backs, whereas that other load, projected onto the innocent, increases the burden on the person distributing the blame. The Beijing Jesuits have not sinned and their labours were of great advantage to Your Excellency. As the whole Court can confirm, the Emperor was placated by their prayers and industry on many occasions, and if any misfortunes were prevented it was by their tears. If Your Excellency were to accuse them of some negligence in discouraging or encouraging either the Illustrious Bishop of Conon or himself, it would perhaps be bearable. But for you to spare yourself, and to connive with the Bishop of Conon in accusing the Jesuits of the most serious crimes, as if by means of some horrible contrivance they had stirred up all the misfortunes which afflicted apostolic ministers so viciously, would be a calumny without parallel. We can only attribute it to a profound melancholy veiling full knowledge of events and circumstances.

### 99. Why the Lord Patriarch’s decree should never have been published

If only the heat of Your Excellency’s thoughts and suspicions had gone no further than railing against the Beijing Jesuits rather than letting loose the floods and in a fatal tempest driving the mission to certain shipwreck! People write to tell us that sometimes in the provinces one hears it said that it would not be so sad if perhaps this mission were to die. Here in Beijing we have more than once heard from one of Your Excellency’s domestic staff that if you yourself had the powers of a papal vicar, this Christianity could be uprooted and a new one planted. What this servant said should not redound to the discredit of his master, yet if Your Excellency has no wish to eradicate the mission, his regulation would not have been published in such circumstances that it is accepted that the Chinese Church, its missionaries, and Your Excellency’s own person are all put at risk. You say you are keeping to the mind of the Church and the Supreme Pontiff in publishing your regulation. I humbly put it to you that even if the regulation which you published agreed in every detail with the mind of the Church and the Supreme Pontiff, the publication itself, in the present time of great danger and of a persecution which is already happening or soon will, is certainly the last thing the Church would have in mind. The Church of God has never been willing to expose any nation, even of lesser peoples, | to a clear danger of persecution and the irrecoverable loss of the Gospel because <!-- PDF page 740 | book page 730 --> of things which are not evidently wrong and which should never have been condemned as evidently wrong. Like a loving mother with compassion for the children she has borne for Christ she patiently bears whatever she cannot separate from the dangers of this life or the next.

### 100. The Lord Patriarch rejects the advice of the Lord Bishops of Beijing and Ascalon

From what the Most Illustrious Lord, our Ordinary Bernardino della Chiesa, has written it is clear how seriously he begged Your Excellency not to proceed to the full publication of your judgment; it is clear from the same source that the Most Illustrious Alvaro de Benavente, Bishop of Ascalon and Vicar Apostolic, and even Bishop Maigrot of Conon urged you against publication not only of the regulation but of the papal decree which might be on its way, and to dissuade you on grounds of the danger long before you were aware of any danger to your own person.

Though I could have wished to write more in this most grave situation, my strength is failing me and an acute illness forces me to draw this letter to a close. My prolixity in writing it is aimed, in all humility, at teaching you where the process first began, and how mistake led to mistake, suspicion to suspicion, rolling on as one storm succeeds another until it becomes a raging tempest. For this, there is now but one remedy: that Your Excellency turn the ship into the wind, abandon any thought of continuing on a course which will lead to certain shipwreck, and head for the place in which the tiny ship of the Chinese Church can find safety. You must suspend the promulgation of the regulation at least until either the decision of Our Holy Father Pope Clement XI can reach China or the new information which has already passed the Cape of Good Hope can reach the Holy See; when the Emperor’s declaration has been seen, a final decision might at last be made. With all my heart and with the most plentiful tears at your feet I beseech Your Excellency not to be angry at our views, but to look with a father’s care at the mission which is about to perish forever.

Beijing, 20 April 1707.

Your Excellency’s most humble servant in Christ

Antoine Thomas of the Society of Jesus

Vice-Rector of the College of Beijing and

Acting Vice-Provincial of the Vice Province of China.[^p740-72]
