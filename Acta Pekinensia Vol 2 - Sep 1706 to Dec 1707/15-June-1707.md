### 1. The order of matters to be narrated for this month

If the events that happened in Beijing during this month were to be reported briefly, the whole business could be finished off in a few sentences. But since many things happened in other places, which affected the Beijing Jesuits and for long afterwards exercised their patience, let the reader also be patient in allowing them to be reported here, insofar as time permits. However I shall keep to this order, first giving an account of what happened in Beijing; secondly, reporting how the Franciscans in Linqing received the imperial residence permit; thirdly, relating the rumours about the Beijing Jesuits which began to be spread in Shandong and other provinces; and fourthly, taking a step in the direction of the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch with a view to preparing for the matters which will have to be recorded about him in the following months.

### 2. A letter from Father Basset to Father Antoine Thomas

On the first day of June or the last day of May, a letter had come from the province of Sichuan, from Rev. Fr. Jean Basset of the Parisian Seminary,[^p522-1] a missionary in that place and Pro-Vicar Apostolic in the absence of the Most Rev. Bishop of Rosalia.[^p522-2] It was written to Father Antoine Thomas, and in it he said that one of the officials who was escorting Father Appiani had treated this priest with kindness on the journey, and deserved our thanks here in Beijing. He also said that the same Reverend Father, while in Sichuan by arrangements made by the Viceroy, was in the custody of a mandarin who was known to the Beijing Jesuits.[^p522-3] He asked that he might be given a recommendation to treat Fr. Appiani kindly. This <!-- PDF page 523 | book page 513 --> letter and a few others which Very Rev. Fr. Basset wrote to some of the Beijing Jesuits beginning from the time he came to know about the Emperor’s decree regarding applying for the piao, about which he had been informed by Rev. Fr. Gerbillon and Fr. Antoine Thomas, were all sent to Rome with our procurators. It was not because they contained anything special, but perhaps because the hostile activity of our adversaries which was fiercer than we deserved, made them useful there.[^p523-4]

### 3. Father Antoine Thomas’s reply

On 3 June, Father Antoine Thomas replied as follows to the Very Rev. Jean Basset:

Very Rev. Father,

Recently, I received Your Reverence’s letter dated 13 April, from which I learned that my last letter, which I sent after I received your penultimate one, had not reached you. I know very well that zhixian[^p523-5] in whose custody you say is Rev. Father Ludovico Appiani. He was given to me as a companion on various journeys, during which he deserved well of me. Therefore I am sending him this letter conveying thanks for the services provided there (for Father Appiani), as Your Reverence requested. And I commend to him our Christian cause, which in the present circumstances is everywhere imperilled, and especially in your own area. But he is powerless except to provide help in minor matters, and it would be only through the Viceroy that anything could be achieved; but to him I have no access either personally or through others in this tempestuous period, in which our friends share our experience of fear, for in trying to help us they may involve themselves in danger. We are experiencing the same difficulties here in the court when protecting our Christians, and the whole mission of China has been so shaken that if God were not with us it would collapse in ruin.

We are expecting the return of the Emperor before the middle of the month, not without trepidation and uncertainty about the future. Nevertheless we do not give up hope, nor do we cease from continually devoting care and effort to save, with the help of God, the mission which is collapsing all around us. But <!-- PDF page 524 | book page 514 | manuscript page 646 --> my greatest grief has been caused by that attempt of the Viceroy, by cunning and with evil intent, to remove the church from the city. | In this matter, the best tactics would be to contrive delays and to gain time, because then God might open up some way of rescuing it. For if this church should perish, built and preserved as it has been with so much effort, what will be the fate of the others in that province and of the unhappy Christians who simply must not be deserted?

Meanwhile, God is bringing it about that in a short time we will be embracing Your Reverence here, safe and sound, since the Viceroy has given you freedom to travel, and soon may Rev. Fr. Appiani be released, on whose behalf I have thanked that mandarin, our neighbour, who for my sake has treated him well here.

Your Reverence’s most humble servant in Christ, Antoine Thomas.

The text agrees with the original copy; but I find a discrepancy in the date; for elsewhere it is said that Father Basset’s letter arrived on 31 May and that the answer was given on 1 June.

I testify to this. Father Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.

### 4. Two Jesuits arrive in Beijing

June 4th. Two Jesuits arrived in Beijing at the same time, Fathers Domenge and Baborier,[^p524-6] missionaries in the province of Shaanxi. From a letter which had been sent earlier to the court, two Franciscan Father—Father Antonio Castrocaro, who was Pro-Vicar Apostolic there, and Father Giovanni Battista da Serravalle—were expected along with them.[^p524-7] When these were invited by the ones mentioned before to be their companions on the journey, they began to make excuses on the grounds that they were waiting for a letter from their confreres. But in fact, as soon as the Jesuits set out from Xi’an fu, the two also set out, proceeding with all speed to Linqing. They arrived there so early that, along with the other Rev. Franciscan Fathers, on June 12th they received the permit from the Emperor, who was <!-- PDF page 525 | book page 515 | manuscript page 647 --> passing through on his return journey, as can be read later in the account of the Franciscan Fathers of Shandong.

From June 4th until the middle of the month, nothing special happened, except that Doctor Borghese began to experience fresh stirrings of impatience, becoming all the more desirous of being far away from Beijing the more surely he feared that the Jesuits returning with the Emperor would bring the news from Nanjing and elsewhere about the serious accusations he had sent in writing to the Lord Patriarch and his household against the Beijing Jesuits. He did not want this to become known because he was well aware that he had written untruths under the influence of his own unbridled passions. In fact, however, we did not know about this; we knew only that he had fairly frequently made threats, saying that he would write, and that he had been detected secretly sending his own couriers to Linqing so that his letters could be forwarded from there by another.

### 5. The Beijing Jesuits go out to meet the Emperor

June 13th. We were told that several members of the Emperor’s family were going out to meet the Emperor, further away than is usual, even as far as 25 or 30 French leagues or more, and therefore that we too should go from Beijing to fulfil the same obligation, and travel beyond the customary point. By letter, Fathers Bouvet and Jartoux were urging us to do this very thing, saying it was absolutely necessary in order to drive away the clouds of annoyance with which, during this journey, on account of the actions of the Lord Patriarch, the serenity of His Majesty had been clouded over.

[June] 15th. We set out from the court and because as yet no one knew whether the Emperor would continue to travel on the river as far as Beijing or whether he would disembark and take the vehicles which had been sent 20 French leagues to meet him, we divided our small band: Father Antoine Thomas with Father Tomás Pereira went downstream with the current, in a small boat, while Fathers José Soares, Dominique Parrenin, and Kilian Stumpf took the land route, having agreed on a location where they would meet after 10 or 12 | leagues.

June 17th. We met together in Yangcun,[^p525-8] moving on further from there in a small boat for 20 or more stadia, and when the sun was just touching the horizon we saw coming towards us the leading boats of His Majesty’s fleet. On these, we spoke for a brief moment with Father Bouvet but we were not able to meet with Father Jartoux, who was in the other with Brother Paramino, because <!-- PDF page 526 | book page 516 --> it was now late in the evening and the boats hurrying to their stopping place would not halt for even such a short while, lest by their delay they might be regarded as interrupting the progress of those following.

The [Eldest] Prince’s boat also came up. When he saw that his larger boat could not come over to us because of the depth of the stream, and that it was not convenient to interrupt the line to wait for ours, he climbed down into a small boat and, drawing aside a little, contrary to what everyone expected, spoke to us briefly about the details he had failed to communicate to the Emperor regarding the news of the death of Rev. Father Superior, Father Jean-François Gerbillon. He spoke also about what he had incorrectly reported to His Majesty about Father Bouvet’s involvement in the events which happened near Yangzhou concerning the Vice-Provincial José Monteiro and his four companions. The Prince very earnestly asked this favour of us, that if we were summoned by the Emperor who was to arrive very soon, when speaking we should be mindful of his words, and not cause him embarrassment on account of his accidental (this is the word he used) mistake in relating what had happened. To influence us all the more to do this he admitted his own excessive impatience, but he made excuses for that and made accusations against Father Bouvet, claiming that he was so hard to understand in what he was saying and so stubborn in maintaining his own opinion that he and others like him could not tolerate it, something which he expressed by the Chinese phrase women tiexun ji naibude.[^p526-9]

### 6. The reason for the Prince’s kindness to the Jesuits

It is hard to believe that the Emperor’s eldest son would humiliate himself to the extent that he would request Europeans to conceal an offence he had committed against the same Europeans. But in fact it will be believed by anyone who knows this Prince and his Father. At the time, he was not thinking of us at all, but of himself, for he considered that the only thing that mattered to him was to prevent his Father from getting to know that he was not ashamed to give false information when he was angry and his passions had been stirred up. This would make him even more annoyed with him, since a few days previously he had been seriously angry with him over the lack of respect shown by him to the Crown Prince when he took for himself a young girl who by right should have been offered to the latter.

<!-- PDF page 527 | book page 517 | manuscript page 648 -->

The Prince had departed, and after about half an hour the boat with the Crown Prince appeared, now stopping, now going in the direction of this bank, now to the other. We did not know the reason why it did not proceed upstream, but we did see on the other side of the river four horsemen moving swiftly who followed the ships which were in the lead, and were ordering now one, now another to go back. Shortly afterwards, as twilight merged into night, the firing of a cannon signalled that the anchor of the imperial boat had been cast, and the removal of the lantern showed that the Crown Prince had halted. It was by no means a suitable time for us to make enquiries about the well-being of the Emperor but it seemed even less desirable to postpone this duty, because it could happen that His Majesty would leave on the following morning while he was still asleep and that there would be no opportunity for us on the evening of the next day. This was because that time had been assigned to the principal mandarins, and the queens were to be transferred from the boats to the vehicles; mainly on account of this, there would be no access for us. Therefore, to make the best of a bad situation, we proceeded towards the imperial boat on foot, and being recognized by the commander of the imperial guard we were allowed to enter further in, and the same man allotted us a place where, on bended knee, we could wait until the Emperor was informed of our presence. We remained on bended knee for more than a quarter of an hour, until at length the Chamberlain, the eunuch Li Yu, came and asked us the reason for our presence.

He was told that we came to enquire after the well-being of the Emperor and to thank him for his kindness to the mission, and for the special care he had taken to safeguard it. The Emperor answered | through the same eunuch, firstly that he was in good health and secondly that we could thank him after he had returned home.

### 7. The Emperor reproaches Europeans, and especially the Lord Patriarch, for their habit of lying

And thirdly he said that we should bear in mind that His Majesty considered that the Europeans had told many lies, and that the greatest liar of them all was Duoluo (the Lord Patriarch). We made excuses for him to the best of our ability, but the eunuch, on returning a second time, said that he had reported what we had said to the Emperor and that he remains sure that that European Duoluo had palmed off some lies to him, and of these he gave particular examples. In answer to this, there was nothing left for us except to suffer humiliation under the hands of the powerful, and the eunuch, now returning to us a third time, ordered us to go away to the Crown Prince and then to the [Eldest] Prince. In reply to this, in all innocence, we answered that we had already greeted the [Eldest] Prince. And the eunuch, rebuking us, <!-- PDF page 528 | book page 518 --> ordered us to go at once, reminding us that it was a command of the Emperor which it was not lawful to disregard because of a greeting we had given of our own choice.

While we were wandering about in the dark, we bumped into a eunuch we knew. We recognized one another’s voices, and he introduced us to the officerin-charge of the [Crown] Prince’s guards, and then with the help of both of them we were announced. The Prince called in Father Parrenin and spoke kindly to him, and questioned him about the death and burial of the Reverend Father Superior.

At this point we turned our attention to seeking out the [Eldest] Prince, who was thought to be distant only about half a French league. However he was more than one league away from there. Father Antoine Thomas and Father Pereira, on account of their age and infirmities, were not able to make their way through the scrub at night, so they returned to the boat, and proceeding upstream found the Prince who was by now sound asleep. The other three Fathers returned to Yangcun but did not find what they were looking for, because the Prince had not put out lanterns, and no indication of his whereabouts could be discovered from the bank due to the darkness. And now the position of the stars, which were at their zenith, indicated that it was 11 o’clock in the night, and our physical weariness combined with hunger demanded we should rest. When the sky began to brighten, once again the firing of a cannon indicated that the sails and oars were now ready. We had lost the opportunity for meeting the Prince but two of the Jesuits followed him again in the boat until sunrise, and then disembarking kept up with him on foot along the bank; but they were in fact told that the Prince was still asleep and that no one was allowed to wake him up.

19th June. The Emperor arrived at the park outside the city to recreate himself by hunting for a short time; after that, he went to his country house beyond the walls of the Palace.

21st June. All of us had assembled there to render thanks, as was said on the 17th of this month. The Chamberlain Li Yu, who had been sent by the Emperor, rebuked us on the grounds that three days earlier, disregarding the Emperor’s command, we had failed to visit the Eldest Prince; the Prince himself complained even more forcefully, emphasizing the point with vehemence that in the Kingdom of the Tartars it is not tolerated that by the unlawful action of any man the word of the Emperor should return to him empty. We answered both of them by saying that the Prince was asleep and that this prevented us from carrying out the will of His Imperial Majesty. The Prince could not say anything further against us, but we do not know whether the eunuch exonerated us with the Emperor.

### 8. Fathers Domenge and Baborier receive the imperial permit

On this same day, we informed the Prince that Fathers Domenge and Baborier were present, the former from Xi’an fu and the latter from Hanchong, to request the permit. But he would not see them at that time, ordering them to come back on another morning.

22nd June. Those Fathers presented their hand-written application, which was set out in the same form as were those of the others who up till this time had requested the permit.

23rd June. They received the permit after first being admitted to the Emperor’s presence.

### 9. Father Buonaventura da Roma, a Franciscan, comes to Beijing, sent by the Lord Patriarch

On this same day, 23rd June, Fr. José Simões of the Society of Jesus, a missionary in Nanchang fu,[^p529-10] suddenly appeared at the gates of the College | together with the Rev. Fr. Buonaventura da Roma of the Order of St. Francis, and they said that they had come to get the permit. Rev. Fr. Buonaventura openly admitted that he had been sent by the Lord Patriarch, as was suggested at the beginning of May, and confirmed what I had written there, and explained, more or less in this way, why he had been sent by the Lord Patriarch. He said: “Fathers Hervé and San Giorgio overtook His Excellency at Wan’an xian and told the Lord Patriarch, after he and several others had been dismissed from his presence, all the details of what had happened in Nanjing from the 8th April until the 14th of the same month. At the same time, they gave a rather rough and ready account of the Imperial Edict which was issued against themselves and the Lord Patriarch and by which, under pain of capital punishment, they were outlawed from China. Also they said they had refused to accept the permit under the condition that they would remain in their churches awaiting the reply from the Holy See to the information sent by His Majesty, and in the meantime doing nothing actively against the laws and the Chinese rites. When he heard this most devastating news, the Lord Patriarch on two occasions, one after the other, had a fainting fit, so that during the following night he had to be revived by strengthening medications. He ordered me to go to Beijing immediately so that under any conditions whatsoever, provided only that his own regulation remained intact, I could get a permit, and when I secured this, I would be able to save the Churches of the Sacred Congregation in Xiaoshan, Nanjing, and Hangzhou. And to achieve this he gave me a rather generous travelling allowance of about one hundred patacas, and also the deeds of purchase <!-- PDF page 530 | book page 520 --> of the houses which had been made earlier. He also recommended, if the opportunity presented itself, that I enquire in his name after the well-being of the Emperor, and to make representations to him as to how deeply he regretted the turn of events, from which he recognized that His Majesty had been offended by him, and how earnestly he wished that he might make atonement.”

Father Buonaventura related all of this immediately on his arrival, because he was aware that the Emperor was planning on leaving for Tartary in the near future; however, on account of the continual rain, His Majesty’s journey had to be postponed at that time for a few days.

### 10. A few observations about Father Buonaventura

It should be noted here: 1. That Rev. Fr. Fray Buonaventura spoke of the mission entrusted to him by the Lord Patriarch at many different times and in many different places, and as people do when retelling the same story used different words at different times. From this, in relation to the one subject, different versions emerge. When it became evident in Beijing that this had happened in respect to these matters, Father Kilian the Procurator, according to a letter dated 7 March 1708,[^p530-11] questioned Rev. Fr. Buonaventura on the more essential issues:

Q. On what day and in what place was Your Paternity sent to Beijing by the Lord Patriarch?

R. I was sent on May 5th from Wan’an xian in Jiangxi Province, immediately on the arrival of the Most Rev. Fr. San Giorgio.

Q. What words did the Lord Patriarch use in sending you to Beijing?

R. He said: You are to go and make sure you obtain a piao and nothing else; then he added: In any way whatsoever that you can. Afterwards, when some time had passed, he said: Take care that you observe my decree.

This corresponds to what I wrote down in my own hand. 16 April 1708.[^p530-12] I testify to this.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.

2. At the time when Father Buonaventura was sent he was acting as interpreter for the Lord Patriarch, and was succeeded by Fr. Hervé until they reached Guangzhou, when he was replaced in this office by the Dominican Rev. Fr. Astudillo.

3. Father Buonaventura had already taken the oath to observe the Patriarch’s decree.

4. At that time, Rev. Fr. Monteiro, the Vice-Provincial, was still with the Lord Patriarch, as was discussed under May.

5. Before this, Father Buonaventure had always followed the practice allowed by Alexander VII, as he himself wrote to the Holy See. And this is proved from the printed petitions which in earlier years our procurators had placed at the feet of Clement XI, and for that reason he was considered more qualified to request and to receive the | permit.

6. The Lord Patriarch was exceedingly aggrieved on witnessing the destruction of the mission, and he admitted to Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial and Fr. da Silva that he foresaw this as well as the danger to his own person before he published the decree. But shipwrecks affect us differently when we are swimming among the broken planks of a ship than when we are contemplating them in safety from the shore.

24th June. To show hospitality, Fathers Tomás Pereira and Kilian Stumpf paid a visit to Father Buonaventura. Our good guest showed he was anxious to look after the interests of the Lord Patriarch in these very unhappy times, and in the disasters we were all enduring; thus he took up again what he had said the day before in the presence of others. The Fathers replied that it was a matter of very great difficulty, and that no one was capable of carrying out these particular tasks; would His Paternity deign to give in writing what it was that the Lord Patriarch wanted, or had ordered, or alternatively what he himself considered as being opportune in this business of the Lord Patriarch? In this way with careful planning, consideration would be given to the desired end and the means of achieving this result; but if he did not have in writing what were the wishes of His Excellency, it would be too difficult, relying only on guesswork and presumptions, to deliberate on such a business which could involve us and others in that same undertaking.

Father Buonaventura replied by stating that no commission had been given to him in writing and that a retraction of what he had done had not been written by the Lord Patriarch, or anything else which would express to the Emperor regret for what he had done, nor his willingness to humble himself before the Emperor. All he had was a small sheet of paper on which he had jotted down, with his own hand as a reminder, what he had heard being said by the Lord Patriarch. He added that if these were judged fit to be presented to the Emperor, he would immediately reduce them to order and to a form fit to be presented to the eyes of His Majesty. He even added that he would make most strenuous efforts, or if he could be permitted to speak in this way, that he would put pressure on His Excellency to adopt whatever he himself in this regard should write <!-- PDF page 532 | book page 522 | manuscript page 651 --> and present, and by putting his signature to it, ratify it as his own. Such a plan was not entirely unsuitable, but fraught with many difficulties.

### 11. The difficulties involved in gaining admission to the Emperor for Father Buonaventura

Firstly, it was not clear with what success a man who was a stranger to the Court would be able to transact such a contentious business with the mandarins who were the intermediaries, since he had come here with empty hands. In the past year, they had been driven to exasperation by the Lord Patriarch, and now they were almost raging with anger because they knew that their mighty Lord had been provoked to a state of hostility toward the Lord Patriarch through the publication of his decree.

Secondly, our guest did not seem to be quite the right person to negotiate with the Prince at this time when he was in a very bad mood, and when the Emperor himself was still smarting from the recent wound inflicted on him, and was all the time laying a heavier hand on His Excellency for his aggressiveness in that he butted in after the Emperor had made an appeal to the Supreme Pontiff in regard to the rites of the empire, which are entrusted to him by Heaven.

Thirdly, because the Lord Patriarch omitted to give Fr. Buonaventura written proof of his appointment as Procurator in a matter of such great importance; nor did he make any request of us in writing, in fact not even in any clear and definite oral form; nor did he give us orders for his business to be negotiated with the Emperor. Much less did he explain whether he paid due attention to the information which was sent by His Majesty as an appeal to the Holy See. And yet he could easily have arranged all these matters in writing, if not personally, at least through his auditor, his chancellor, or his secretary, or even through our Vice-Provincial who was there. In the absence of any authentic document in which the Lord Patriarch explained what he had in mind, who would dare to approach the throne of an angry Emperor, without being afraid that the second state would be worse than the first? The Fathers also could not help calling to mind what | happened in Tartary in the month of August of the preceding year, when Fr. Appiani, in the name of the Lord Patriarch, asked permission to depart and His Majesty refused to accept it unless it was in writing. And when it was granted, the Lord Patriarch repudiated it shortly afterwards, denying it and insisting that that he had never given Fr. Appiani permission to ask for it.

Fourthly, even if everything had been put in writing by the Lord Patriarch without any ambiguity, the order given by the Emperor still stood, by which the Fathers were forbidden to make any mention of the person or affairs of the Lord Patriarch in the presence of His Majesty. If therefore it was dangerous <!-- PDF page 533 | book page 523 --> to present written satisfaction (if it had been sent) how great was the peril in rashly proposing a matter of such great moment to the Emperor without anything in writing, when no evidence of it was available?

Fifthly, spurning the danger, however serious it might be, we would nevertheless try to do something to serve His Excellency and for the good of the mission—but we needed time.

### 12. The Lord Patriarch is deported to Macau

But we were not given time, for the Emperor was going to Tartary and the Lord Patriarch had been taken away to Macau, and so they were separated from one another by a distance of more than five hundred French leagues. And now for a letter to reach Macau, and for another letter to return to be shown to the Emperor, four or more months were required. And for one which the Jesuits or Fr. Buonaventura might write to the Lord Patriarch at the end of June, no reply could be provided until the end of October. And what that response might be, no one could discover by mere guesswork.

Sixthly, and this was the most important of all, the greatest obstacle to any effort of ours which we might attempt was that decision made in May when, on sending away the two who had been banished, he ordered the Lord Patriarch to be taken away from Guangzhou to Macau and there to await the response from Rome. And in fact he was taken away on June 16th, but it was not yet known for certain at the Court whether at the time this had been put into effect. And so it seemed most imprudent to raise any new business with the Emperor on the strength of a procuratorship entrusted so casually to Father Buonaventura without anything in writing and without the due formalities, without which not even the Lord Patriarch would admit anyone to his tribunal.

For these and similar reasons, no one ventured to advise Rev. Father Buonaventura to prepare himself to act as Procurator. But he, on another occasion, after calling together Fathers José Soares, Pieter van Hamme, and Kilian Stumpf, revealed other moves suggested to him by the Lord Patriarch if they should perhaps prove advisable:

To these Fathers he made the following proposals:

Firstly, the Lord Patriarch is suggesting to us that he himself may be able to write and to arrange for his letter to reach the Emperor in person. He said that an opportunity for writing was provided for him by the mandarins, for they, in the name of the Emperor, as he was leaving the Court, asked for some more meteoric stones which he said had been left in Guangzhou. Thus, by sending the stones now, it would be easy to send a letter also.

Secondly, let the Fathers remember that His Excellency is able to inform the Emperor that something more than the right of patronage in China is being <!-- PDF page 534 | book page 524 | manuscript page 652 --> claimed by the King of Portugal; something which this monarch will find not at all pleasing, and not without its consequences.[^p534-13]

Thirdly, if any misunderstanding occurred, it was entirely the fault of the interpreters, since His Excellency at no time spoke to or heard the Emperor speaking except through others. And so His Majesty could easily be made to understand that neither his mind regarding the Lord Patriarch nor the feelings of the Lord Patriarch towards him, have ever been faithfully revealed, and there would be no lack of proofs by which it could be shown with what respectful and eager dispositions he always took care to carry out the will of the Emperor.

### 13. Father Kilian Stumpf gives an answer to Father Buonaventura’s observations, and gives a warning of the dangers to be feared

The three Fathers listened to these diverse statements but they wished Father Kilian alone to respond, since he was the procurator, and he provided a reply to this effect: Most Rev. Father! There is nothing that we desire more than to aid and secure the safety of the person | of the Lord Patriarch. For if he is kept safe, it is possible to hope that the mission also will be saved. Do not therefore hesitate to reveal everything to us, whatever you may have in your instructions, so that the truth may be distinguished from what merely appears to be true, and the useful from the harmful in this present business.

When Father Buonaventura said he had nothing else written down on the sheet of paper which he was holding in his hands, Father Stumpf proceeded in this way:

As to the first point, the Most Excellent Lord seems to be mistaken, for the Emperor has sent to the Supreme Pontiff his decree in which he ordered the Lord Patriarch to leave Beijing; in addition to that, we have a copy of another decree, a very drastic one which the Emperor had already prepared in the case that the Lord Patriarch should postpone the day of his departure or attempt further delays. Therefore it is unreasonable to suppose that the Emperor, who in anger was ordering him to depart, should have made such a trifling request of him when he was leaving. The meteoric stones may have been requested through the mandarins, perhaps on the orders of one of the Emperor’s sons, but let no one be under the illusion that this was a request from the Emperor. Notwithstanding this, His Excellency could write to the Emperor, and if he were <!-- PDF page 535 | book page 525 --> to send the letter to us, assuredly we would not commit such a serious offence as to suppress it. If however he were to express in it the matters which are mentioned in the second point, he would commit an offence against the Crown of Portugal, and not without doing harm to himself. Consideration must be given as to what will happen and what the Emperor will think if the bulls of the Supreme Pontiffs on behalf of Portugal were produced in which the right of patronage and much more extensive rights have been granted to the Most Serene Kings of Portugal and their successors. His Excellency has already experienced the hostility with which the Emperor listened to accusations made previously by him in this same month of the previous year.[^p535-14] And certainly now, at the present time, it is to be feared that he may experience even harsher treatment on account of those accusations, and even more serious ones, since now at this very moment it may be presumed that the Lord Patriarch has been delivered at Macau into the hands and the custody of the Portuguese.

As to the third point, said Father Kilian, something might have been possible in the previous year after the first and second offence committed here by the Lord Patriarch. But now, after the publication of his regulation and his attempts to block the Emperor’s appeal, how can the blame be attributed to the interpreters? Can His Excellency, I ask you, put so much reliance on the devotion of one or another of the Jesuits, who at times have been interpreters, that such a one would be willing to lay down his life to save the Lord Patriarch? And after, without any reason and although innocent, having been accused of interpreting falsely accept the death penalty which would be imposed by the Emperor? And what would happen to Fr. Appiani, who was taken away from him and is still held in chains? He knows, and only too surely he knows, that the Emperor is bitterly hostile to that man and is looking for charges against him. And if the Lord Patriarch, with the authority he has, brings forward this accusation, what else would the Emperor wait for before he had Father Appiani’s tongue cut out and his head cut off? But let us suppose that Fr. Appiani is willing to be sacrificed to save His Excellency: will the mandarins also be willing? There is no doubt that the fate of the mandarins who were the intermediaries would be the same as that of Father Appiani. Therefore there would be no doubt but that they would defend themselves, their children, and their families from death and calumny by every means in their power. All of these things of their very nature are bound to ruin whatever up till now could be found here at Beijing to protect our religion, and also to cause the greatest possible trouble for the person of the Lord Patriarch, and the missionaries and our holy religion. <!-- PDF page 536 | book page 526 | manuscript page 653 --> Therefore let Your Reverence forgive us if we do not allow ourselves to be so terrified by these dangers that in our confusion we would descend to such disastrous measures.

There still remained the business of applying for the residence permit, which Father Buonaventura definitely wanted to obtain so that, as his zeal prompted him, he could remain in China. He readily acknowledged that he had to beware of everything that has caused others to be banished and to steer clear of those things which might require discussing dubious matters in the presence of the Emperor and the Prince, which could implicate the person of His Excellency. And so | on 26 June, together with Father Simões, at the Royal Villa, he presented to the Prince a document in his own handwriting in the form of a petition, requesting a permit. Good fortune favoured him, and he was given a promise that his petition would be granted. The Emperor had to set out, according to a decision made earlier, on the following day and so there was a huge crowd of people all around his throne with business which had to be finished before he left, and the Prince did not think it was opportune to bring the two petitioners into His Majesty’s presence. He decided it was sufficient to state verbally that each of them had provided what had to be provided, and therefore seemed worthy to have the royal favour conferred on them through a permit.

### 17. The appalling calumnies inflicted on the Jesuits by this same doctor

From the many calumnies which Fr. Buonaventura, while he was on the Patriarch’s ship, discovered to have been written against us by the doctor, and which had been sent as being suitable for preservation in the records of the Apostolic Visitation, these below are just a small part.

2. ### 18. Their falsity is demonstrated

The first, with regard to the suspicion of poison, is in this form:

I, the undersigned, being questioned by Brother Giuseppe Baudino as to whether I have taken an oath to Monsignor the Patriarch of Antioch as to what way the malady which he suffered at the thermal springs resulted from the administration of poison, I said, and I say again, that no doctor can testify and declare under oath that such a malady is the result of the administration of poison if he does not see with his own eyes the preparation and the giving of the poison. The reason is that poisons are produced in the human body, poisons in no way less potent than those which can be artificially prepared. Such are plagues, gangrene, malignant fevers, hot and cold fevers, and also other illnesses more sudden and more dangerous. And any time he states that such a malady is the result of poison, he does not mean to imply that it was the result of poison that was administered unless he specifies it and distinguishes it from one occurring naturally. In such a situation a doctor must proceed with great caution when he names those responsible in public. A doctor who declares that poison has been administered is not safe in his conscience unless he has evidence in addition to that which is derived from his own professional skill.

And I | affirm this. 29 July 1707, Beijing.

Giovanni Borghese, with my own hand.

That this agrees with the original two copies, I so testify.

Father Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.

The retraction presented above does not seem to be sufficiently rounded out. Perhaps he might have provided a more adequate one if he had not known that in Beijing, and in fact in the whole of China, there was not one competent judge before whom the Jesuits could bring a case against blatant calumnies of this kind. For what ecclesiastic in China would permit a case to be brought up before him with members of the Lord Patriarch’s household as defendants, and indeed in relation to false evidence deposited in the chancery of the Apostolic Visitation?

### 19. The doctor withdraws the charges he spread against the Jesuits

If however we had appealed to a local court, there is no doubt that the doctor would have been forced by it either to prove his charge or to pay the penalty; but an upright conscience and Christian charity extended even to our enemies meant more to us than an excessive zeal in preserving our own good name. And yet, if expressions used by the doctor in his retraction are correctly interpreted, they in fact prove what (as he said) he wanted to prove: namely that he could not <!-- PDF page 540 | book page 530 | manuscript page 656 --> provide any proof that a poison had been given unless he testified at the same time that he had seen it handled and administered. Since however he did not testify that he had seen with his own eyes the handling and the administration of poison, the consequence is that any testimony about the poison so given as a cause for the malady was worthless in law.

Likewise he gave a similar reply to the second and third point on 10 July 1707:

I, the undersigned testify, according to what Brother Baudino told me, that the Eldest Prince made an urgent entreaty to his father that the Monsignor, the Bishop of Conon, should be imprisoned and handed over to the Tribunal of Justice; that I have never said that the Jesuits made a similar urgent request for the death penalty for the Monsignor of Conon; also I have written to the contrary, and such is the truth.

In addition I certify that Brother Baudino has never said to me that Monsignor the Patriarch would be confined on an island, nor even that the Emperor would have him deprived of his entourage. And I testify to this, 10 July 1707.

Giovanni Borghese, in his own hand.

That this corresponds to the original two copies, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, SJ, Apostolic Notary.

The fourth point does not require retraction. The falsity of this testimony was evident to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing from the letter of Fr. San Giorgio, which he received with the letter from the Beijing Jesuits at the same time. Fr. Buonaventura personally heard Frs. San Giorgio and Hervé tell what happened on the Lord Patriarch’s boat. And by now, from other sources in China, the fact of this decree is known to everyone and will be very well-known in the whole of Europe.

Buonaventura to tell the doctor that in the future he was to avoid making false charges against priests who were innocent, and if he wanted to do the right thing, not only was he never to write in the future to the Bishop of Beijing, but he was to erase his name from the catalogue of his memory, to the effect that he was no longer a person with whom he could communicate.

### 21. The Bishop of Ascalon’s letter to Father Antoine Thomas

Very Rev. Father,

[A][^p541-17] Since the middle of the month of April, I have written three or more letters to Your Paternity, but on account of the Emperor’s absence from the court and for other reasons as well, no means of delivering them is really secure. ### 22. Why the Dominican Fathers did not remain in China

[B] At this time, some Dominican Fathers passed through here who in Zhejiang along with the clerics had refused to accept the royal permit; it did not suit them to remain in China subject to the promise that they would not continue their missionary duties until Fathers Barros and Beauvollier returned and would live as simple hermits in their houses. And they rightly rejected this promise and condition, because it involved very serious inconveniences, and in addition Fr. Navarrete in the year 1674 brought up this question in the Congregation of the Holy Inquisition in Rome, and it was decided by the consultors that such a <!-- PDF page 542 | book page 532 | manuscript page 657 --> promise was not lawful. Hence if the French Fathers[^p542-19] had made this promise they would have caused great inconvenience to the rest of their confreres, and to some extent would be confirming the Lord Patriarch’s presumptions. It would have been better to take the stance of Father Monteiro: But under no circumstances will I make any such promise.

[C] I wrote to Your Paternity how I received the edict of the Lord Patriarch which had been drawn up on 25 January at Nanjing. I received it when I was on my way to the Court to apply for a permit. I made an appeal to the Apostolic See, and forwarded an authenticated copy through a servant to the Lord Patriarch, who was on his way to us. He received this on about 20 April near Nanchang.

After he had delivered the document with the appeal, the servant should have gone on to Nanjing with the letters for Your Paternity and others, especially Rev. Fr. da Silva, but the Lord Patriarch intercepted my letters to prevent the news of my appeal from reaching Beijing and Nanjing and ordered my servant to come with him without sending me an answer.

### 23. The Bishop of Ascalon is treated shamefully by the Lord Patriarch

[D] But at length, on 16 May, he reached Nanjing where I was waiting for him. As soon as he saw me, making every effort and even using threats, he tried to induce me to withdraw my appeal, or at least to change the wording to what he wanted. And when he saw that I was not budging from what I had decided to do, and intended also to reinforce my appeal with another document, he gave up hope and treated me shamefully both by what he said and did, to such an extent that I was forced to prepare a new appeal on account of the fresh provocations and also to take action to preserve my dignity. But the said Lord on the 18th proceeded on to Guangzhou without giving any answer to my appeal nor to my additional document; after | trampling on all my rights and filling the whole place with his uproar, he departed. Because he was very careful to avoid putting anything in writing actively or passively, the result would be that everything in Europe would depend on his unqualified account.

### 29.[^p542-20] The reasons why the Bishop of Ascalon appealed to the Roman See

[E] But the worthy Lord will find himself mistaken, just like anyone else who has not had previous experience in such negotiations. The reasons for the appeal, considered in general, are that the edict is ill-timed, and aimed only at the provocation of the Emperor together with the destruction <!-- PDF page 543 | book page 533 --> of the Christian Law. And also because everything which has happened in the Court from the year 1706 ought be referred to the Apostolic See for a definite decision regarding the controversies; even if a decisive decree regarding them has already been issued, the case must be investigated further. Likewise because the mission given to Fathers Barros and Beauvollier is equivalent to an appeal; for through that, the Emperor is having recourse to the Pope against the Lord Patriarch, particularly in relation to the matters in dispute. Likewise because I, although I am the least of all, have a right to be heard in the same way as the Bishop of Conon, and recently my writings have been sent to Rome. Other things which are in the appeal can be reduced to these, and although my documents have been in the hands of the Lord Patriarch since April 20th, he has not decided whether to defer to it or not.

[F] I received Your Paternity’s letters and others from Father Kilian which were written at about the end of March, and I have already answered all of them. I also received at the same time the very cruel letters directed to Father Gerbillon and his fellow Jesuits by the Lord Patriarch. I rejoice on Your Paternity’s behalf. I hope in the Lord that he may offer us something for the benefit of the mission, and not only something for the spiritual progress of we who are suffering.

### 30. He explains what he wants the Jesuits to do for him

[G] I come finally to what concerns me personally. Since, on account of the Lord Patriarch’s edict, I have decided not to approach the Emperor, and it is not appropriate for me to do so, I shall wait here at the disposal of Divine Providence, for my fate is in his hands. I had written to Your Paternity and to your fellow Jesuits making some requests in the event that the Lord Patriarch should defer to my appeal, but these now serve no purpose and certainly it is not necessary for Your Paternities to do anything special for me. Further reasons are because I will not promise to live here or anywhere else in China as a simple xiudaode[^p543-21] and because I will not make any promise which is contrary to the edict of the Lord Patriarch, since I want to keep myself free from blame so that I can fight for the cause more efficaciously in Europe. In these circumstances, if there were some means by which the Emperor would grant me, without those promises, to remain in the city of Guangzhou until the arrival of Fathers Barros and Beauvollier, and with the liberty of going to Rome if it should be necessary, that is something worth working for. And whatever Your Paternity and your companions could do to <!-- PDF page 544 | book page 534 | manuscript page 658 --> achieve this, I would accept with gratitude. For my presence here is not really necessary, and in fact the climate here is colder than in Guangzhou, and causes me grave inconvenience; already I have administered confirmation three times throughout the whole province.[^p544-22]

[H] There is much writing which I must do so that it can be sent to Europe, and in Guangzhou I shall be able to do this, and in a variety of languages, because in that place there is no shortage of secretaries, and also news comes in from every direction. And so this would be more convenient for me, and since this concession is being made to other missionaries, in my case it would not be conspicuous seeing that it is for an old man regarding whom he has nothing to fear. Anything else special granted to me might honour me but would not be useful. More than once I have written to Your Paternity to ask that your fellow- Jesuits would inform me of any way in which I can assist the mission, because I will leave nothing undone provided that it is not unreasonable. And so Your Paternity is not to hesitate to inform me, personally or through others, about everything.

Everything up to this point had been written by the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon with his own hand. What follows in this same letter, and will be written down here immediately, was written by the hand of his secretary Rev. Fr. Fernández Serrano, under his dictation. It continues in this way.

[J] I had finished writing these things when a malignant abscess attacked my right hand, with the result that I suffered severe pain there; but now the complaint is easing. However, if I can write again within a month | I will be fortunate.

I can add nothing new here except that I am convinced that the Lord Patriarch is quite determined not to make a juridical response to my appeal, but merely an extra-juridical one, which is insulting and extremely hurtful. But none of these things deter me; rather, on account of those affronts I will defend the justice of my cause and the preservation of the mission with all the more zeal before the Apostolic See. This whole conduct of the Lord Patriarch is so well publicized by now that we must, not only may, consider him a tyrant, since all his actions and thoughts are manifestly leading to the destruction of the Catholic faith.

### 31. The Bishop of Ascalon passes judgment on the Lord Patriarch

[K] I do not say these things to make Your Paternity and fellow Jesuits disinclined to obey him; rather I urge you to remain obedient to him, as I do myself, since the Apostle orders us to obey our superiors, even those we find disagreeable. But I do advise you that in certain matters which are very prejudicial to and obviously harmful to the spreading of the Faith, Your Paternity and fellow Jesuits should follow the intention of the Apostolic See rather than the orders of the Lord Patriarch, which are contrary to the Christian Faith and to charity. This is something the Emperor is already threatening. Far be it from me to say that the Apostolic See wants this: anyone who tramples on all the laws to bring about such a perverse result must be considered a wolf and not a father. But he does not frighten me as he does others, and I am almost alone in this, for the missionaries are intimidated, even though they share my opinions, but are unwilling to involve themselves in these matters against such a formidable superior. Mistakenly some of them think that he will be judged by Our Most Holy Lord [the Pope] on the basis of personal affection, and not in accordance with justice and truth.

### 32. Why the Bishop of Beijing acts so supinely

I, when I am able, will write to him, and I shall make no further requests on my own account, especially if I am permitted to remain in Guangzhou and there finish off the writing which I have to send to Europe.

I take it for granted that Your Paternity has already taken measures to remedy the difficulties in sending letters both ways between Guangzhou and Beijing, and as a result there will be safer communication between us.

I greet your fellow Jesuits in the Lord, and I wish Your Paternity all the best.

Nan’an fu 26 June.

Your Paternity’s most devoted servant, Friar Alvaro etc.

Bernardo de la Encarnaciôn, Fr. Francisco de la Concepción, Fr. Fray Martín Alemán, Fr. Fray Miguel Fernández, Fr. Fray Francisco de San José, discalced friars of Our Holy Father Francis; and by Fr. Giambattista da Serravalle, Italian Friars Minor of the Reformed Observants, the last two recently come from the province of Shaanxi to receive the said permit in this city.

On the Eve of Pentecost, 11 June, there arrived at this church of Linqing the Rev. Fr. Bouvet, and after some discussion with those who had just come they were informed of the way to write their letters requesting the permit. We were all firmly convinced that we were obliged if asked a second time to go jointly with the newly arrived Fathers, that we would reply completely without any mental restriction, and that we would follow the rules and direction of His Excellency the Lord Patriarch and the decision of the Holy See in the matter of the Chinese Rites disputed for so many years. And, although Fr. Bouvet argued for a different position: that we should at least agree to remain in China suspended <!-- PDF page 550 | book page 540 | manuscript page 661 --> pending the return from Rome of Fathers de Barros and Beauvollier, the Emperor’s envoys, as certain Fathers | of his Company in the South have done. We were little inclined to this solution because of the clear provisions in the decree of the Lord Patriarch and for many other reasons that came to mind. The letters of the newly arrived Fathers were as follows:

### 37.[^p550-27] The document prescribed for making an application for the permit

I, N …, an Italian, of the Order of St. Francis, N. years of age, came to China in … I live in Xi’an fu in the province of Shaanxi preaching the Law of God; I have studied Philosophy and Theology and I have followed the practice of Father Matteo Ricci and of the other earlier Fathers. Insofar as it depends on me, I do not have the intention of returning to Europe. I have prepared this document according to the orders of the Emperor.

Linqing zhou. 12 June 1707. In the 46th year of Kangxi, on the 12th day of the 5th Moon.

Fr. N. (as above)

When the day of Pentecost arrived Rev. Fr. Bouvet came to my house in the morning and after Mass began to arrange for the reception of the permit as on the day before. He continued to argue for his opinion, talking until we arrived at the riverbank. After we got to the boat along came Rev. Father Jartoux and Brother Paramino, who had gone to their house to eat. Rev. Father Jartoux began to discuss the content of our presentation (this Father had orders from the Prince to act as our interpreter). He raised various questions with us and when he heard our absolute determination not to follow the wishes of the Prince, nothing was decided, especially regarding the chief point of disagreement as to whether we should remain suspended in our houses. After begging divine help and special guidance in so a serious matter, together with Fr. Bouvet, who for this reason had returned again from the boat, we all set out together with the Lord Bishop and Fr. Carlo da Castorano who, since they already had a permit, returned home.

### 38. The Franciscans continue to tell how they requested and received the permit

They led us into a temple of the idols on the bank of the river, to which came the Prince who, after seating us all on cushions, began to interrogate us all together as to whether any of us had received any permits, to which we replied that none of us had. Then after he had gone into the inner part of <!-- PDF page 551 | book page 541 | manuscript page 662 --> the temple and paid his respects to the idol, he sat down and called us and asked us in order, firstly, if the Fathers who had recently come from Shaanxi had been given the same document which the rest had received. Fr. Jartoux replied that they had. The second question, addressed to Fr. Antonio Frossolone, was whether he requested the permit. He replied that he did. The third question was to Fr. Bernardo himself, and he replied that he did. Fourthly, he asked Fr. Frossolone if he followed the practices of Fr. Matteo Ricci. He replied that previously he had followed them, but now he followed the instructions of the Pontiff; this question was repeated twice and he replied each time the same way. Then he posed the same question to Fr. Bernardo and he replied[^p551-28] that previously he had followed the practices of Fr. Matteo but, since the instructions of the Pontiff which prohibited them and which men had to heed, that he could not obey. When the Prince heard these replies he became very angry, and violently threw the petition of our men on the ground; turning to Fr. Jartoux he rebuked him, saying: “You people told me that all were in agreement as before, and that all was arranged, and now I find that they are all of another opinion and are trying to deceive me, like this Lao Hongren (Frossolone) who tried to trick me another way. Take them all outside | and examine them, and don’t bring them to me again until they have decided whether or not they want to request the permit.”

### 39. Father Frossolone is questioned by the prince

We got up and went out to the entrance hall of the temple, entering a room accompanied by the Fathers of the Company, the mandarin Zhang Bowang (Zhang Changzhu, since Zhang Bowang was his father, long since dead),[^p551-29] and a scribe. The aforesaid Zhang urged us to ask to receive the permit. He said that if we did not want to follow Li Madou, at least we should get the permit in order to remain in China without preaching against the said Father. The Reverend Fathers and the scribe urged us the same way, but since we all now agreed in substance to reject this, we replied and gave them to understand that, given the pontifical decision, we were unable to do so. Meanwhile the Prince gave orders that we should be called into his presence one by one. The mandarin Zhang took Fr. Frossolone <!-- PDF page 552 | book page 542 --> by the arm and led him inside, and the Prince asked him: 1. If he sought the permit? He replied that he did. 2. Why did he not want to follow Fr. Ricci? He replied: because the Pontiff had ordered the contrary. 3. Why, when we passed through before, in the previous examination, had he not replied to this effect? He replied: Because I had not yet received the decree, and for this reason in that examination I did not speak up. 4. The Prince said: “Now I understand you after so many detours. You are saying that you will do nothing except what is approved by the Bishop of Conon.” To this Fr. Frossolone said: “I have never seen the Bishop of Conon, nor his books, and I have never followed nor do I now follow his practices, but have followed and follow the instructions of the Holy Pontiff.” 5. The Prince urged him to take the diploma saying: “Since you are now so determined, you could live a Religious life in your house without preaching; because if you intend to preach that it is not licit to venerate and sacrifice to Confucius, the ancestors, etc.[^p552-30] you will get yourself killed. What do you say to that? Do you want to receive it on that condition?” He responded: “I cannot.” The Prince replied: “And afterwards do you wish to preach against these practices in China?” Reply: “I intend to follow the instructions of the Holy Pontiff.” 6. [The Prince] severely reproached him, saying that he was very confusing and was trying yet again to trick him. “If I were to kill you by cutting off your head, what would you think of that?” The Father replied: “I am at your disposal!” When [the Prince] heard this he ordered him to be thrown out since it was all over.

### 41. They receive the imperial permit

Then there followed the Pro-Vicar Apostolic Fr. Antonio da Castrocaro and [the Prince] asked him through Rev. Fr. Jartoux if he wanted to wait for return of the Fathers who had gone to Europe. At this question several times repeated, Fr. Antonio became flustered since he had a fixed determination not to remain in China without preaching. The Prince waited for him to reply and Fr. This only made him more confused and without knowing what he was saying, he said: “I intend to obey the order of the Emperor.” He tried to withdraw what he had said, since he realized that it was not well expressed, but the Prince did not give him time and immediately went on: “Do you follow Matteo Ricci?” He answered: “I have followed him.” The prince replied: “Do you still do so?” Reply: “I have told you <!-- PDF page 554 | book page 544 | manuscript page 664 --> that I did follow him.” “And who do you follow now, tell me?” The Father replied: “I intend to follow the orders of the Holy Pontiff.” [The Prince] insisted: “If the Pontiff orders you not to preach, would you preach?” He replied he would not. “I ask you, do you want to return to Europe?” Reply: “That is not my intention.” “Should the Pontiff order you to return, would you return?” “If he should order it finally, I will seek permission of His Majesty.” [The Prince] closed the discussion, and promised him the permit.

The Prince asked Fr. Juan Battista [Serravalle] in a very friendly tone of voice: “Do you intend to obey the Holy Pontiff?” He replied that he did. Do you wish to remain in China? Yes, said the Father. If the Pontiff should tell you to return to Europe, would you go? He replied that he would, but that he would seek permission from the Emperor to leave the kingdom. The Father[^p555-34] was handed over to the Viceroy, and he passed him on to the mandarin of Dongchang fu where he resided, to fulfil the orders of the Emperor.

### 44. Why the Franciscans disagreed with the Jesuits

Moreover, this gathering of nine Franciscans in the residence of the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop could not refrain from contriving something regarding the Jesuit Fathers, and especially the ones from Beijing; things which have already been mentioned, or are to be mentioned, as their present circumstances, affairs, and feelings suggested. Their desires above all centred on the one thing they wanted, that the Chinese rites should be abolished, and they did not want any obstacle to be placed by the Emperor to their being eliminated by virtue of the pontifical decree. And even if there were no pontifical decree, they nevertheless wished the Patriarch’s regulation to have such force that the rites, on the grounds that they were different from normal Church usage, should fall into disuse. And in this they were thinking the same as all the Jesuits think and have always thought.[^p557-36]

And a human motive enflamed this pious desire, because the Lord Patriarch had showed that he was opposed to the Jesuits and that he would always be favourable to the Franciscans. He showed this both in Guangzhou, where he had given one hundred patacas to Fr. Osca[^p557-37] and caused to be covered up in silence a very grave violation of the Sacred Canons which had been imprudently perpetrated to the great harm of many people,[^p557-38] and also in Beijing, where with great liberality he made good the loss which Father Frossolone suffered from brigands when he was travelling. Also, and especially in the province of Shandong when after his unfortunate departure from the Court he <!-- PDF page 558 | book page 548 | manuscript page 666 --> was being taken there in slow stages. For in addition to the 100 ounces of silver which he gave to Father Fernández to travel to Liaodong, he gave alms also to the Lord Bishop himself as well as to the rest, and 20 patacas came the way of each person, as a letter of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing testifies. This is to be recorded in the appendix.

And all of this was a major cause for disagreeing with the Jesuits.

And there was gossip of all kinds among the Franciscans, to the great detriment of the Beijing Jesuits. Although they were not the only creators of these rumours, for many had been written to them by the followers of the Patriarch, they took pleasure themselves in rather freely risking the skin of the Jesuits. I would willingly omit relating these matters here, | if it were not that I have an obligation to future generations. Because with the beginning of this century and with the end of the one which has just past, the enemies of the Society, being at a loss to find something with which to assail the Society, are searching far and wide and are actively engaged in attacking us with recycled falsehoods.[^p558-39] Therefore it is absolutely necessary to provide some of the absurd statements of those Reverend Fathers together with a refutation of them, so that missionaries who shall arrive in times to come may know not only the complaints which have been made against us, but also a just defence which they can use to protect themselves and our posthumous good name.

### 46. The charges are refuted one by one

There are many things which I am omitting as I said above; now let me propose replies to the eighteen points which have been presented. And I am presenting them both as they were given at the time by Fr. Girolamo Franchi of the Society of Jesus, who had such things thrown in his face,[^p560-42] and also as they were amplified by someone in Beijing for the fuller information of our opponents.

Re 1. It would have been desirable for the Lord Patriarch to make an investment in even the slightest humiliation to appease the Emperor’s anger, averting the banishment of so many missionaries and halting the ruin of the infant Church in this empire. But nothing like this eventuated. Anyone who wants to get a clearer knowledge of the stubborn mind of the Lord Patriarch, who rejected any form of humiliation, should reread the records of the month of August of the year 1706 and of June of this year, where we wrote about the matters which the Lord Patriarch, according to the testimony of Rev. Fr. Buonaventura, indicated or threatened that he would write to the Emperor. And from these last also it will be clear just how incredible is this rumour which the Reverend Franciscan Fathers have been spreading in the province of Shandong and in the whole of China, mistaken either through their own predispositions or by information from other sources.

<!-- PDF page 561 | book page 551 | manuscript page 668 -->

Re 2. Their own account, given above on page 66143 proves that the Reverend Fathers did not get the permit in Linqing so easily. However they did get it comparatively easily, because the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing had experienced a more difficult refusal over several days at Beijing in the month of January 1707. But the main reason why they found that the Prince and the Emperor were more accommodating in comparison with that occasion was because His Majesty knew that the said Most Reverend Lord had for so many months been an intimate friend of and a table-companion of the Lord Patriarch, as well as of the Bishop of Conon. And for this same reason, Rev. Fr. Frossolone had to submit to a more intensive examination in Linqing, and was finally banished without a permit because by the answers he gave he confirmed the presumption that already been developed against him. This can be seen in the account given by the Franciscan Fathers above.

### 47. The Emperor’s prudent manner of acting

Re 3. This conjecture of the Rev. Fathers is based on a reasonable foundation. For since it is known for certain that the Emperor sent to Rome what had been authentically presented by the Lord Patriarch at Beijing, and also what had been provided by the Most Rev. Bishop of Conon Charles Maigrot, by Rev. Fr. Ludovico Appiani, and by Rev. Fr. Guéty, it could be presumed without being inconsistent that the manuscripts of the missionaries of Shandong etc. would all be sent to Rome by the Emperor, each at its own time; in doing this, in fact he would do no injustice to anyone. For in sending such documents to the feet of the Supreme Pontiff the Emperor 1. shows the great respect in which he holds him; 2. proves the justice of his own cause, showing that he is far from acting precipitately in the destruction of the mission and is willing to await the judgment of the Supreme Pontiff in matters that concern Europeans; 3. wants to make it clear to the whole world, | in the event that the Supreme Pontiff should judge that the mission should be abolished, that no one can call into question the prudence and holiness of Clement XI, and that he himself has proceeded along the path of reason and of the law of China, and has not banished the missionaries as an act of despotic power. These are the actions of a high-minded man and are worthy of a mighty monarch. Who therefore can be offended when in circumstances such as these his manuscript has been sent to Rome?

Re 4. What the Reverend Fathers who made the promises when they accepted the permit in Linqing intended, only they themselves know, and some time in the future that Judge will judge them, the One to whom alone the hearts <!-- PDF page 562 | book page 552 --> of men and their more secret intentions are laid bare. On this question, let the reader see what was recorded under April 1707 regarding the explanation which the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing gave for his promise, and for the very similar promise contained in the manuscript of his associate Rev. Father da Castorano and of the other Very Reverend Franciscan Fathers of the province of Shandong. But what they promised according to the understanding of the Emperor, the Emperor himself will explain when they will be accused of having acted or preached against the Chinese rites. Certainly the Emperor will judge that he has the right to explain the sense in which he required and understood their promise. If it appears to some that they cannot be incriminated by the Emperor in the case that they made promises in accordance with their own conscience, they will have very great difficulty in saving themselves from the penalty that the Emperor threatened by public decree to impose.

### 48. Other statements made by the Franciscans are refuted

Re 5. This borders on the ridiculous, to say nothing worse about it. These Reverend Fathers admit that they have followed the practice of the earlier missionaries by allowing the Chinese rites. And this is proved by what they have written with their own hand, and by numerous testimonies already presented in Rome. Why did they permit the rites? The reason is that they did not regard them as being evil in themselves, and according to the law of China, they cannot be omitted without serious punishment in the case of others, and in the case of children under risk of the death penalty. Since they knew that capital punishment was fixed by law, that a law had been passed, and that there was an emperor who enforced the law, pray what is the origin of this presumption that they think His Majesty upholds the Chinese rites as a favour to the Jesuits, and that he would abrogate them if he had not been requested by the Jesuits to maintain them?

It would be going beyond what I intend to do in this record, if I argued my case and demonstrated to what extent the Emperor believes that it is his obligation to preserve intact in China the customary rites of Confucius and the ancestors. But it seems opportune that this fact should be established by one example which is so well known to many missionaries that it cannot be denied. About 11 years ago, there was a Governor by the name of Cao who came from Guangdong.[^p562-44] He is still alive, and his son, one of the Doctors of the Hanlin <!-- PDF page 563 | book page 553 | manuscript page 669 --> yuan, lives in Beijing. At the time when this man, according to the law of the empire, should have resigned his magistracy because his mother had died in Guangdong, he of his own choice remained in the province which was very far distant. He relied on the personal favour he enjoyed with the Emperor, and also on the fact that he was recognized as a Tartar and was not so strictly bound by the laws of China. He presented a petition and showed how well he had fulfilled the duties of his office for two years, and that if he resigned his magistracy it would be to the detriment of the public good. The Emperor was willing to give him a dispensation on the grounds that it was a special case, instructing him not to resign his office to mourn the death of his mother because here and now this seemed necessary for the safety of the empire. Not long afterwards, one of the Censors came forward and accused the said Cao before the Emperor of being guilty of an offence against the rules of all propriety and the laws of what was held to be most sacred. When at first he ignored the accusation, that person repeated it, and next in a rhetorical manner described the shamelessness of a man (this is the way he spoke) who would go so far as to subvert filial responsibility. The Emperor was distressed, seeing that he had caused himself to be exposed to the animosity of the Chinese people while the Viceroy was being depicted as having violated the laws in such a paltry fashion.

He did not want to be deprived of the power of being able to grant a dispensation, at least in a single case, so he dismissed that censor from office. This man, in a third complaint, thanked him for being freed from the burdens of office, | but he included in his words of thanks that for the third time he stood by his view that His Majesty should not permit that the obligations of children should be allowed to lapse through the irresponsibility of that Cao, on the grounds that it was for the public good. He held it as certain that the empire could not remain secure if the duties of children towards their parents were not enforced according to the laws. Finally, the Emperor ordered the Governor to deliver the seal to his successor and to mourn his mother at home for three years, according to the law and custom of China. From this instance it is clear: 1. that such a law is actually in force; 2. that it has such force that without being subject to most severe censure, the Emperor cannot dispense from it even in an individual case; and 3. that the Emperor had acted in this particular case to the best of his ability to exempt only one man from the rite, and did not succeed without creating a critical situation both of scandal and of antipathy towards His Majesty, which by the Chinese is now broadcast widely and destined to be remembered forever.

<!-- PDF page 564 | book page 554 -->

How then is he described as being so lax and liberal that although he issued his decrees in the presence of the Lord Patriarch and at Nanjing against Fathers San Giorgio and Hervé, etc., and thundered against the Europeans who were violating the rites and preaching in favour of their violation; despite this, say the excellent Fathers of Shandong, he would have connived at their offence if only he had not heard the petitions of the Jesuits pleading for the preservation of the rites? What mighty obstacles placed by others does not the human heart imagine when it does not get what it wants!

As for their saying that all that is left is that the Sultan of Turkey should also write to Rome on behalf of the Jesuits, they say very little, for many Franciscans have written in favour of the Jesuits in this regard, and indeed it is they who have provided proof of what they held, both in manuscript and in printed books, as has been demonstrated personally to Clement XI and is established in books printed under his orders. There are some who have written on the opposite side, but it is clear to the reader how these for the most part have written incompetently and how there is no comparison between the latter and the former. If they had zeal, they did not disguise their real feelings, and I leave the decision to the reader of both.

### 49. A reply to the sixth point

Re 6. The Emperor will truly be sick and tired of the matter, and in fact he is already such, as here in Beijing we are well aware. But it is not to be hoped that he will abandon the case just to avoid being further bothered; rather it is to be feared that he will loosen the reins of his anger and with one stroke get rid of all future troubles for himself, and throw out the missionaries from China.

If only the Very Reverend Fathers would acknowledge the many, frequent and long-lasting annoyances that were inflicted on the Emperor when the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch, even after the Emperor had made an appeal to the Supreme Pontiff, tried to disregard, despite no decision having been made yet by the Pontiff and on his authority alone, the declarations made by the Emperor, even though they were authoritative on matters relating to Chinese language and customs! If only they would recognize that he assigned the case to be judged by the Bishop of Conon, who was manifestly ignorant of Chinese affairs and who had in his ignorance and inexperience given false information to the Holy See! And if only they would acknowledge also that, even if it is not a miracle, it is the next best thing to one that a monarch, incomparably the greatest and the most powerful in the Orient—whether he is idolater or atheist is irrelevant—having been provoked so unnecessarily by the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch, has restrained his anger better than would perhaps some Catholic princes who are not the equals of this monarch!

As for the Lord Patriarch being recalled with honour, that is something that you might prophesy with a smile on your face, and which a prudent person might wish but not hope for, for there is no chance whatsoever that this wonder is likely to eventuate. If however it should happen, we will praise the right hand of the Omnipotent in which are the hearts of kings.

Re 7. This is said through sheer ignorance of Chinese ways. The basis of their ignorance is that some people judge Chinese affairs by comparing them with something similar in Europe. There are good missionaries who would wish to be addressed by some title which would give them authority among the gentiles as well as among the Christians, | and which they would accept for the honour and love of God, without however making any sacrifice of the humility which becomes a Religious. Since this was not granted by the Emperor, it seems to them that they have a just cause for complaint. However they wished the Emperor to speak of them as Doctors, a title which means far more in China than it does in Europe.

I do not pass judgment here on the thinking of those who want this, but I do wish that all of those who speak in this way would reflect on whether they would dare among their own confreres in Europe to request or allow that they would be proclaimed Doctors by the Most Christian King, the Catholic King, or the Emperor.[^p565-45] Why then are they resentful that something is not granted to them in China which they would not dare to request in Europe without embarrassment and confusion? What happened to them in Linqing in this regard happened also in Nanjing, Suzhou, Hangzhou, and Yangzhou to almost 30 Jesuits, and not one of them complained that their qualifications were ignored on account of an undistinguished title or a graceless name.

I say again that those who seek these things do not know what they are saying. Great honour is conferred in these letters patent, but they do not recognize it. In them it is stated that after having been summoned and admitted to his presence they have looked on and spoken to His Majesty, a privilege for which many Chinese would be willing to pay talents of silver. But while the Emperor testifies in the letters patent that this honour has been most generously bestowed on this one and that one and on all the individual missionaries, he also opens the way for them to the tribunals of all the mandarins, so that unless they themselves give cause for deciding differently, they may not be barred by any official, even a viceroy, for they are men for whom the Dragon Gate has opened and they have been considered worthy for the Emperor to <!-- PDF page 566 | book page 556 | manuscript page 671 --> cast his eyes on them. It is also a remarkable honour that the letters patents are strengthened by the seal of the Inner Palace, which is a sign that missionaries are reckoned among the household and domestics of the Emperor, and by this fact they are freed from the power of the tribunals, so that the mandarins cannot make any decision involving them in the affairs mentioned there without reference to the Court. Let them pay due respect to their letters patent and they themselves will be honoured. This is what Fr. da Silva, Fr. Barelli, Fr. Baborier, Fr. Domenge, and Fr. Buonaventura da Roma did, and they have been left free of any molestation by the mandarins.

Miguel Fernández says that an envoy was sent to Macau by the Emperor so that the Lord Patriarch would be kept there until the two Jesuit Fathers returned from Rome; he adds that another qinchai[^p566-46] was sent secretly to order that the missionaries who had been sent into banishment by the Emperor should likewise be detained there, and that the Emperor’s anger had cooled because the Lord Patriarch had written a letter to the Emperor in which he humbled himself. Franchi following the account given by Father Miguel Fernández. It is true that such a young man was engaged, not in fact for the service of the Prince but for the personal service of one who belonged to the Prince’s household. Whatever this young man may have said, the result proved that the contrary was true, when several missionaries were permitted to leave Macau for another destination and no one was unconditionally detained. ### 51. The ninth point is weighed

Re 9. This observation arises from the first impression which forcibly strikes the minds of some Europeans before they reflect for a second or third time. In fact however it proves nothing. | They might argue in this way: The Emperor allows the Muslims to bury <!-- PDF page 567 | book page 557 --> their dead in their own way, and to disregard the rites for the dead, etc., therefore with more reason he will allow the Christians to disregard them. These arguments have no more force than if they were to say: The people of Amsterdam permit free exercise to seventeen different sects, to Jews, to Adamites,[^p567-48] etc., therefore much more will they permit this to Catholics. They would more rightly conclude: Therefore much more they ought to allow it, and this is true, but it is false to say that the people of Amsterdam do actually allow it and that the Emperor does allow it.

Then too it is false to say that the Emperor does permit the Muslims to disregard the Chinese rites for Confucius, and I mean those to whom the rites pertain. For it is well known in China that many Muslims study letters and take examinations to qualify for degrees to gain the office of mandarin, and today there are many xueguan[^p567-49] or prefects of scholars who live in the School of Literature and Confucius and whose ex officio obligation it is to perform the rites of Confucius and to impose and enforce their performance by others. Has any missionary at all in the last 100 years or more ever seen that such Muslims were permitted to omit, let alone to deprecate, the Confucian rites? If the commoners who are Muslims are permitted to disregard what is due to Confucius, the same is permitted to Christians, and even to gentiles. For no one is bound to recognize Confucius as master unless he so wishes, but once he has decided to devote himself to letters and to live his life with the privileges of men of letters, he is bound to those things which are common, and to the more particular obligations relating to the office which men of letters have accepted; therefore it is false to say that the Emperor permits the Muslims to be remiss in honouring Confucius. But it is true that in this matter he makes the same concessions to both Christians and Muslims.

With regard to the rites for the dead, it is true that Muslim commoners do not use the tablets, and even though privately the Emperor is aware of this, he does not forbid it. But it is also true that for this reason they are generally detested and that the name of Muslim is a term of reproach among the Chinese. The reason why charges are not brought against them, and why consequently the <!-- PDF page 568 | book page 558 | manuscript page 672 --> tribunals do not apply the penal laws against them, is because many centuries ago, in time of war they came into the kingdom with the Tartars as military allies, and with continuous natural increase they have grown in numbers up to present day. And in fact they have increased to such an extent that in some of the provinces they have virtual control of entire cities and are a force to be reckoned with in wealth, numbers and their military prowess in the empire. But on the other hand, if all the Christians throughout China were gathered into one place they would not equal a hundredth part or even a thousandth part of the Muslims. And in the course of half of a century they have twice been solemnly accused of trying to overturn the rites and the laws of China. It is well known that this happened in the time of the exile of all the missionaries in the years …50 when Yang Guangxian[^p568-51] stirred up a persecution, and in the year … when Zhang Peng’ge,[^p568-52] who is still alive today and who is more powerful than all the viceroys, lodged an accusation. And hence the mandarins are all the more vigilant to see that Christians do not speak or act against the rites, because they see that so many Muslims disregard the tablets. They do not want the ceremonies for the ancestors to be abolished by two classes of people lest, as they say, the whole of China should degenerate into moral barbarism.

It must be made clear also that long ago a printed book was given by us to the Emperor in which we gave the reasons why in the cult of the dead we do not perform or allow superstitious practices. Nor, for this reason, did the Emperor persecute Christians; in fact, he favoured us all the more, openly granting freedom for the Christian religion after this communication. Therefore the complaints made by the Reverend Fathers are frivolous, whether they want to bring accusations against the Fathers of the Society on the grounds that the Emperor though their fault grants less to the Christians than | to the Muslims, or because they wish to prove from the example of the Muslims that it is possible to abolish the Chinese rites. No one doubts that they can be prohibited by <!-- PDF page 569 | book page 559 --> the power of the Supreme Pontiff. But whether they would be prohibited to the greater gain or loss of the Church and of souls, already sad experience has made more than sufficiently clear.

### 53. The 14th point is examined

Re 14. If the Emperor’s declaration, which is new, repeated, and has been dispatched to the Supreme Pontiff, is worthless or nearly so because it was not published, as they say, it follows therefore it would have been very profitable if it had been published; therefore the one who prevented its publication seriously hindered the salvation of souls, and that person did far more who before this declaration could reach the Supreme Pontiff wanted to make it ineffective by publishing the opposite. However, to prevent my pen from straying too far, and so that it may give a more concise answer, I say that it definitely cannot be concluded that the declaration was of no value because it was not published. For it to be useless, it would have to be proved that it was incorrect. For if it is true, why would it not be effective in facilitating the progress of the Gospel, and the conversion of the whole of China? These are the things which we want to do, and anything that contributes honestly to achieve these purposes is of value, whether it is published or private. And even though what is published has more weight, anything that is private is not to be disregarded.

I put this question too: If the Emperor had declared in the same way that the word Tian means for the Chinese a material heaven etc., would they have regarded that as a public declaration? Would they publish it themselves? Why then do they deny that it is a public declaration and prohibit its publication when the Emperor has said that Tian means an immaterial heaven, the living and true God, the same one which the Christians adore? Are we saying that a declaration made by the Emperor to the whole Tartaro-Chinese Court, and to the Europeans summoned from the Lord Patriarch’s side for this very purpose, and moreover ordered both verbally and in writing to be sent also through the Lord Patriarch to the Supreme Pontiff, and actually sent there through two Jesuits, is to be regarded as private while the Lord Patriarch’s regulation read before eight Europeans will be considered to have been published? When something is public knowledge in Beijing, how can the provinces be ignorant of it? Let those who may so wish make enquiries in China as to why now Europeans are bound by public edict to obtain a permit if they want to remain in the empire, and immediately they will understand that this comes about because the Europeans dispute the meaning of the Chinese rites, and because the Emperor has explained his meaning with amazing patience and that he has banished those who stubbornly reject his explanation. Now that a law has been passed with exile as the penalty for all those who preach against the rites as explained by His Majesty, surely they do not ask for further publication!

For something to be considered public in a general sense, the manner of its publication is not important; nor here was there any need for this publication to be made with all the legal formalities. For the Emperor was not declaring <!-- PDF page 572 | book page 562 | manuscript page 674 --> something new with the intention of binding the Chinese to hold that the word Tian means the immaterial Heaven. Rather he declared to the Europeans, from the fullness of his knowledge of Chinese teaching, that the Chinese have held his belief and still hold it. He was not obliging the Europeans to believe this, but in a spirit of goodwill he was stating it to be true, to the end that if they embraced it in accordance with his explanation of the Chinese words they could preach the Gospel and remain in China. But if they rejected what the Chinese words said and still preached, they would be banished | from China. And if publication in this way does not seem to the Very Rev. Fathers to be sufficient, even when it was made face to face by the Emperor to the Lord Patriarch, the Bishop of Conon, Father Appiani, Angelita the Secretary, the Jesuits, and the Court, and sent to the Supreme Pontiff in writing, at least they will not be able to deny that throughout the whole of China, the penalty was published against any Europeans who preached against the rites as explained by the Emperor and against the meaning of the Chinese expressions as determined by the Emperor.

### 54. A reply to points 15 and 16

And in this, no injustice is done to the person of His Excellency by those who, from one who demands trust, in their turn demand that he should establish trust according to the canons and produce the pontifical decree, by showing it at least to the local ordinaries by whom it was requested in accordance with the law. However, he has never shown it to anyone either in the original or in an authenticated transcript, and he has not even shown his faculties to the Primate of the East, the Lord Archbishop of Goa, or his vicar for this region, the Lord Bishop of Macau, nor to the vicars apostolic or to the local Ordinaries within China. And he freely admits that he has not presented them. On what title established by law therefore is everyone obliged to trust one who, disregarding the law, refuses to prove the legitimacy of his own office and of his faculties?

### 57. Some of the happenings at Guangzhou in the month of June

The Most Excellent Lord had returned to that city from Beijing on 24 May, as was reported above, grief-stricken and in desolation on account of the events recorded as having happened in Nanjing, in Hangzhou, and in Nan’an during the previous months. His grief gave rise to such anger that he prepared himself to get revenge, substituting suspicions for real proof, and <!-- PDF page 576 | book page 566 --> because at that particular time and place there was no opportunity to institute suitable proceedings against the Jesuits, either genuine or trumped up, some people were content to calumniate the Beijing Jesuits, denying what was true and affirming what was untrue. They regarded this as a matter of indifference provided that they were able to some extent to satisfy their mindless feelings of rivalry. The things that were said and spread abroad in Guangzhou, although all were spread for the same purpose, were in the main of two kinds. For some, to all appearances, related to the controversies; others touched the person of the Beijing Jesuits. But at the same time all aimed at affronting and calumniating the said Fathers.

Therefore the reader will notice that the accusations against individuals are put first, then there are added the complaints made against all in general, arising from the controversies. ### 62. An answer to points 7, 8, 9, 10, and

Re 7. It is true that the Lord Patriarch proposed this at Beijing in about the middle of January 1706, and it is also

### 11 of the accusation

true that Father Kilian replied that it is a false comparison and that he would submit written proof of this, proof which had already been prepared, as soon as His Excellency was willing to accept it in juridical form; but the Lord Patriarch did not ever permit this.

Moreover, an answer to this comparison has been given so often both in China and in Europe, that it is remarkable that it should come up for discussion again. The honour paid to God and the honour paid to men are subordinate to one another, in the same way that religious obligations to God and our filial duty to parents are related, but not in such a way that duty to parents is of the same order as religion, nor the honour paid to men is of the same order as honour paid to God. St. Thomas distinguished these brilliantly in 2a. 2ae. q. 102 ad. 1.[^p582-72] And nothing throws so much light on it as that most divine utterance of Christ when in accordance with his teaching authority and power he was instructing the learned man of the synagogue, and said in the 22nd chapter of Matthew: The second commandment is like the first—that the neighbour is to be loved, definitely not with divine love, as if man very closely resembled God, but with a similarity according to precept, intention, love, and end. They are similar in that God gave us this precept of this very love since man is the image of God, because man is to be loved on account of God, and because likewise eternal reward has been promised for this love. Apply these principles to the thanksgiving prescribed for Confucius in China and to the dutiful remembrance of dead parents among Christians, while always rigorously rejecting whatever in the course of times has accrued due to superstition and the defects of ignorant men.

### 64. The examination of Father Sabino Mariani and his answers

In the 45th year of Kangxi, in the last month, on the 27th day,[^p584-76] the first-born son of the Emperor (the Prince) together with the courtiers [Zhang] Changzhu and Zhao Chang questioned Sha Guoan (Father Sabino) in this way: “Was Duoluo (the Lord Patriarch) really sent by the Supreme Pontiff? Does he have anything to establish the authenticity of his legation? Were you sent by the Supreme Pontiff with Duoluo? Did you come in his entourage of your own free will? If in fact you did come with Duoluo, doubtlessly you are perfectly well aware of the reason why he came. Finally, when Pursai tried to recover the royal gifts from you, you <!-- PDF page 585 | book page 575 | manuscript page 683 --> refused to hand them over, but demanded something which would establish his credentials. Why did you act in this way?”

Father Sabino’s reply: “I was not sent by the Supreme Pontiff with Duoluo (the Lord Patriarch); but since I had previously wanted to come to China, I seized this favourable opportunity and came with Duoluo. Duoluo was in fact sent by the Supreme Pontiff. But I am completely ignorant of the reason for his coming. It is not true that I demanded from Pursai something which would establish his credentials. I do indeed admit that Pursai, who had been ordered by the Emperor to conduct us as far as Guangzhou, did return there afterwards and did demand back the gifts from me. But why, before I delivered them to him, would I have required something to establish his credentials? I believe that the mistake was made by the one who translated my words.”

After this, Sha Guoan (Fr. Sabino Mariani) was questioned further in this way: “Have you heard that Yan Dang (the Bishop of Conon, | Charles Maigrot) is criticized very severely by Christians because he does not follow the practices of Li Madou (Father Ricci); because he speaks imprudently of the teachings of Confucius, Mencius, etc.; and because he tramples on the Royal Tablet of the Emperor ( Jing Tian) and gives orders that it is not to be displayed? If you did indeed come to China to stay, tell us whether you follow Li Madou or Yan Dang (the Bishop of Conon) and Fang Zhou (Father Guéty).” Father Sabino replied: “I have heard nothing at all of this business of the Bishop of Conon. I arrived in China only recently, and since I do not know the language, teachings, and customs of China, I am not yet able to decide whether I should follow Li Madou (Ricci) or the Bishop of Conon and Father Guéty. But when I shall have learned the language and rites of this kingdom, then I shall follow the practice of the party which I can see has the majority of followers.”

On the 28th day of that same month, when what is related above had been communicated to the Emperor, His Majesty said: “Even though this man came with Duoluo, nevertheless he says he does not know the reason for his coming! If the gifts have been recalled, it was their fault; the cause of their being brought back is not clear or praiseworthy either. If indeed, as he himself said, he did not come precisely to accompany Duoluo, but as it were on his own account, it was not unreasonable to summon him to come back here. Nevertheless, let him be told that since he came with Duoluo he is to go back with him. And let me know what he answers.”

On the 29th day of the same month, the Emperor’s eldest son together with the courtiers Hescken, Zhang Changzhu, and Zhao Chang communicated the Emperor’s message to Father Sabino. He replied to this effect: “After I have thanked to the Emperor for all his kindness, I want to set out on the 2nd day of next month and to return to the Lord Patriarch.” When this had been reported <!-- PDF page 586 | book page 576 | manuscript page 684 --> to the Emperor, His Majesty on the same day gave his orders and said: “Hand this man over to the mandarin Guo Zhaojin, the son of Guo Shilong the zongdu of the province of Guangdong, who is to see that he is conducted back to Duoluo (the Lord Patriarch).”[^p586-77]

### 65. Who was this Father Sabino Mariani?

If he reflects on this carefully, the reader will easily understand that we should pay little attention to whatever this Fr. Sabino Mariani and his friends say, whether they are affirming or denying that he requested a permit so he could remain in China. For since he was the Lord Patriarch’s auditor; says that he came with the Lord Patriarch merely because the occasion presented itself and that he is completely ignorant of the reason why the Lord Patriarch came to China; denies that he demanded credentials from Pursai, which in fact he had done in the presence of several missionaries, and that he had ever heard anything of those events involving the Bishop of Conon; and claims this even when he is being questioned personally on behalf of the Emperor in legal form, he will feel even less shame in denying everything which we have proven to be true and in affirming everything to the contrary, even things which he knows to be false.

This is not however to be attributed to lax theology, as if he learned it on a ship, but to an ingrained habit, for this priest during the whole of his life was a pettifogging lawyer in Rome; then, he abruptly laid aside the writing instruments of an attorney and assuming the garb of a cleric attached himself to the Lord Patriarch for this expedition after being made an auditor | for the Apostolic Visitation.
