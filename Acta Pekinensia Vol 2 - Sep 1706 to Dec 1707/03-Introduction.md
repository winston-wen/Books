The Acta Pekinensia is a significant document, not only in the history of the Catholic missions in China and more generally in Catholic church history but also in the history of Sino-Western relations. It recounts the papal legation to China of Charles Thomas Maillard de Tournon, from his arrival in China in April 1705 to his death in Macau in July 1710—and the immediate aftermath.[^p11-1]

It was written by a German Jesuit missionary-scientist who worked in various capacities at the Chinese court in Beijing. Kilian Stumpf was, at the time of the legation, director of the Imperial Glassworks located in the Beitang or French Jesuit Church where he, and the Legate himself, resided during his time in Beijing. The Jesuit General had appointed Stumpf to be the rapporteur for the papal legation and secured for him the status of a papal notary.[^p11-2] And the Vice-Province of China appointed him their procurator in their dealings with Maillard de Tournon.

After the Legate left Beijing Stumpf continued his task, recording the events and documentation relating to the affairs of the legation and its impact on the China mission. Necessarily this section, of which the present volume is the first instalment, is more dependent on letters and reports from other parts of China than immediate experience but the title of Acta Pekinensia (“What Happened in Beijing”) is still justified since the perspective is that of the imperial court and the reaction of the Emperor Kangxi himself to the actions of the Legate, and the effects of his determinations dominates the narrative.

It may be useful to summarize here the events of the Legate’s eight months in Beijing (4 December 1705 to 28 August 1706) which are covered in the first 400 pages of Stumpf’s manuscript and the first volume of our translation and summed up in a Compendium by Stumpf, which we translate as a kind of prologue to this volume.

Maillard de Tournon arrived in Beijing seriously ill and his illness, which made him not only physically feeble but often very irritable, seems to have contributed to the failure of his embassy despite the many relaxations of protocol granted by the Kangxi emperor. Furthermore, for reasons never fully explained, he failed to present his credentials to the Emperor. He represented himself as a religious man, envoy of the head of his religion, and come on a religious mission to visit and regulate the members of that religion. This may have been partly due to a felt difficulty in explaining that the Pope was both a religious leader and a temporal sovereign. But he was addressing a ruler one of whose main duties was to supervise the orthodoxy of the behaviour and beliefs of his subjects. Far from being pleased with his less formal reception the Legate stood on his dignity and demanded, while a guest in the Jesuit residence and under constant observation by Chinese servants, the full honours of a prince of the church, which were of a kind that in China were only given to members of the imperial family.

Tournon had an audience with the Emperor on 31 December 1705. Moreover there was considerable traffic between the Emperor and the Legate conducted through Imperial Household officials, whom Maillard de Tournon mistrusted and seriously misunderstood and underestimated. Communications were further confounded by poor interpreters and the Legate’s distrust of Jesuit interpreters. At the first audience on 31 December the Legate insisted that he had no other business except to thank the Emperor for his favours to the Christian religion but he had already raised issues such as appointing a permanent representative of the Pope in Beijing, which was contrary to Chinese precedent. Even at the level of formalities problems quickly arose. When the Emperor made arrangements for the usual exchange of gifts, Tournon rejected the Jesuit Joachim Bouvet chosen by the Emperor as a gift-bearer as a sign of his special approval—Bouvet was a member of his own household as an imperial tutor in mathematics—and refused to name him as such in the official communique to be approved by both parties.

The missionaries—and soon the Chinese Christians and the Emperor— were well aware that the chief business of the papal embassy was the Chinese Rites question which had been debated in Rome for a decade or more. Tournon refused to endorse Kangxi’s position, which had been formally announced in an imperial declaration of 30 November 1700, and members of his entourage attempted to impose their views about ancestor rites and rituals for Confucius on the Christians of Beijing. The Legate believed that the matter had been all but decided before he left Rome in July 1702 but he was not to get definite news of the decision taken in Rome in November 1704 until after he had left Beijing. Then Tournon took a fatal step by calling to Beijing to be his champion on the

<!-- PDF page 13 | book page 3 -->

Rites question Charles Maigrot, Vicar Apostolic of Fujian, who had started the renewed controversy by formally condemning the Chinese Rites in 1693 and referring the matter to Rome.

In February 1706 the Legate ordered the Jesuits to request permission for him to buy a house in Beijing despite their warning that the Emperor did not want permanent diplomatic representation of the Holy See in China. They reluctantly raised the matter and the Emperor forbade them to raise it again, for which they were blamed by the Legate. De Tournon then shifted ground and began a long series of inquiries in an attempt to convict the Jesuits of usury over their leases of houses to provide a regular income for the activities of the mission. Despite their many representations of Chinese law and customs demonstrating that the transactions were leases, not usurious loans, Tournon condemned them and removed from office the Jesuit Visitor Grimaldi as well as the leading Portuguese Jesuit and intimate of the Emperor, Tomás Pereira. He also insisted on the Bishop of Beijing, Bernardino della Chiesa, making a formal visitation of the Portuguese Jesuit College, contrary to their papal privileges. Finally, while recuperating at the hot springs outside Beijing in June Tournon requested a confidential interview with the Emperor to discuss matters concerning his “household,” probably to complain about the Jesuits. The Emperor was infuriated and recalled the papal gifts.

On 29 June Kangxi gave a second decidedly cool audience to the Legate followed by entertainment in his gardens outside Beijing, and then departed for his summer hunting expedition in Manchuria. Meanwhile Bishop Maigrot, who had just arrived in Beijing, was interrogated by the household mandarins and then sent to Manchuria for further interrogation by the Emperor himself. The Emperor from Manchuria ordered Tournon to leave Beijing after writing a final communiqué, to be approved by Kangxi and the Legate, which led to further disputes with officials over the contents. After a very unsatisfactory encounter with Maigrot in Karahoton, Kangxi finally issued a decree of disapproval of Tournon’s actions and ordered him to leave for the South, which he did on 28 August.

This is the point at which the narrative in the present volume begins. While he was on his slow journey by water to the south of China, Maillard de Tournon and his suite became more and more convinced that all their problems were due to the machinations of the Jesuits. They began to claim that his illness was due to poisoning by the Jesuits, that the Beijing Fathers had entrapped Maigrot into coming to Beijing, and that the Emperor’s recall to Beijing of the Legate’s Chinese secretary and his interpreter was their doing.

The Emperor after his encounter with Maigrot became more and more irate and began to exile the Europeans associated with Maillard de Tournon, including <!-- PDF page 14 | book page 4 --> Maigrot. He ordered that the official documents of the legation be collected from the imperial archives and sent to Rome, and sent two of the court Jesuits, Barros and Beauvollier, as his personal envoys to the Pope to accompany the documents. Unfortunately they and the documents perished in a storm off the coast of Portugal. Further envoys and another set of documents were sent later but by the time they arrived Maillard de Tournon’s version of events had prevailed in Rome. Kangxi also called to Beijing Maigrot’s Chinese secretaries, whom he accused of not disillusioning the bishop of his false ideas about Chinese beliefs and practices. And crucially, he placed his eldest son, who had long been suspicious of the growing numbers of Westerners in China, in charge of the affairs of the Europeans.

In December 1706 the Emperor ordered that any missionaries who wished to stay in China must receive a piao or residence permit which would be issued on condition that they promised to follow the practices of Matteo Ricci, that is, the Jesuit practice regarding the Chinese Rites. Much of the later sections of the Acta Pekinensia is devoted to this issue: the apparent inconsistencies in granting or denying the piao, the Legate’s insistence that if they apply for it missionaries must simultaneously reject the condition, and subsequent the expulsions of large numbers of missionaries including some Jesuits.

Poor communications with Europe caused further problems. When Tournon’s first very optimistic accounts of his reception reached Rome, Pope Clement XI issued a congratulatory brief to Maillard de Tournon and before long promoted him to cardinal. By the time his red hat arrived in China the Legate was under house arrest in Macau by explicit order of the Emperor. He was not to be allowed to leave China until the Emperor’s envoys to Rome had returned.

The main cause of Kangxi’s anger was Tournon’s actions in Nanjing. On 7 February 1707 he issued a decree forbidding the Rites and demanded an oath of obedience to it from missionaries. It is interesting that he wrote to the Pope the next day admitting that he had not received a formal copy of the 1704 decree, and in fact he probably never did; yet he justified his actions to the missionaries by claiming that he had. Nor does he seem aware that the decree while formulated had not been promulgated and did not fully correspond to his own regulations. Many, including the Bishop of Beijing, had already applied for and received their piao. Henceforth missionaries interviewed by the Eldest Prince on behalf of the Emperor during Kangxi’s southern tour were asked where they stood: some replied ambiguously and were granted their permit, others were expelled.

The Jesuits and many others appealed to the Pope against the Nanjing regulations, pointing out that it was not only based on false information but would result in the destruction of Christianity in China. And meanwhile the

Portuguese authorities in Goa and Macau rejected the Legate’s jurisdiction as contrary to their long established padroado over the Asian missions. After his arrival in Macau at the end of June 1706 the Legate proceeded not only to condemn their actions but to excommunicate the leading officials of Macau, some of the Jesuits, and other leading churchmen including Bishop Cazal.

The year 1707 ended, as does this volume of the Acta, with yet another imperial edict against Maillard de Tournon and the imprisonment in Beijing of Ludovico Appiani, who had been Maillard de Tournon’s interpreter. Also there occurred in December 1707 the first cases, soon to be multiplied, of local officials not respecting the residence permits of missionaries. The news of Kangxi’s cooling off towards the Christian Church had clearly begun to spread on the bureaucratic grapevine.

The events presented in this second volume of the Acta Pekinensia are less dramatic than those in the first but are full of interesting insights into the conflicts among the China missionaries and religious bodies, the internal processes of the imperial household, and the personalities of the Emperor and his eldest son. Some are first-hand reports by Stumpf himself, some by others of the “Beijing Fathers,” and many from Jesuits and others in the provinces (the originals in several languages). Also included are the detailed appeals against the Legate’s decisions by senior and experienced non-Jesuit churchmen. Together they give a varied and nuanced picture of the complex situation of Christianity in China and the consequences of the actions of Charles Thomas Maillard de Tournon, Patriarch of Antioch, as Legate a latere and Apostolic Visitor to China.

As in the first volume, the translation is based on a transcription from the original manuscript of the “Acta Pekinensia” in the Jesuit Roman Archives ( Jap. Sin.138) made by three Latinists in Poznan (Poland): Monika Miazek- Meczynska, Ewa Jarmakowska, and Katarzyna Prychitko. A first draft translation was then made by Jesuit classicists (in Australia, Joseph Holland, John Wilcken, John Begley, and Stan Hogan; also Gerard J. Hughes of Campion Hall, Oxford University). This was then revised and annotated by Paul Rule and Claudia von Collani, who take ultimate responsibility for the text as it now appears. The Compendium was translated by Paul Rule with the assistance of Claudia von Collani.
