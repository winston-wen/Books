December 1st. Our people in Guangzhou sent news that the Very Reverend Carlo Turcotti had died there in the month of October. He was the Vicar Apostolic in Guizhou and bishop-designate of Andreville, and a member of our Society. In addition Fr. Tomás Pereira early today suffered a partial stroke. The Bishop of Conon briefly replied to the news and said that this gave him an opportunity to go to the College both to visit the Fathers and to excuse the foolish behaviour of Father Guéty, or even to make amends for it. He said that he might be able to persuade some of us that the same Father had produced his flowers or rather thorns like this from a garden other than his. Father Guéty had done nothing other than what he used to do when in the company of his fellow clerics:[^p241-1] verbally criticize the Portuguese, whether in general or as individuals. “Indeed,” replied Father Superior Gerbillon, “there seemed some foundation for this, since it is a known fact that Guéty had gone from his father’s inn to the workshop of the clockmaker N. Martinot, from which he came to China where he learned to read the breviary at the hands of the Reverend Gentlemen who were educated in the Seminary.”[^p241-2]

### 1. Letter of Father Mezzafalce to Doctor Borghese

December 2nd. This morning the mandarin Zhang Charki, in whose house Father Mezzafalce remained confined, showed the Prince[^p241-3] a letter he had written to Doctor Borghese. Fr. Parrenin was summoned and translated it, showing easily that it only contained a request to obtain clothes made of lighter skins suitable for the biting winter weather which he could wear inside the house, since he found woollen garments such as we wear indoors too heavy. It must be noted moreover that Father Mezzafalce chose to apply to Doctor Borghese in this matter because on the 25th of the preceding month he had received garments from the same individual suitable for going out of doors. Such lined skins are worth in Beijing a hundred Roman scudi, except those finished with silver and gold thread.

### 2. Petition of the scholars of Fujian summoned to the royal court

In the evening there arrived the petition of one or, as they wished to have it, two scholars of Fujian, sent from Huaian[^p242-4] by one of our Fathers, Manoel de Sousa,[^p242-5] a missionary there. These scholars are the ones whom the Emperor had ordered to be summoned before the Military Tribunal, having been denounced by Fr. Guéty in the month of September as teachers of the Chinese language to the Bishop of Conon. Since they were now closer to the royal court they thought a pre-emptive measure was in order. They formed the idea of presenting a petition to the Emperor either together or individually. Finally, Xavier Jiang,[^p242-6] who had a Master’s degree in literature and was an apostate in regard to religion, finished his petition in Huaian and sent it ahead through the good offices of the Father. The other was a faithful Christian with a Bachelor’s degree in Chinese literature. He completed his work during the remainder of the journey.

This is what Fr. Manoel de Sousa wrote about them both on 17 November 1706:

Today I received in my church the two men from Fujian, the juren[^p242-7] and the xiucai[^p242-8] who have been called by the Emperor. They asked me to send to Your Reverence the enclosed bin.[^p242-9] Their intention was that I beg Your Reverence that when they arrive in Beijing Your Reverence should present this bin in their names to the Emperor. I send this copy by courier before they arrive to entreat Your Reverence to see to it also that you might establish good relations between the Christians of Beijing and those of Fujian. They begged me in these terms: that I should see to it that they receive them since the Christians of Beijing might not be friendly towards them or regard them as enemies for contradicting up until now the Chinese Rites (these are their very words). They requested that before they arrive in Beijing Your Reverence should present this bin to the Emperor. However, then they contradicted themselves and requested | that when they have arrived in Beijing they might seek out Your Reverence to help turn the bin into a better version and gain your assistance in offering it <!-- PDF page 243 | book page 233 --> to the Emperor. The reason why this bin which I send to you by courier is by the juren called Jiang, Christian name Francis Xavier, is that the xiucai has not yet finished his bin but will do so as soon as he arrives in Beijing, and they beg that Your Reverence should, through my intercession, grant them the favour of offering the two bin to the Emperor. I could not in charity fail to do what I could for them so I told them I would write to Your Reverence with a plea on their behalf … 17th November 1706.[^p243-10]

### 3. The Fathers decide that the petition should not be sent to the Emperor

This petition, in respect to its contents only, seemed able to be presented to the Emperor with some hope of success, given the fact that it not only excused its author but significantly shifted the blame even from the Very Reverend Bishop of Conon himself. It declared that he had thus far acted out of a scruple rather than from ill-will or stubbornness against the Chinese Rites. Taking into account other circumstances, however, the Fathers decided that nothing should be attempted until the scholars themselves had arrived. This was because, suddenly summoned to the Prince’s presence, as happened with Fr. Mezzafalce, whether through terror or for some other reason, they might say things which clashed with the wording of the petition. This was particularly because the name of only one of them was affixed and the frame of mind of the other was not known, nor what version of the petition might be read out in the future. The version of the petition and what happened at Beijing concerning the petition will need to be examined under the 9th of December.

### 4. The Tartar sent by the Emperor to Guangzhou takes back the gifts assigned to the Pope

December 3rd. From Guangzhou were brought letters from the Tartar named Purgama or Pursai[^p243-11] who had set out for Guangzhou at the beginning of October. That person announced to the Prince and through him to the Emperor that within the space of twenty days he had arrived in Guangzhou and had already, in accordance with the imperial decree, received from the hands of Father Sabino the gifts sent from His Majesty to the Pope on January 1st. Happily he passed over in silence whatever had been done foolishly by Fr. Sabino, both his stubborn holding on to the gifts and his lack of respect for Fr. Bouvet which was shamefully apparent both to him and others. At the same time a letter <!-- PDF page 244 | book page 234 | manuscript page 475 --> arrived from Fr. Bouvet informing the Superior, Fr. J.F. Gerbillon, about the whole affair. Here I will set out his entire account to serve as a record of the incident.

A copy of Fr. Bouvet’s letter:[^p244-12]

Yesterday (October 30th) at midday Pursama entered our house. We had been warned of his arrival only half a quarter-hour before, although he had arrived the day before (October 29th) … We went together to find M. Sabino who had been given notice to come here since he was a little late. I wanted to make Pursama stay but he was in a hurry and I had no means of detaining him. After having a cup of tche[^p244-13] at M. Sabino’s house, Pursama told me to announce to M. Sabino the order which applied to him and me also. I had hardly begun to open my mouth when this poor gentleman, whom I pitied because of the peculiar mortification that he was feeling, let Pursama know in no uncertain terms, forcing himself to speak Chinese, that he did not trust me and that another interpreter should be called. Upon which, over my objections, he ordered in Chinese one of his servants to go and find Father Visdelou. I was obliged to tell him in a European language that Father Visdelou had nothing to do with this matter; that it would even be inconvenient for him to come since Pursama wanted to leave at once for Macau to carry out the orders of the Emperor and could not wait so long; and finally, that if he did not trust me, it would be better to go and find in | the nearest church the first missionary he could find etc. All this was to no avail; he held out for Father Visdelou. Seeing this, I sent myself to the two churches of the Franciscan Fathers which were nearer than ours, and ordered a servant to stand at the door of the house where we were, to tell Fr. Visdelou, if he came, that it was not convenient for him to enter. This I did because basing myself on the information you had given me, I judged that it would be very unfortunate if Father Visdelou were to get mixed up in this affair. While we waited for an interpreter, M. Sabino made M. de la Motte[^p244-14] come down. He knew a few words of Chinese which would have been enough, but M. Sabino was not content with this. After we had lost almost an hour waiting, along came Fr. Buonaventura,[^p244-15] who had lived in Nanjing.[^p244-16] Pursama announced the imperial <!-- PDF page 245 | book page 235 --> edict to him. M. Sabino, disregarding the warnings he had been given about the infinite harm that his peculiar protests would do to our religion, insulted this Father in the same way he had me, and said curtly that he wanted another interpreter.

### 5. The gifts are handed over against the resistance of Father Sabino

At this point Pursama ran out of patience, and said in a threatening tone of voice that he would send somebody to the Viceroy and the jiangjun[^p245-17] to take away the gifts by force. Hearing this, I took Pursama by the hands and entreated him not to inflict on us so bloody an affront. I excused as best I could M. Sabino, saying that he knew neither the language nor the customs and that in the embarrassing situation in which he found himself, of having to explain to Monsignor the Patriarch and to His Holiness about the presents that he had held in his hands for so long, it was pardonable that he should try to get some assurances, and that it was rather his embarrassment than defiance which was responsible for the delay. I begged M. de la Motte to join with Mgr. de Tilopolis[^p245-18] in persuading M. Sabino to see reason. M. Sabino seeing that I had taken efficacious measures to prevent Fr. Visdelou appearing, and not wishing any other interpreter than he, demanded a copy of the order, but he did not have anybody to copy it. Furthermore, Pursama seeing his bad manners did not want to give it to him. “What,” he said, “we have to return together to Beijing, and you do not trust me?” I was obliged to renew my requests and to represent to M. Sabino the bad reputation we were all getting through his fault. All this made no impression on him, nor all that Fr. Buonaventura and M. de la Motte, who seemed to totally disapprove of his conduct, could say. Finally Father Jaime Tarin[^p245-19] arrived, someone to whom M. Sabino seemed to have no objection, and after two hours he was obliged to give up and hand back the gifts, after having heard from his mouth of the substance of the order which affected both of us. Nonetheless, he saw fit to ask Pursama for greater security for a receipt which the latter refused to give him and that I tried to prevent him demanding, saying that it showed <!-- PDF page 246 | book page 236 | manuscript page 476 --> a lack of respect etc. After Pursama had finished his visit and taken back the gifts in the presence of the zhixian,[^p246-20] who had arrived at the end and assisted at this fine ceremony, in order to put M. Sabino’s mind at rest he took the Manchu order of His Majesty and explained it word for word in Chinese to Fr. Tarin, who, only understanding half of it, or rather, seeing | that all the blame was placed on the Lord Patriarch, excused himself from speaking. The result was that everyone including Pursama turned to me, and since M. Sabino raised no objection, I found myself obliged against my will to interpret this humiliating and terrible order. When I had done so, Pursama asked M. Sabino if he had understood. He replied, showing contempt for me, that he had not. All the servants and suite of the two mandarins and ourselves had heard the Chinese interpretation that had been made to Father Tarin to content M. Sabino … Pursama seeing such obvious discord between us, at least on the part of M. Sabino, reproached me for what I had written to Beijing, which was quite contrary to what he saw. All I could reply was to say that it really was the case for my part, that M. Sabino had for his part stated the same to me, and that I had believed it to be so. “Scandals must necessarily come.”[^p246-21] Let us pray to all the holy protectors of this mission.

This was what Fr. Bouvet wrote on November 1st, 1706.

December 4th. The Bishop of Conon declared that neither he nor his servants could any longer endure the separation of eating arrangements and kitchen in same house. Accordingly he wanted from now on to share lunch and dinner with us on a daily basis. Father Superior Gerbillon had for a long time been suggesting the same thing, and it was appropriate that this should happen for the common good to edify Christians. However, as happens in human affairs, no activity in this life is so holy that it does not leave a space for human weakness. So the more desirable seemed to be a good relationship between us and the Very Reverend Bishop, the more fraught the actual meeting, with us being mindful how charity might be harmed on one side or the other. For this reason we agreed amongst ourselves that whatever propositions the Lord Bishop should utter, however twisted or miscalculated they might be, should be quietly passed over so that there would not be an opportunity for arguments. And so it was.

### 6. The Bishop of Conon professes his goodwill towards the Society; but does not show it: on the contrary …

December 6th. On the two preceding days we had spoken in a friendly fashion after the meal. Today the Bishop, wishing to show his marked confidence in us, encouraged us to speak openly about any complaint the Jesuits had about him. He was convinced that, up to the present, he had given ample satisfaction in every respect, and that everyone now believed they were content. Then by name he mentioned Father Grelon[^p247-22] with such composure and so freely that it seemed that we could no longer keep silent without him using our silence against us as evidence of our intention of insulting him. So we simply brought up his letter to Beijing in which he had written to Father Gerbillon the Superior “that the Jesuits are deceiving the Church of God through their perjury, which, because of their evil intent, cannot be forgiven them.”[^p247-23] The Bishop shrugged off this savage slander against us, saying that by his expression he was not referring to the Jesuits in general, but to those such as those to whom St. Francis Xavier had written when he had written to the Jesuits in Goa.[^p247-24] Good. This was a sufficient explanation for the French Fathers.[^p247-25] What would the Bishop have said if the Portuguese Jesuits had complained about the same insult? He could have by a similar evasion answered that it had to be understood solely of his fellow-countrymen, whom he alone knew, and with whom the Seminary of the Ferry-boat[^p247-26] is in conflict. He would <!-- PDF page 248 | book page 238 | manuscript page 477 --> have been able to confirm this in fine fashion because he had written and published that Father Bouvet, whom he named, had by a false oath deceived the Church, the Pope, and the Most Christian King.[^p248-27] Secondly, in the man’s writing, a place was found where he said that it was easy for the Jesuits to win support for their opinions. Again, that serious authorities suspected that the Emperor by using the expression Jing Tian had wanted to deceive the Jesuits etc. The Bishop did not deal with the first point. On the second point he said that this | argument could be sustained since we had said that these words had been given in a precise sense.[^p248-28] The answer was given convincingly that those words had been intended by the Emperor in the sense which the Bishop opposed.

### 7. The Bishop defends badly his cause and his feigned goodwill

The Bishop denied that he knew in what sense the information had been given but claimed that it seemed to have been given in a precise sense. He attempted to prove this on the grounds that the Mathematical Tribunal may be called qintian jian.[^p248-29] Against this it was pointed out that the word describing the Tribunal is pronounced Qin, which is different in sound and meaning from Jing,[^p248-30] which in the tablet is associated with Tian. The latter signifies “to venerate,” but the former indicates things given by the King or things which belong to the King. It is so used in the expression qinchai[^p248-31] (“Imperial Legate” and so on). The Bishop did not agree, wishing to dispute the point, saying that even if the word may be different the meaning was the same. On this issue he was either ignorant or he thought we were ignorant and hoped to be able to impose on us.

In addition the Bishop wanted not only himself to be reconciled with the Jesuits, but also the Bishop of Rosalia[^p248-32] and the whole Seminary together with

<!-- PDF page 249 | book page 239 -->

Bishop Pallu. He spoke as follows: “When as a young man I finished my studies in Paris I had firmly decided to join the Society, but a weakness in my eyes constituted an impediment. The Bishop of Rosalia continually showed a love of the Jesuits to such an extent that I was convinced over a period of many years that he at some stage would be accepted as a member of the Society.” Thirdly, after it had been decided by the Sacred Congregation that the oath[^p249-33] should be imposed on missionaries, Bishop Pallu returned from Rome to Paris. With the agreement of the Seminary he had written to the bishops so that as a concession to missionaries, and so to Jesuits, the oath should be withdrawn. To this last point the answer was given that the following was said by those who know what happened, including ourselves: Father Pallu had drafted that oath and then at Paris, not on the advice of those at the Seminary but by the order of the Most Christian King, had begged that the said oath be withdrawn by the Sacred Congregation, but he had not achieved this end. With regard to his other points each of us were inwardly laughing since we were being treated by him like children. From the day he entered our house he had protested that he was just a child from Paris, quite incapable of any deceit or underhandedness. Let these examples, chosen from many possible ones, suffice to make the point.

On this very same day, Fr. Antoine Thomas, with the approval and at the request of the Superior, Fr. Gerbillon, handed over two letters to the Bishop of Conon. One of these was from the Legate; it had been brought by Father Mezzafalce and had been deposited in the meantime with the said Father on the instructions of the Bishop of Conon himself, who feared he might incur some harm if it should come to light. Having received them from Father Thomas, he made excuses and offered to make amends for the faults of Father Guéty, as was recorded under the month of September.[^p249-34]

December 7th. The Bishop informed us that he had today received a letter from the Bishop of Beijing, included in which was one from the Legate. All the while we were waiting to see to if he would return to yesterday’s frankness and similarly bring forward something in which he might claim to have been harmed by us so as to receive reparation. Nothing of the sort emerged in the conversation. Nor were we inclined to say more about those matters to which he was known not to be able to frame a reply and we preferred to follow the <!-- PDF page 250 | book page 240 | manuscript page 478 --> example of Our Master, Jesus, and let the issue rest rather than argue heatedly about what should be rejected.

### 8. The two literati from Fujian enter Beijing

At sunset the two scholars from Fujian, Leantius Li and Xavier Jiang, stood at the door of the College, wishing to be received as guests there. Our response was: given the way things stood, it was impossible to offer hospitality without harm both to themselves and us. | They went off to lodgings and were left in peace there on condition that they presented themselves whenever they were summoned for interrogation.

December 8th. The issue concerning the petition sent ahead by these literati from Huaian was taken up again and …

December 9th. After a consultation on the matter, Father Antoine Thomas discussed it with Zhao Laoye as follows according to a letter he wrote to Father Kilian to be preserved in the archives. This is what it says:

### 9. The Fathers consult the mandarin Zhao on the matter of these literati

December 10th. Yesterday in the evening I went to the house of Zhao Laoye. I found him at home and gave him to read the document written in Chinese by the Christian from Fujian named Jiang. When he had read it in its entirety I asked if he could give some advice in support of those two Christians. He told me that it seemed wise that they themselves plead their case in the presence of the Prince along the following lines: “We come from the remote province of Fujian, and since we have never appeared before the Prince nor the Emperor, it is impossible that we should not be frightened when questioned and should make mistakes in our answers. Accordingly we humbly beseech the Prince that he would kindly grant us the favour that we submit our replies in writing.”

Then Zhao Laoye, having handed back the Chinese document, solemnly instructed me not to divulge to anyone that I had discussed the matter with him and had shown him the document. Then he said: “Moreover, this written submission will not be enough. They will be examined about what they have thought and said in previous years. If this accords with their present statement they will be safe in the future.” It was now night and I did not know where they were lodging. They were to be examined on the following day, and I could find no way of instructing them along the lines of the advice outlined above.

After this I discussed the matter of Father Appiani who, it was said, was being brought to Court weighed down with chains. This constituted a major insult to the Divine Law and to us, with fear amongst Christians both here and in the provinces bound to follow. I asked if he could suggest some plan to avoid this significant reversal. His answer was that no way of remedying the situation <!-- PDF page 251 | book page 241 | manuscript page 479 --> occurred to him. It was not possible to further plead his cause before the Emperor. In conclusion I ask that Your Reverence consult with the fathers and if anyone has a good idea please communicate it to me.

In the residence of St. Joseph. 10 December 1706.

Antoine Thomas.

### 10. Father Appiani is taken in chains to the criminal tribunal

The presence of the literati was a source of considerable anxiety to us. They were summoned today, but not called to testify. The case of Fr. Appiani was more worrying. He had been expected for a long time and entered Beijing in an open cart just as night was coming on. He was taken directly to the Criminal Tribunal. There, as he was laden with chains, he was not placed in the prison but spent the night in a house up against the outer wall within the Palace of the Tribunal, in the custody of an officer and two substitute mandarins. We were quite ignorant of his arrival in this manner.

### 11. The Fujian literati are questioned under three or four heads

December 11th. The two scholars were summoned for the second time to the Villa where the Emperor caused the following propositions to be put to them. 1. I, the Emperor, am not holding you as prisoners. 2. You are scholars; you undertook studies so as to secure preferment. How did you come to embrace Christianity? 3. How many followers of this Law are there in the province of Fujian? 4. What is their status?

We have never discovered with certainty the replies of each,[^p251-35] except that each of them declared that they had discovered nothing wrong in this religion and so had decided to live their lives in accordance with it. From then on we became less urgent in investigating the answers given in that inquiry. The following days were very unpleasant, full of questioning and overflowing with a veritable torrent of submissions. |

### 12. How Father Appiani was arrested

Late in the evening, without us knowing anything about it, Father Appiani reached Beijing.[^p251-36] Today, as soon as we learnt of the entry and place of detention of Fr. Appiani, a servant was sent from our house to try to find out what help he expected from us. Meanwhile Father

<!-- PDF page 252 | book page 242 -->

Parrenin, returning from the Villa, was greeted by a member of the Criminal Tribunal. This individual was happy to tell a European what had been done and recounted it to the priest, who did not previously know him, how Fr. Appiani had been captured.[^p252-37] A certain yuan[^p252-38] called Weilan had been sent by the postal route as far as Yangzhou. If Father Appiani had already passed it by he was to go as far as Nanjing. If he had not yet reached Yangzhou, he should turn back and should look for him along that river.[^p252-39] It was discovered, however, that Father Appiani was near Huaian and was travelling in the same ship the Legate was on. There he was immediately clapped in irons. Father Appiani attempted to clear himself of any charge, while Weilan answered that he was not acting on his own part, but was obeying the order of the Emperor to bind him. The Legate stood there witnessing this and by means of nodding and gesturing asked that he too be put in chains. As he left the ship Father Appiani had asked if he could take one of his servants. Weilan granted this request but I do not know why this was not carried out. This was the report of the member of the Tribunal. Our servant returned with his task unfinished. Sent a second time he noticed that there was a window facing the west in the corner which was least under observation. There the servant called out to Father Appiani. He, recognizing his voice, asked him immediately where Father Mezzafalce was staying. The servant had scarcely answered that the said individual was in the house of the mandarin Charki when he was ordered off by guards. They said that Father Appiani was lacking nothing, that he had clothing and a bed, that food was being provided by the tribunal, and that it was strictly enjoined that no one should speak to him.

### 13. The Fathers ask without success that he be released from the public prison

Father Parrenin notified the Bishop of Conon of these developments and Father Kilian informed the College and the Eastern Church. When the Jesuits met together they decided that the Emperor should be approached in a more effective way so that he would allow Father Appiani to be relieved of his chains, released from the public prison, and placed in one or other of the churches. This course of action seemed not to be without danger because it could happen that His Majesty, having imposed such firm prohibitions on any further intercessions, might perhaps be stirred to more intense anger, to the very obvious danger of the whole missionary enterprise. However, this was speculation. What was clear was the shame arising from Father Appiani’s <!-- PDF page 253 | book page 243 | manuscript page 480 --> chains, which was giving rise to fear amongst the Christians, to the disgrace of holy religion, and affording unjustified satisfaction to the pagans. And so, on

December 12th. All the Jesuits went to the Villa very early in the morning. There, on their knees, they made their requests three times to the Prince. They received nothing except a cold response: Nimen bu yao guan ta, “it is none of your business.”[^p253-40] The Prince said this in a mocking tone. He rushed out the door and gave orders that the Bishop of Conon, Father Guéty, the three Christian prisoners, and the two literati from Fujian be brought there to the Villa. The Fathers were to wait there. Since the Prince had designated none of the Fathers, no individuals seemed to have been granted permission to withdraw. Only Father Kilian, under the pretext of something he had to do, hurried to the city to warn the Bishop of Conon and to be present when he was to be taken away. When he was brought the news of his forthcoming interrogation the Bishop of Conon trembled all over. We asked, since it was the appropriate time, that he should hurry to have his lunch with us, before he was called. He saw us lunching in low spirits. He himself was scarcely able to take his food, with his hands flapping aimlessly. From time to time he blurted out unclear questions whether from lack of confidence or seeking advice. | In the conversation after the meal, he showed himself more at ease.

### 14. The Bishop of Conon is taken to the Prince to be interrogated

After about an hour and a half a mandarin named Yang, the substitute of Henkama, arrived expressing the wish that the Bishop of Conon would go with him to the Emperor’s son. Father Kilian was successful in his request to the escort that the Bishop be conveyed in a sedan chair. The Bishop himself was not reluctant to go on horseback but it seemed more appropriate for a variety of reasons that he be conveyed unseen by chair. Thus the Bishop was carried in quite a courteous fashion, with no appearance of being a prisoner, but we were not permitted to accompany him.

Meanwhile the Prince had gone off into the city. While the priests waited in the Villa, Father Parrenin learnt from the mandarin Zhao that yesterday the Prince, when he went with him to announce to the Emperor the arrival of Father Appiani, had sought at the same time that the Bishop of Conon should at last be clapped in irons and examined under torture concerning his violation of the imperial yubi. The Emperor however had replied in the following terms: “If we act in this way with this newcomer, his crime must be brought forward and a legal process set in train, which, in accordance with our laws, will end with <!-- PDF page 254 | book page 244 --> his execution.” “When these matters were raised,” said the mandarin, “I put it to the Prince as we were departing that the Emperor’s mind on the matter was quite clear—he did not want the blood of this man to be shed in China.”

### 15. The scholars from Fujian are praised. The Prince pours scorn on the Christian religion

The same mandarin praised both scholars. Jiang because he had talents worthy of a man, Li because he had shown himself to be a fervent Christian by presenting excellent arguments while the Prince railed in vain against and showed contempt for our Holy Law.

### 16. The examination of the Bishop of Conon begins

Returning to the Villa late in the evening the Prince summoned the Bishop of Conon. He was then examined on a few points with none of us in attendance.[^p254-41] At the beginning of the examination the question of his decree was raised. His Majesty says: “Over the two centuries since the arrival of Father Ricci the fortunes of your religion have advanced without rancour or disturbance. But now with the arrival of Duoluo (the Lord Patriarch) everything is in confusion because he is rising up against the laws of the empire. You are responsible for such a change. What answer do you give? A man of this kind ought to remove himself to some concealed cave in a far-distant mountain.”

The Bishop answered: “I have found much good in Confucius and other Chinese books; I have also found some things which, in my judgment, do not agree with the Christian Law. Accordingly I have written to the Pope to seek his opinion.” Mandarin Zhao asked: “What did the Pope reply?” The Bishop: “The decision of the Pope has not yet arrived.” Zhao pressed on: “In that case why have you, without waiting for the Pope’s decision, proceeded as though it had been decided to condemn our customs as bad and to order that they should be considered bad by others?” The answer came: “I thought I had such power.” “But,” retorted Zhao, “you could not have such power. Who, in a matter concerning royal authority, after the case has been referred to the Emperor, is able to act in such a case without waiting for His Majesty’s response?” The Bishop began to <!-- PDF page 255 | book page 245 | manuscript page 481 --> defend himself against such charges on the grounds that he did not adequately understand those speaking and, perhaps, he was not adequately understood by his hearers. Accordingly he asked that one of our Fathers be given him to act as interpreter.

December 13th. Once again the fathers gathered at the Villa to intercede, but utterly in vain. Early, at the usual hour, yesterday’s proceedings were conveyed to the Emperor as well as the statements of the Bishop of Conon, and His Majesty assigned to him as interpreters Fathers José Soares and Dominique Parrenin. While the others returned to the city those two priests were compelled to be present at the inquiry in the Prince’s palace. First of all they informed the Bishop about the reiterated petition of the Prince that he should be permitted to examine the Bishop under torture about the violation of the royal Jing Tian yubi[^p255-42] and about | the answer given to the Emperor that it was an offence involving the death penalty if it were to be proven true: the laws of China would offer no clemency. In the second place, they excused themselves for both sitting on the floor while the Bishop himself would be compelled to answer on his knees, since this was the express order of the Prince.

### 17. He is accused of causing disturbances in the province of Fujian

The mandarins who were going to conduct the questioning repeated the Emperor’s order of the previous day. Then the question was put more precisely: why was the Reverend Bishop himself disturbing the peace of China and the missionaries? He replied that he had not disturbed the peace nor the preaching of the Gospel. If he had given an instruction of this nature, the instruction should not be condemned because his instruction had no effect. He declared that the testimony of Peter Wang[^p255-43] against him, that he had caused disturbances in Fujian, was false. Peter Wang was summoned and again testified about the aforementioned disturbances. When it was put to him that the instruction of the Bishop of Conon had no effect in regard to tearing down the inscriptions (the Bishop had been ordered to withdraw from the proceedings), the wretched man not only did not cease his charges but rather launched into a more serious accusation, declaring that one had been removed in Nan’an.[^p255-44] Father Soares tried to silence the erratic Peter Wang by a glance of his eyes but he became even more vehement. “Look at me,” he said, “bound with nine chains, a few feet away from <!-- PDF page 256 | book page 246 --> death and will I still be prevented from revealing the truth? What I said was true. I saw the complaints of fifty Christians written about this case to the Bishop of Macau who informed the Bishop of Conon by letter.”

### 18. The Bishop is assailed by the testimony of many

As he gave this evidence he followed it up by reciting from memory the complaints of Christians, adding and explaining to what degree and how the Bishop of Conon had acted against the Jesuit missionaries, giving their names insofar as he was able. Peter Wang was discharged and the scholars from Fujian were brought in. It was relayed to them that the Bishop of Conon denied that disturbances had been caused by him. Both of them again gave positive testimony about causing disturbances. One of them named Jiang found a way of excusing the Bishop of Conon, namely, that he was a foreigner from Europe, even though in Chinese jurisdiction, and in this particular matter he was following his own practice. This European, he declared, was not so bad, but there was another far worse than him, the chief foe of our Rites, who wished to abolish them by one stroke. He has on different occasions spoken against me as I was defending our Rites so fiercely that he seemed about to pronounce judgment on me. This Yan Dang who is before you attempted to placate him but he grew even more importunate in attacking the Rites and said: “If you do not allow me to act, you are not the zhujiao[^p256-45] of this province of Fujian.” That person is called Liang Hongren (Bishop de Lionne)[^p256-46] and he has already departed for Europe. Moreover those things which Yan Dang says and does are not his own idea. He has not learnt to read our books adequately but has taken his ideas from another European, called Wan.[^p256-47] He was extremely capable in disputation. He wrote against our rites and, dying, left his manuscripts to Yan Dang. From them this man acquired weapons. He had the texts of our Classics, previously collected by Wan, transcribed by his xianggongs, whenever he needed them. He himself understood neither the writing nor the meaning. Then he challenged the Bishop of Conon who was standing by to read one page of each book if he could. The inquisitors asked, where was the European named Wan? He answered that he had already said he had died. He was, in fact, Father Francisco Varo, of the Order of St. Dominic.

After this Father Guéty was again questioned about the same disturbances which had arisen in Fujian. He hesitated at first, indicating that he did not clearly remember them, because he had not been there. Then | he admitted that he had heard about them from Christians and told them what he said he had heard.[^p257-48] Then the Bishop of Conon was recalled and he was asked what he had to say about the testimonies of Peter Wang, Jiang, and Li, his teachers, and of his companion, Father Guéty. He answered with a Chinese expression that it was true that ge liao he yihui le,[^p257-49] that he had started a fire for one moment but that he had immediately sorted things out with the Christians and the missionaries. This confession of his was immediately dispatched to the Prince who smiled at the witty excuse about a fire started for one moment of time. Those presiding at the inquiry pressed on to discover finally what was the real cause of the disturbances.

### 19. The reason for the disturbances incited by the Bishop of Conon

Peter Wang, Xavier Jiang, and Leantius Li testified that the reason was that the Bishop of Conon was unwilling to allow that the word Tian signified and signifies in China the real God[^p257-50] and that he prohibited honour being paid to Confucius and dead ancestors. The Bishop replied that he had not prohibited bai zongci[^p257-51] or bowing in honour of Confucius, nor had he banned the entire cult of ancestors, but that he said he wanted to ban the offering which is called ji.[^p257-52] The mandarins chuckled at this, asking what, to your way of thinking, is the difference between bai zongci and ji zongci? Or between greeting Confucius by bowing the body, bending the knee and lowering the head to the ground in his honour, and the offering of fruit, food, etc. to the same Confucius? Surely both rituals are of the same type, with the same object, the same purpose? Or are we doing something different in pressing our forehead to the ground in the presence of our Master’s tablet, and something yet again by holding up wine in the same presence? On that point he said and wanted it said to the mandarins that even the Jesuits condemn as wrong the ritual called ji. The Fathers did not <!-- PDF page 258 | book page 248 --> want to interpret this since it was false. They showed the Bishop books printed by the Jesuits which said that this ceremony was not permitted by the Jesuits because they said that it was bad, but because it was not obligatory and it was appropriate that Christians withdraw from this practice.[^p258-53]

The mandarins no longer wanted to listen to the person being examined with the interpreters arguing amongst themselves in an unknown language. Moving back to their own concerns, they forcefully inquired as to why the Bishop of Conon, without waiting for the answer of the Supreme Pontiff, had himself made a judgement in China. They asked in addition would the Pope when sending out men to preach the religion prescribe the practice to be observed in preaching that they should not attack the rites of foreign peoples before they had warned the Pope himself about them?

### 20. Very wise instructions given to the missionaries by the Sacred Congregation

Of course we presume this to be the case, they said, when we are referring to that wise ruler who was chosen by so many wise men as the head of the wise. The Bishop denied that such an instruction had been given to him or his people by the Holy Pontiff. Father Parrenin reminded him of the instructions put out by the Sacred Congregation and printed at Paris.[^p258-54] The Bishop replied that those printed had not been prescribed by the Sacred Congregation, but had been produced by the Secretary of the Congregation alone, and because of them there had been numerous quarrels at Rome. He conceded it was true that they had been printed by the Directors themselves at the Paris seminary as though they had been issued by the Congregation itself. In a similar fashion the Bishop of Rosalia had spoken about the same point to Father

<!-- PDF page 259 | book page 249 | manuscript page 483 -->

Superior J.F. Gerbillon when he was accompanying the Emperor on a tour of the southern provinces. The same bishop wished him to acknowledge the truth of the following proposition: that it was entirely his fault and that of his fellows that they brought such instructions into the light of day. Father Parrenin said to the Bishop of Conon that in the present time and place the said instructions should not be revoked in such a controversial fashion | by the Sacred Congregation and the Pope, as they are full of wisdom and worthy of such authors even in the estimation of the pagans. The mandarins, not tolerating European languages to be inserted into the interrogation, pressed on more firmly with the issue of what instruction the Pope had given.

### 21. The Bishop of Conon answers that only one instruction was given

The Most Reverend Lord declared that this was the only instruction they received: “If the customs of the peoples do not accord with Christian law, change them all; if you change them badly, I will punish you.” It is not the right time to comment on this. However, if an instruction of this nature had really been given to those bishops on some occasion by the Supreme Pontiff, it cannot be denied how carefully they have taken pains that it not be known in Europe and in China. Moreover, it cannot be explained why now, in a time and place so full of danger, the Bishop of Conon should have produced it to be kept forever in the court records in Beijing.

The mandarins went on to ask: does the Bishop of Conon consider himself a person of greater or lesser status than the Lord Patriarch. He answered that he was of lesser status. They took this up and went on: “But the Patriarch said in the presence of the Emperor that he did not dare to decide anything before receiving the Pope’s determination—and yet you dared to do so?” The Bishop made a distinction, by alleging a difference in that the Legate had not examined the rites. “And had you examined them!” retorted Zhao. “You were not even capable of examining them. You did not know our writing, our books, our language or our customs. This is corroborated by your testimony and by our manifold and definite experience.” The Bishop responded: “I had thought I had sufficient understanding of all these matters.” “We acknowledge” said Zhao, “your former pretension to real knowledge; but know you see the state of affairs is otherwise, with the Emperor himself very kindly teaching you the true meaning of our customs. It is, therefore, right that you acknowledge that you have been too hasty.”

### 22. He is forced into a very tight spot

The Bishop replied: “I have done nothing wrong because I examined the matter before I made a pronouncement. At that time I did not know about the documents of the Emperor which they brought forth subsequently.” “Let us leave aside years gone by,” said the mandarin. “This very day, after so many repeated and irrefutable assertions, explanations, and exhortations on the part of His Majesty, why do you put off confessing your error?” The Bishop replied: “I admit that I have breached the laws of China and am liable to punishment to be inflicted by the Emperor, just as the kings of Europe are able to punish those who transgress their civil laws. But I have not sinned in the eyes of God, although I confess that I deserve punishment.”

Henkama asked, seemingly outside the framework of the inquiry: “In your law, if someone fails to employ that prudence which he is able and duty-bound to employ when dealing with some serious matter, would he be considered to have sinned?” The Bishop replied: “He would be considered to have sinned.” Henkama went on: “But you have not employed due and all possible care in this matter on which depends the survival or ruin of true religion in this empire. Therefore etc.” The Bishop answered: “In this matter I have employed all possible care.” The mandarin pressed his point: “You were able to ask and discover that the Emperor was of a different mind to you concerning the word Tian and so on. You neglected the task of inquiring and discovering the facts, and do you still claim to have exhausted all possible avenues? Why do you not acknowledge that you were deceived, that you behaved badly and failed in your duty?”

### 23. The Bishop pronounces that he has done nothing wrong etc.

He answered: “I have done nothing wrong; I have in no way failed.” “Come now,” said the mandarin, “look at this tuft of silk cloth. Let us suppose that you were looking out of the corner of your eye and, mistaking the colour, you said it was red, through an error of eyesight. Now on closer inspection you realize it is yellow. In this case would not you acknowledge you had made a mistake?” The Bishop replied: “I would not have spoken badly because when I had said it was red, at the time I would have judged it to be red.” The mandarin, seeing that he was not able to give an answer, but wishing to play with words, put forward another case. “When our Great | Lord set up the Jing Tian inscription, do you think that the said words had been examined by those who hung them up in their church?” The reply came: “I believe these words were examined.” The mandarin pressed on: “If they examined them and used them, then they must have understood their meaning, namely that Tian has exactly the same meaning as Tianzhu. Why then do you accuse those distinguished men of error?” The reply came: “Everyone has his own interpretation. I have not determined the matter <!-- PDF page 261 | book page 251 --> definitively; this task belongs to the Supreme Pontiff. He can revoke, correct, or annul my solution and punish me if I have made an erroneous conclusion.” The mandarins wished to reach some resolution of the matter and so they pressed the Bishop. “What will you do now? It is more than obvious that you are in the wrong. The matter rests in your hands; you see to what extent you are mistaken in your view, nevertheless you persist in preferring your interpretation, conceived in error, to the interpretation of His Majesty. This is tantamount to willingly and deliberately wishing to destroy your religion in China. If all missionaries were like you, which of them would not be thrown out of this empire?” The Bishop replied: “I think nothing of the kind. I am not destroying religion. The Emperor may do what he wants. I am in his hands.”

### 24. He cannot be brought to confess his error

The mandarins took great exception to this final expression and attacked it in many ways. Finally, perceiving that the Bishop of Conon could not be brought through his own initiative to adopt a well-founded attitude of submission, they said in very bitter tones: “You know you have done wrong, but you are not able to overcome your spirit of obstinacy so as to arrive at the point where you admit your error; may what you have constructed so badly end in ruin.”

These were the main points of the interrogation. Many other questions were asked and answers given. Since they did not touch on the truthfulness of those speaking, nor add weight to the circumstances, I considered it preferable they be left out rather than included and so bring shame to others.

When the examination was over the Most Reverend Lord, together with the priests, was permitted to leave the Prince’s Palace and stay in the same house with them in the Changchun yuan.[^p261-55] Given this opportunity both priests employed a large number of arguments to convince the Bishop of Conon to deign to take into account the good of the entire mission by acknowledging his error. He however had once and for all reached the following determination: if, in fact, Rome makes a decision, he will be able to arrange for another one to be <!-- PDF page 262 | book page 252 | manuscript page 485 --> sought on the grounds that the previous decision was based on a false exposition of the facts of the case. “In fact,” he said, “it will be a consolation for me if Rome has based its decision not on the strength of my exposition, but on the basis of what has been written by Jesuits.” This was the stance of the Bishop of Conon.

December 14th. Today it seemed that the inquiry would continue. So Fr. Parrenin, now aware of the fixed state of the mind of the Bishop of Conon, who seemed to wish everything to collapse in ruins with him rather than confess his error, began to beseech His Reverence to remain silent rather than provoke by new answers further questions which would be frustrating for him and dangerous for the mission. Fr. Soares added that it was unclear why His Reverence was prevented from admitting some error of judgement about terms and Chinese Rites before the Emperor had declared to him on his own authority the meaning of those terms.

### 25. The ancient Chinese recognized the true God under the name of

The Bishop replied: If the priest had some plan whereby he could be released from the present irksome non- Tian sense, he should suggest it. However, he would cease at this time and place to put forward arguments. He said he would submit himself to the Emperor’s judgment if he were to determine today that henceforth Tian had the same meaning as Tianzhu; but he would never declare that the ancient Chinese acknowledged the true God under the name Tian. Father Parrenin replied: how much harm did Your Reverence fear would ensue if the Chinese believed their ancestors acknowledged and worshipped the true God under the name of Tian? Clearly it was beyond understanding why you reverend gentlemen wished to appear | scrupulous and were prepared to pronounce in favour of atheists, namely, that the Chinese had never acknowledged the true God, rather than agree, in a spirit of service to the Gospel, that the Chinese acknowledged and worshipped the true God under the name of Tian, since they are unable to point out any other word in the whole Chinese language which the Chinese seemed to have used for “God.”

About midday, Fr. Appiani was brought from the Criminal Tribunal to the Villa. He was conveyed in an ordinary carriage, as we learned on the following day, with a mounted mandarin riding before him. Several unarmed soldiers were sitting with him. He spent the night there, but we were unable to discover where he was lodged.

December 15th. All the Fathers whose health permitted it went to the Villa with the aim of interceding if they were able. The eunuch Zhang Qilin asked Fathers Grimaldi, Soares, and Kilian what finally would happen with the Bishop of Conon. He made much of his stubbornness. He pointed out how <!-- PDF page 263 | book page 253 --> kindly the Emperor had tolerated his mistakes and how gently he had guided him along by communicating his pronouncements personally, by revealing the meaning of texts, and by determining with his imperial authority the true purpose of Chinese customs. Then he exhorted us to intercede for him no further, saying that the request could not possibly be heard by the Emperor, who was convinced that this man had acquired a reputation in Europe by his writings, a reputation which he was not only unwilling to diminish by his own admission of guilt, considering an insult to the Emperor to be of little importance, but was solely concerned not to lose the esteem which he believed he had amongst Europeans.

This morning Fr. Parrenin learnt from Henkama about yesterday’s examination of Father Appiani. He said firstly that he had in no way been badly treated. Secondly, he said that he had been asked about several matters of lesser importance but, especially, why he had been thrown out of the province of Sichuan, and why he had said to the catechist, Jin Xiu, that it was the Emperor’s command that he write down what he had copied from the writings of the Bishop of Conon. Did the Emperor give such an order? Appiani denied the charge that he had been expelled from the said province. He also denied that he said it had been ordered that this catechist write. However, since it was the Emperor’s command that the Bishop of Conon should commit this to writing and he himself was unable to write them down, and since there was no one else to whom he could entrust the transcription, he thought he was complying with the Emperor’s wish if he caused this man to do the writing. So, to allay the catechist’s fears, he said it had been ordered that he write down what the Bishop of Conon had found in the teachings of Confucius that did not accord with Christian doctrine etc. These items tally with the report Appiani himself wrote to us on the 18th about his examination, as is mentioned in that day’s entry.[^p263-56]

### 26. The end of the interrogations

Today the Emperor determined that the interrogations of Father Appiani and the Bishop of Conon were concluded. The latter accompanied by us returned to the house on horseback; the former was brought back to the Tribunal in a cart escorted by unarmed soldiers with our servant following him and offering some refreshments. In our house the Bishop of Conon, in the presence of the superior J.F. Gerbillon, renewed the request which he had made on the 14th to Fr. Antoine Thomas that a letter be <!-- PDF page 264 | book page 254 | manuscript page 486 --> sent to Fr. Le Breton to burn on his instructions all papal briefs and similar writings, in case the Emperor should order that things of this kind should be sought in accordance with the written testimony given in the trial of Fr. Guéty, and thereby gain a pretext to persecute missionaries. If this be the case with papal briefs, what about other manuscripts and printed works? There were indeed many reasons for fear.

December 16th. There seemed to be no activity about this business but, in fact, the imperial judgement was being drafted. At our house, the Bishop said to Fr. Gerbillon, the superior, that he was very happy with the services of Fr. Parrenin, which he had offered in days previous as an interpreter. He also said that he willingly consented to being sent by the Emperor to whatever province he chose with permission to spend the rest of his days in some Franciscan church. | At the same time he told him to be wary of Peter Wang because, seven or eight days before he was thrown into chains, he had said to the catechist Jin Xiu that if any harm came to the fathers, he would become another Yang Guangxian against us.[^p264-57]

### 27. Certain details about Fr. Appiani

Sixthly, that he had given his indoor clothes to the servant to be washed.

December 18th. The servant brought the items requested to Fr. Appiani. He further asked that shoes be obtained for him and began to complain as to why the Fathers were not speaking on his behalf with the Emperor, or was it really possible that we would allow him to rot in gaol? This kindness of ours deserves to be rewarded by you.” It was appropriate to reply to this request neither positively nor negatively, since the affair was not yet concluded.

### 28. The judgment of the Emperor and of the judges assigned to the inquiry

Most of the fathers waited in the palace in a state of great trepidation in order to hear something definite about the judgement which was said to have been brought down. They were informed covertly that it contained five points. 1. Yan Dang, He Nadu, and Fang Zhou,[^p266-60] men guilty of creating disturbances, are to be taken under military guard to Guangzhou and handed over to the Viceroy and zongdu to be thrown out of the country. 2. Peter Wang, Thomas Gu, and John Jin Xiu are to be given forty strokes as disturbers of the peace, and to be exiled under the supervision of the Military Prefect in the province of Liaodong. 3. The two scholars from Fujian are declared innocent and are to be sent home. 4. Bi Tianxiang (Fr. Appiani) has caused a disturbance in Sichuan. ### 29. The Bishop of Conon hears the judgement with disdain

In this crisis in affairs, looking around for help and advice in this shipwreck, as it were, of the mission, we begged even the Bishop of Conon himself for help in the shared enterprise. The superior Fr. J.F. Gerbillon outlined to him what he knew, asking if some solution occurred to him. The Bishop, listening to everything with more disdain than usual, told us to find the remedy. He condemned as less than just the judgment against Fr. Mezzafalce and the catechist. With respect to himself he asked whether something could be said to those coming from the Tribunal whose task was to take him to Guangzhou. The Father replied that people of this kind are only sent to execute the sentence and all entreaties put before them would be pointless since they were in no way permitted to bring the requests or arguments of those on whom sentence has been passed to the Tribunal or the Emperor. Then and only then His Reverence showed some semblance of distress.

Henceforth no idea occurred to us, anxious as we were either to deflect or diminish so many disasters, but to send Fathers Gerbillon and Soares as darkness came to meet with the mandarin Zhao, with the purpose of asking what hope or recourse was left.

### 30. The Emperor’s son exerts himself to eradicate the Christian religion

[The mandarin] said that he had thought for a whole day about a solution, but he had completely failed to find one. No compromise could be effective on account of the Prince, who had taken upon himself the task of destroying our holy religion in China and had declared himself an enemy of Tianzhu. “To this end,” he added, “he has made use of many irregular manoeuvres which are now beginning to bring about a result, to the dismay of us at Court. ### 31. The Fathers’ lack of concern, or rather their charity and preparedness to forgive

“In fact, in the present circumstances you could not complain about the Emperor, since, as he very rightly says, from the time of Ricci everything had been quiet, but now peace has been disturbed throughout the provinces by a number of individuals. You never warned His Majesty of this, nor were you intending to warn him, even though you knew of it. When did you ever warn about the Bishop of Conon, Guéty, or Appiani? You remained silent until, having become more confident, they willingly placed themselves in the Emperor’s hands and presented themselves of their own accord to be scrutinized. On this point the Emperor said: “It is worthy of belief that many similar people are lurking in the provinces.” When you keep quiet about this activity, it is absolutely necessary to take into account the state and public order.”

At this point the mandarin gave as his advice that we should on the following day ask that the judgement be disclosed to us by the Prince. In the event that he refuses, we should seek it from the eunuch Zhang Qilin. And if he too refuses, it is almost certain that the eunuch would refer our petition to the Emperor. In this way they could make a start to presenting our case, but not guarantee it would work or secure a favourable conclusion.

Accordingly, the superior Fr. J.F. Gerbillon raised the possibility whether, if the precise wording of the judgement proved unobtainable, it might be appropriate to fight for the common good by pointing out that innocent people were caught up in the judgement. And since none of the missionaries had thus far been accorded permits, all could be driven out by local officials without any recourse. It seemed therefore appropriate that His Majesty should be petitioned <!-- PDF page 268 | book page 258 | manuscript page 488 --> to at least suspend the publication of the judgement by granting a moratorium until the missionaries could request and obtain permits. This was the only course of action that the mandarin Zhao approved of attempting on the following day.

December 19th. Very early in the morning we approached the palace and petitioned the Prince on our knees | that he would provide us with a copy of the judgement handed down. This he immediately granted, but with a surly expression and amidst threats that we should keep in mind how much we owed to his father for such excessive kindness thus far lavished on us. The copy was in Manchu. Its points agreed with those which had been secretly mentioned by Zhao on the previous day. There was only this one additional clause: “that those three priests should be expelled to Macau.”[^p268-62]

Nevertheless, we are obliged by the present parlous state of affairs to repeat our requests, especially since it is stated in the judgment that all Europeans who have not been afforded permits should be expelled. Thus at one blow all of them will be attacked by the local governors, since none of them have permits.” The Prince prevented us from saying more by declaring that all should come to Beijing to request letters of permission.

### 32. The Fathers request that the promulgation of the judgement be delayed until the missionaries can acquire permits

But we insisted: it was necessary to deal with the matter immediately and we were beseeching the Emperor’s clemency through which missionaries were, up to the present, attracted to come to China, leaving behind their native land. We requested that the judgment be suspended throughout the provinces until the missionaries were able to ask for permits. The Prince, having heard our request, brought it to his father and then said on his return: “This is what I said to my father, the Emperor: ‘The Europeans say that the Emperor’s favours have been so numerous and so great that they are not equal to the task of offering thanks, and even less do they dare to make further requests. But it occurs to them regarding the judgment that has been brought down that missionaries who through no fault of their own lack permits, simply because of that lack of permits, may be immediately expelled from the empire if the imperial judgment is conveyed to the <!-- PDF page 269 | book page 259 | manuscript page 489 --> local officials.’ My father, the Emperor, replies to you as follows: he is suspending publication of the judgment in the other provinces. He is giving orders that those officials from the Tribunals who were to accompany the Bishop of Conon and Fr. Appiani to the provinces of Guangdong and Sichuan should publish the judgement only there. After a period of time when we believed that the Prince was in a calmer frame of mind we asked with deference that a copy of the judgment in Chinese be given to us. This was because we needed to send it to missionaries throughout the provinces, and, in particular, to His Excellency, the Legate. However, we could not mention his name in the Prince’s presence for fear of provoking his anger.

Then, when the Prince had withdrawn, we prostrated ourselves and earnestly entreated the eunuch, Zhang Qilin, repeating the same requests for a second time after lunch, namely, that His Majesty would deign to give orders that chains be removed from Fr. Appiani. The eunuch wished to comfort those who were suffering and promised that our humble petition would assuredly be presented by him to the Emperor. On the following day, speaking to us through a latticework screen he said that each individual should pay the penalty for his fault, and that the punishment of Fr. Appiani had nothing to do with us. Then he suddenly disappeared from view.

We requested that since he was not going to begin his journey today, | we should be allowed to keep him with us during the night. ### 33. As he leaves, the Bishop of Conon makes some requests of the Fathers

Then we went to the Military Tribunal, a distance of half a league. There we found the Bishop of Conon and Fathers Mezzafalce and Guéty cheerful, at least on the surface. The only small irritation was Macau, if he were to be confined there. He declared that the Prince was frightening <!-- PDF page 272 | book page 262 | manuscript page 491 --> and had unhinged his mind. However, this gentleman laughed freely as though nothing bad had happened through his fault. His face showed no trace of shame.

He was not absent through his own fault. ### 36. How Father Appiani was captured

Before I completely leave these gentlemen as they make their departure, I shall briefly relate how this day Father Appiani explained to Father Parrenin the events that led to his being cast into irons. He said: “I was with the Abbate di San Giorgio on His Excellency the Legate’s boat. We had just taken a delightful breakfast when, lo and behold, a local mandarin appeared. He began his conversation by asking for snuff. He then asked me my Chinese personal name and family name. He soon indicated that two mandarins had come from the royal palace wishing to speak briefly with me. I immediately advised the Legate that some command of the Court was doubtless to hand. Scarcely a moment had passed when the two mandarins entered, declaring that the Emperor did not want me to follow the Legate any further on his journey. They then placed a chain around my neck. When the Lord Patriarch saw this, he rose and attempting to grasp the chain in his hands said that the chain should be placed on him. But the mandarins caused me to be brought outside and taken into a nearby shrine. From this place we set out on a journey. What I endured on the first day gave me much scope for exercising patience. From then onwards, they treated me well, with courtesy and great fairness. So, Father, I ask that in recompense, at my expense, you buy and offer to the said mandarin a garment made of European cloth, worth ten ounces of silver (the equivalent of forty French livres).”

December 22nd. The Prince, in the Emperor’s name, said to the priests in the palace that when Pursama arrives with Sabino, only Pursama should report on his assignment. (In two days’ time the Emperor was going away on a hunting trip.) Fr. Sabino will be placed in the College.

### 37. The form of the patent letters or the wording of the permit to be obtained by the missionaries

On the previously mentioned days the Prince was shaping the wording of the permit in his distinctive way, which was aggressive and hostile to Christianity. When the Emperor saw it[^p274-66] he ordered it to be changed because, although the Prince had given the task of drafting it to mandarins who were friendly towards our Holy Law, and during the following night they had removed some words and had replaced them with others, they had not dared to openly oppose the Prince’s intentions. ### 38. A letter of Father Superior to the Lord Patriarch outlining what happened in the month of December

Most Excellent Lord, The Peace of Christ. It is with unutterable displeasure that I consider myself obliged to inform Your Excellency of the very sad conclusion of the matters involving the Bishop of Conon and Fathers Mezzafalce, Guéty, and Appiani. When the examination of each individual had been concluded, the Emperor on the 17th of the present month <!-- PDF page 275 | book page 265 --> passed sentence, a copy of which I send with this letter, faithfully translated from the original Manchu which was corrected in the Emperor’s own hand, a copy of which I have seen.

On the tenth of the current month Father Appiani arrived in Beijing. He was immediately brought to the Criminal Tribunal. He was not kept in gaol, but in a certain open building guarded by ten soldiers. I had at the time just sent off my letter by the courier, the last letter which I had the honour of addressing to Your Excellency, when I was informed of his arrival. As soon as possible I sent the most reliable of our servants to try to get information. Was it permissible to speak to Fr. Appiani? Did he need anything and how was it possible to help him? Our servant was denied any permission to speak to him. However, one of the soldiers informed him that I had inquired if there was anything that he needed.

On the following day, when the servant went back there, after providing money to the guards, he succeeded in exchanging a few words with Fr. Appiani. I had instructed the servant about the nature of the case and what accusations had led to his imprisonment. The servant also distributed money to those he had to. On the same day we all went to the Changchun yuan with the intention of requesting with all the insistence we could that His Majesty should deign to order the chains to be removed from this missionary, and that he should be given the opportunity to be housed in one of our churches. To this end we offered pledges. On our knees we begged the Prince to obtain this favour for us from his father. But he replied only in tones of bitter mockery. It is true that, despite this, others did not neglect to inform the Emperor that we had come and what we were asking for. Nevertheless, there was no reply.

We learnt only one thing: that His Majesty had commanded that the Bishop of Conon be questioned on that day by confronting him with Fr. Guéty and the Chinese men who had given witness against him. The Prince asked the Emperor that the Bishop of Conon and Father Guéty should both be chained and tortured if they did not confess to the charges of which they were accused.

### 39. The restraint of the Emperor

The Emperor did not wish this. He said: “If it should come to that and we press those two Europeans more vigorously on the charges brought against them, namely, the desecration of the royal Jing Tian inscription and also their prohibition of the cult of Confucius and ancestors, since these are capital offences according to the laws of China, it will be necessary to behead them. This is a course of action I will not take.” The Emperor’s pronouncement was the reason why the Bishop of Conon was less rigorously examined. He was even brought to the palace in a sedan chair and that day questioned quite leniently, because by a happy chance the Prince was <!-- PDF page 276 | book page 266 | manuscript page 493 --> focused on other matters. Accordingly he left him to Zhao and Wang Laoye (or Henkama). They were obliged by the Emperor’s command to question him. The Bishop of Conon could not adequately grasp what they were saying, nor could he himself speak intelligibly. He accordingly craved the Emperor’s indulgence that an interpreter from amongst one of fathers who lived in Beijing be granted him. On the following day we returned to the Imperial Villa to continue our requests to the Emperor. We received no answer except that the affair would be shortly concluded. Fathers Soares and Parrenin were appointed interpreters for the Bishop of Conon and were ordered to stay in the Villa because they were to assist the inquiry. When Fr. Parrenin was appointed principal interpreter, he did what he could, as did Fr. Soares, to help the Bishop of Conon. And we used all our energy to sway the minds of the judges in his favour. Early on the following morning the replies of the Bishop of Conon were presented to the Emperor together with the questions put to him. | I have not yet received them in written form, and so I shall not write them down now for Your Excellency but outline the gist of them.

### 40. What was the principal charge against the Bishop of Conon

They particularly pressed the charge that since he was lacking in knowledge of the Chinese characters and books, as had been shown by the two scholars from Fujian who maintained to his face that he neither understood the books nor could read any unless he had the pronunciation of the characters in European letters indicated in the margin. He was further charged that while they had daily interpreted the true meaning of the canonical books for him, it was not that which he had adopted, and that everything which he was accustomed to assert against Chinese teaching and customs was in no way of his own devising, given the fact that he understood neither. He had, in fact, taken all this from the writings he had inherited from his first teacher, the Dominican Fr. Varo. In addition they challenged him to read in their presence any Chinese book if he could. When the Bishop of Conon could not reply to these charges, he was asked if he felt no scruple over the fact that he had provided reasons for the expulsion forthwith of all Europeans from China and the extinction of the Christian religion in this empire. He was doing this by stubbornly defending his faulty opinion against the interpretation of the Emperor, who is amongst the wisest in the empire. He should take care that he does not deceive the Pope by writing against the Chinese rites, which he had not understood, and presenting himself to the Pope as though he understood them. In addition, it was proved against him that in the province he had proceeded to actually prohibit the rites without waiting for the Pope’s answer.

### 41. His reply

The Bishop of Conon replied that he had exercised care in judging things that he judged necessary. He said that he experienced great difficulties in dealing with Chinese beliefs and customs, yet he had found many features in them that were good and quite in conformity with the Holy Christian Religion. He admitted he was the cause of some disturbance amongst the Christians of Fuzhou fu, but this had only lasted for a brief period; that he abandoned what he had started, and the royal inscription had not been removed. The unfortunate Peter Wang was present. To support the testimony he had given he declared that although the royal inscription in Fuzhou had not been removed, it had been removed in Nan’an.[^p277-67] Father Soares tried to prevent the man from speaking about this by raising his eyebrow, but he went on: “Here I stand, bound in chains, a fraction away from death, and am I to be prevented from telling the truth?” By a happy turn of events the Prince was absent from the interrogation and Zhao and Wang (or Henkama) pretended not to hear and did not have recorded in writing this testimony of Peter Wang, so as not to be obliged to prolong the inquiry and to summon to Beijing those who had removed the royal inscription.

When the replies of the Bishop of Conon had been presented to His Majesty, Father Appiani was ordered to be examined in confrontation with Jin Xiu the xianggong (catechist). Father Appiani answered respectfully and well. He said it was true he had told this xianggong that it was the Emperor’s order that what the Bishop of Conon had found in the teaching of Confucius which did not agree with the Christian religion should be written down. But it was not true that he had said it was commanded that Jin Xiu should do the writing. It was false that he compelled him to write by means of threats and insulting words. He said he had simply said it was appropriate he should write because the Bishop of Conon did not know how to write it himself. The xianggong did not dare to assert the opposite to his face, although he had written this down previously in his deposition.

### 42. What was sought from Father Appiani

Fr. Appiani was further asked: was it true that he had stirred up crowds in Sichuan? He answered it was not true, and this could be ascertained from local mandarins. Thus he provided the occasion for being sent back to Sichuan for further questioning. The questions and his answers were shown to the Emperor on the 15th of this month. The Emperor answered that he would enter the city on the following day | and that <!-- PDF page 278 | book page 268 --> each of them should be remanded to there and a judgment should be made there about them. We were informed of these details in the Villa and the Bishop of Conon himself returned with us to our house.

On the 16th the Emperor returned to Beijing and on the 17th the matter was referred to His Majesty, who gave judgement, and I am sending an account of this which is as accurate as I can render it. The sentence was communicated to us on the 18th. In vain we made every effort to secure a change or to alter the Emperor’s mind but he took no notice of our requests. The only thing we were able to achieve was the postponement of the publication of the sentence throughout the provinces in all the tribunals. Thus the missionaries would be given time, as long as they wished to remain in China, to come to Beijing, present themselves to the Emperor and be able to apply for permits. With the help of these their position in the provinces would be secured. When we represented that missionaries had not up to the present been required to hold such permits, we also said that any such which they might have, had never thus far been demanded of them.

### 43. To what missionaries and by what law are permits to be issued

The answer was: permits will be given to those who have been adjudged not to be agitators and enemies of peace, and do not speak badly of the teaching and laws of China before they understand them, and who wish to remain in China and not return to Europe. We entreated the Prince for time to be accorded us so that the missionaries could be warned about this order. The Prince took our request to the Emperor, who replied that he granted a postponement of the judgement’s publication; he ordered, however, that we ourselves should make it known to the missionaries by declaring to them that should come to receive letters of permission at once, that is as many as wished to stay permanently in the provinces of China. This is the sum total of what we were able to achieve. Now before many days a certain decree of the Emperor was communicated to us: that we should summon all missionaries to Beijing because the Emperor wished to see them so as to choose those who would remain in China, namely those whom His Majesty judged not to be inclined to stir up crowds. In this communication he said he was giving us the option to summon, if we were so minded, all the Europeans. If we preferred they would be summoned by the Emperor through his Viceroys and sent to Beijing. Against this we pointed out the considerable inconveniences which compliance with such an order would entail and entreated that we be granted a full three-day period before the said decree should be put into effect.

### 46. What remedy for the present troubles

I will say one thing, that it is necessary in the present state of affairs that great satisfaction be afforded the Emperor with profound humility if anyone ventures to hope that the Emperor might be won over and be persuaded to revoke what he has decided. I believe as long as Your Excellency continues in China reluctant to please the Emperor, his anger will continue to flare, rather than die down. This is especially true <!-- PDF page 281 | book page 271 | manuscript page 496 --> when, apart from everything else, his first-born son, like an inciting fire, does not cease to be provocative and brings the blaze to life by breathing on it.

There is a strong rumour that the Emperor at the beginning of spring will go to the provinces of Nanjing and Zhejiang. If this happens |, we will try to request that missionaries be permitted to seek permits there and that they should present themselves to His Majesty on his journey. I ask that that a holy blessing be conferred on me and all my companions.

Your Excellency’s most humble and obedient servant,

J.F. Gerbillon

Others gave it out that the missionaries had become like bonzes who are accustomed to be provided with letters of permission by the Tribunal,[^p283-73] and now they barely survive and are even subject to the cudgels of the mandarins.

### 50. What was liked about the letter and what was criticized by certain missionaries

Some complained that nothing was set out in those letters which could constitute a respectful reference to Europeans. They were said to be Europeans in deprecating terms, just as a man from the low classes is said to be from Shandong or Guangdong. At least there should have been set down: Mr. N. a European doctor,[^p283-74] etc. And even His Excellency the Legate himself <!-- PDF page 284 | book page 274 --> professed to hold this view in a certain letter to Fr. Gerbillon written in Nanjing after his bitter missive of January 18th. Thus personal and superficial considerations were prevailing. They ignored that it was a question of all Europeans being expelled from China. The Emperor, putting aside the great offenses that he had suffered, was unwilling to consent to his son’s wishes that Europeans should be once and for all eliminated. He persuaded his son that he should allay his anger and devise letters of permission to save those Europeans in China who would leave the laws of China untouched. The son himself, although unwilling, granted this concession to the Europeans. And the Europeans, moved I suppose by a sense of righteous indignation, rose up against their unwilling benefactor. They claimed they were insulted on the grounds that they were not called “doctors” but “Europeans” by the Emperor and his eldest son.

### 51. What answer should be made to them

Good Lord! Without a murmur, for forty six years now in Beijing in the splendour of the palace under the Kangxi emperor, we have daily endured being called and written down as “European men.” Never, even from a very well-disposed emperor, have we asked to be called “European doctors.” If there was an offence, we endured it for God’s sake. How could we have asked for such a thing either for ourselves or others from a wrathful emperor at the very time when, having pronounced sentence, he had one European in chains and was sending two into exile, together with a third, Fr. Guéty, who two months before in Manchuria had so insensitively taken up the title of “doctor” to the amusement of all the camp.

As to what is said about being placed on the same level as the bonzes, let us grant it to be true and that the Europeans have become subject to the cudgels of the tribunals. If blame is to be assigned to anyone, let it be assigned to those through whose fault the Emperor was offended, those who without understanding them set themselves against the laws of China and exposed themselves and others to the penalties set down for those who fail in their duty. In addition, thought should be given to the immunity of the church in China. Whoever comes and wishes to stay among the pagans, both Manchu and Chinese, for the love of Jesus, ought daily be prepared for such experiences if they have come to preach him. Mission fields do not flourish here as mere badges of honour, rewarded by ecclesiastical promotion. There is still a great difference between Beijing and Rome, between a light under a bushel and a flaming torch on a candelabra.[^p284-75]

<!-- PDF page 285 | book page 275 | manuscript page 498 -->

Then what is said about our level footing with the bonzes is not true. The bonzes are controlled by one of the tribunals and the local mandarins. But because of their permits the Europeans are not subject to any tribunal. Nor, as Henkama rightly says, is there any mandarin who, on seeing them, will not show them respect. They are permits directly issued by the Emperor, those called long piao,[^p285-76] the sign of the dragon, bearing the seal of the Master of the Imperial Household. Recourse to him is granted to European missionaries, | and by reason of this they are exempt from the authority of the ordinary officials.

In addition it is there clearly set out that a European man has been admitted into presence of His Majesty, which in China is the highest honour and together with the aforesaid permits indicates a singular privilege. However, careful restraint in using them is always necessary.

The end of the year 1706
