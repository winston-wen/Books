### 1. The Lord Patriarch’s decree against the Chinese rites is delivered to Beijing

On March 3rd a copy of the decree against the Chinese rites which the Lord Patriarch had published in Nanjing on February 7th and dated January 25th arrived in Beijing as was mentioned in its proper place in February. It was sent through Fr. José Monteiro the Vice-Provincial together with some other letters, all of which I will include here. And since these things were done by people who were not in Beijing, no one should complain if the strict order of time cannot be adhered to.

Among the letters, the first place will be given to one from the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch, which was addressed to Father Antoine under the date February 10th.

### 2. His hostile and contentious letter

May I offer you my genuine sympathy for the trouble with your eyes, and I thank Your Reverence for all the other things which are contained in your letter, which was delivered to me yesterday by Father Vice-Provincial. You will receive news of the Apostolic Decision about which you seem anxious, from the same Father who was present at the publication of the decree. Among his instructions he has the obligation of communicating it to all of his subjects, and of exacting an oath guaranteeing absolute conformity to it by all who wish to remain in China.

From this Your Reverence will understand how alien to what is due to my office are those requests which were made to me at Beijing with regard to the controversies, and how much more wisely the Jesuits would have acted by trusting in and acting according to my advice. Anyone who at his first step diverges from the right path continually strays further from his goal. Superiors are always to be treated with trust and confidence, lest the wrath of God come down on those who are not faithful to Him.[^p326-1] But Your Reverence, and with him the other Jesuits, will hear from Father Vice-Provincial what my mind is on this subject. With the love of a father I am opening up a way by which all the affairs of Your Reverence may be put in order, by which peace will be restored to the mission, and the missionaries who are heralds of true peace will not be subject to reproaches from the gentiles on account of contention and rivalries between <!-- PDF page 327 | book page 317 --> them. Let old things pass away and everything be renewed.[^p327-2] Would that all may turn out happily like this with the help of the Divine Providence, which at times, in a wonderful way, by the abundance of grace, out of adverse and desperate circumstances, sometimes provides favourable and unexpected results. To plead for this grace, in union of prayers, I am sending also to the Bishop of Beijing the Brief of Indulgence[^p327-3] to be published everywhere; meanwhile let Your Reverence pray to the Father of Mercies on my behalf, to the One who consoles us in all our tribulations, so that we too may be able to bring consolation to those who are in great distress; and then I will willingly spend myself on your behalf. May God be propitious to Your Reverence.

Dated at Nanjing. 10 February 1707

Your Reverence’s most devoted servant,

Charles Thomas, Patriarch of Antioch.[^p327-4]

No one could understand this letter from the Lord Patriarch and since he referred himself to the letters of Father Vice-Provincial, we scrutinized them diligently, with the only result that we understood the Patriarch’s letter even less than before.

### 3. The Jesuits have differing views about the letter

He seemed to be wanting to say to us that he was still leaving a way open for us by which the decree passed against the rites could be mitigated or suspended. And this was in accordance with the letter from Father da Silva and Father Monteiro, which stated fairly clearly that we were making a request that we should be allowed to negotiate with the Emperor so that there would be no need for an examination, but rather that the permits should be offered indiscriminately to all missionaries rather than granted as a concession. But this did not make sense. For if, as the Lord Patriarch wrote on January 18th, the practices of the Society had been condemned by the Supreme See and if the Lord Patriarch was certain that there was a definitive pontifical decision and one binding in faith against the Chinese Rites, how could he find a way to get around a decision of the Holy See?

<!-- PDF page 328 | book page 318 | manuscript page 521 -->

But it was unthinkable that this decree of the Patriarch in its entirety had been issued only so that the Jesuits would be forced either | to say the same as had been said in the past by the Bishop of Conon and Guéty, etc., and as their associates were to say in the future, with the result that they would have to leave China; or, on the other hand, would be obliged to arrange that the followers of the Bishop of Conon should not be presented to the Emperor, and thus the Jesuits would be able to enjoy a truce until the pontifical decree itself was produced. But I say this was unthinkable because it seemed wicked to expose the mission to complete destruction to save the Bishop of Conon from appearing to have made a mistake, and on the strength of a completely baseless supposition that the Jesuits of Beijing could have prevented the Emperor from vindicating so forcefully[^p328-5] the laws of his empire.

But I will omit any further argument and will quote the rest of the letters for others to reflect on, so that perchance after the event they may be able to come more happily to an understanding of what has already been mentioned.

### 4. A letter from the Father Vice-Provincial to the Beijing Jesuits regarding the Lord Patriarch’s decree

The second letter written on February 10th was one from Father Vice-Provincial, José Monteiro, to Father Antoine Thomas, Vice-Rector at Beijing, regarding the communication of the Patriarch’s decree published on February 7th. The text of the letter follows:[^p328-6]

At the request and order of the Most Excellent and Most Reverend Lord Patriarch, Apostolic Visitor, etc., I give notice to Your Reverence, and through Your Reverence to all the Fathers of the Vice-Province resident at the Court and in the provinces of the North, of the attached decree published here on the 7th of February this year. Your Reverence will see to it that everybody will swear to observe it faithfully and sign to that effect, and Your Reverence should return it to me with the signatures of all attached to the same copy or another or others so that I may deliver it to His Excellency in accordance with his orders. Since His Excellency in his decree places this obligation only on those who make a request to remain in China and in the mission, I impose no obligation to remain on anyone who does not feel disposed to do as he demands. Before I give permission for anyone to leave the mission, whether they would prefer to stay or are unable to do so, I cannot permit nor will permit it unless they first swear to observe the decree and sign to that effect. I trust in the spirit of the Fathers that nobody will depart the mission.

<!-- PDF page 329 | book page 319 | manuscript page 522 -->

Finally, Your Reverence will communicate to all the Fathers the decree of His Excellency of January 28th together with the other paper containing my petition and His Excellency’s reply, so that all may know the obligation it entails. I did not put in my application the names of the Fathers who serve the Emperor where you are nor that of Father Fridelli[^p329-7] since I am assuming that Your Reverences have not requested the piao from the Emperor. If you should for some reason be obliged to request it, you must beforehand, if you can do so, or afterwards by an appeal like the one I have made, seek an exemption from His Excellency lest you incur suspension by virtue of his decree.

I recommend myself to the holy sacrifices of Your Reverence.

Nanjing, 10 February 1707,

Your Reverence’s least Servant, José Monteiro.

The Patriarch’s decree which was issued on January 28th and delivered to Fr. Monteiro on February 1st together with the petition from this Father and the answer from the Lord Patriarch were included by us above under January 31st and February 6th.

### 5. A letter of the same Fr. José Monteiro to Fr. Tomás Pereira on the same matter

The third letter is from the same Father to Fr. Tomás Pereira written on February 11th.[^p329-8]

My dear Fr. Tomás Pereira,

The most serious danger we are in is that that firstly His Excellency has ordered under pain of | suspension ipso facto from the offices of missionary and priest that nobody request the piao from the Emperor without protesting his obedience to the Holy See and to his superiors. Secondly, he issued and published on the seventh of this month his decree in which under pain of excommunication latae sententiae reserved to the Pope and himself the orders that when being interrogated about the Chinese Rites in the process of requesting the piao we should reply in conformity with the decision of 20 November 1704 in Rome, which as His Excellency states in the same decree is totally opposite to what with so much effort we defend. His Excellency sees the danger to the <!-- PDF page 330 | book page 320 --> mission, and the threat of death or exile of the missionaries, and considers that the safety of the mission rests in the hands especially of Your Reverence and the rest of the Fathers of the Court.[^p330-9] It may be that he will seize the opportunity to send a letter to the Emperor which he believes will serve to open a way for Your Reverences to approach the Emperor. He has said many times that the efforts of Your Reverences from now on must be to no other end than the good of the mission and that in this matter there is no need to wait for advice or orders.

When we present ourselves in one place or another to request the piao we are obliged henceforth without any qualifications to say that we accept and promise to remain always in China, subject to due obedience to the Holy Apostolic See and our Superiors, and may not omit saying this without incurring suspension ipso facto from the office of missionary and priest. If we happen to be asked our opinion whether the Laws and Rites of China are licit, we are under no obligation to say anything, but if they persist in such questions, we are obliged to respond that Shangdi and Tian are not our true God; that the ji to Heaven, Earth, etc., and to Confucius and the ancestors, are illicit etc.; and that tablets of the ancestors cannot be retained. The Jing Tian inscription given by the Emperor must be removed from the churches. Having received this notice Your Reverence will consider seriously what can and ought to be done to conserve the mission. You should pay attention to avoiding any diversity in the approach or statements of one missionary from another on the occasion of receiving the piao. Your Reverences and all the Company will have much to endure since you will be blamed for what has happened just as you have been blamed already for so many things.

In the present changing situation of our principal affair it is essential to give complete attention to securing that none of the missionaries be ejected from the mission, that all experience the same benevolence of the Emperor, and finally that the Lord Patriarch receives no further molestations. If we can do this we shall gain much merit in the sight of God and can hope to dissipate much of the discredit that the Company has incurred in the recent controversy, and in the end we will not be ordered by the Pontiff to leave the missions of the Orient. The Lord Patriarch says that the preservation of the mission lies in Your Reverence’s hands, and it is to the advantage of Your Reverence and all of our people that Your Reverence works in this matter in such a way that either his view is proved true or his error is made manifest. I beg Your Reverence over and over again, for Jesus Christ and the zeal that you have for souls and the good of our Company, that you devote all your energy and all the means at your disposal <!-- PDF page 331 | book page 321 | manuscript page 523 --> to confront and placate the anger and disgust of the Emperor against the Lord Patriarch in order to save the mission and all the missionaries in it.

I commend myself to the holy sacrifices of | Your Reverence. May God preserve your health and may the same Lord assist you to achieve what I hope and pray for in this matter.

Nanjing, 11 February 1707.

Your Least Servant,

José Monteiro

### 6. The Emperor is offended and angry

The obscure succession of events connected with this matter, by following the present letter of Father Monteiro, began to make sense as follows. No one, not even the Lord Patriarch, had any doubts but that the Emperor has been offended by His Excellency, and was angry with all those who follow the Bishop of Conon. This was established by the many decrees in the Chinese language which had been handed to the Lord Patriarch. It was clear in the exile of the Bishop of Conon and Father Guéty, and even more obvious in the banishment of Father Mezzafalce, not because he was a follower of the Bishop of Conon but only because he had said to the Prince and through him to the Emperor that he neither disapproved or approved of the opinion and practices of the Bishop of Conon regarding the Chinese rites.

### 7. With whose advice the Lord Patriarch’s decree was composed

As a result of this, the Emperor had issued a decree throughout the whole of China that henceforth no European was to remain in the empire unless he had the royal permit. However he would grant these to no one who answered in the same way as Father Mezzafalce, much less to those who in a preliminary examination answered in the same way as the Bishop of Conon. It was clear that all the European missionaries would have to leave China rather than dare to face the examination; or if they did face it and still approved of the opinion of the Bishop of Conon, or even refused to disapprove of it, they would immediately be ordered by the Emperor to remove themselves completely from the empire.

In this predicament, the Lord Patriarch was looking for a solution from about the 15th of January until about the 25th of the same month. As his advisers he was using the Most Rev. Frs. François Montigny, a Frenchman who had been in China for four years; Pierre Hervé, a Frenchman who had been in China for about seven years; Bartholomeo Carvalho from Manila, who had been ordained priest three or four months previously; Ilarione Sala, who had been in China for two years; and Francesco di S. Giorgio and Andrea Candela, both of whom had come from Europe recently with His Excellency.

<!-- PDF page 332 | book page 322 | manuscript page 524 -->

These men considered that the best plan was to draw up a decree to oblige all the missionaries, irrespective of which of the practices they had followed in China, to respond that they were following now, would follow in the future, and were bound to follow what the Bishop of Conon had done and said. In this way it was thought that the result would be that either all the missionaries would remain or none. They were not afraid that no one would be left because they supposed that the Jesuits wanted to remain and also were able to remain. And so it seemed that what should be done was that they should, by the Lord Patriarch’s powers, be bound by a common bond to all the other missionaries, so that when they survived the shipwreck they could save the others.

So that this could be done effectively and with justice, the Jesuits would have to be portrayed as having been condemned by the Holy See, and a definitive decree of the Holy See produced. Even if such had in fact been in existence in China, it did not preclude the right to make an appeal on account of its false basis and of the incompetence of those providing evidence, as had been revealed perfectly clearly, especially since the Emperor himself in the manner of an appellant had sent fresh information to the Supreme Pontiff opposing the Bishop of Conon and the Lord Patriarch himself. Therefore it seemed more advisable to issue the Lord Patriarch’s decree as if it were founded on a pontifical definition (irrespective of whether it actually existed or not in China) along with the most severe penalties to be incurred ipso facto if obedience was not given to the one who was present and issuing the order. And in this way, the transgression against the person of the Apostolic Visitor etc. would immediately seem much more offensive in the loyal and scrupulous opinion of the missionaries, as well as inspiring a more resounding feeling of dread. It is true that they were not unaware that this involved considerable risk. For it was impossible to doubt that a gentile Emperor, after | he had made an appeal, after he had avenged the laws of his country by exiling the Bishop of Conon, after he had published his own decree in Beijing and in the provinces of Guangdong and Sichuan, and after he had summoned all the Europeans for an examination and to accept the permit would by any means coolly accept being resisted to his face by the Lord Patriarch. For this was what he was doing by forcing the missionaries under a grave penalty to give those very same replies, or others very similar to them, on account of which just recently he had reluctantly spared the Bishop of Conon from capital punishment.

### 8. The destruction and ruin of the Chinese mission if the Lord Patriarch’s decree is put into practice

Since these perils were by no means unknown, prudent men were in no way able with a good conscience to proceed with exposing the mission to certain and irreparable ruin. Further, it seemed absolutely necessary to proceed in this way, because there appeared to be no other course by which those who followed the Bishop of Conon would be able to maintain their presence in China or to be saved by others.

How could a conscience find peace when it was crying out that the spiritual ruin of countless numbers of the Chinese race should not be risked to vindicate the opinion of one or two people, not for the salvation of souls, but to protect an individual’s reputation?

Can you imagine that a few Jesuits would be able to keep in check by their own efforts all those dangers and inconveniences, the destruction of the mission, and what was the basis of all this, the Emperor’s anger. And also that they could be put under an obligation by censures, forced by fear of personal danger, and likewise mollified by the request of their Superior, and cajoled with the hope of a letter to the Emperor which had been promised by the Lord Patriarch.

This is about all that could be gathered from what Nanjing had so far sent us. Perhaps these same matters will become clearer from additional things to be related in due course.

### 9. A letter from Fr. da Silva to Fr. Antoine Thomas and the Beijing Jesuits

The following letter was written later, but because the couriers were on holidays it was sent in the same post by Fr. Antonio da Silva to Fr. Antoine Thomas, dated February 17th.[^p333-10]

My Reverend Father Antoine Thomas, Vice-Rector etc. and the other Beijing Fathers,

I received a letter from Your Reverence written on the 20th of January and thank you very much for the news contained in it. The Lord Patriarch is very upset that the piao or royal permit which has been given to missionaries to remain in China was granted without first consulting him and I am afraid he has got into his head a suspicion that the invention of this piao was suggested by our Fathers of Beijing. Further, he is convinced that everything has been done to throw out all those who are not of the Company and says that he has received a letter from Beijing in which he is informed that the Emperor asks <!-- PDF page 334 | book page 324 | manuscript page 525 --> questions of the missionaries, especially since the Fr. Vice-Provincial and I had convinced him that the opposite was the case, and given everything that has actually happened, we don’t know what to believe. Time and time again I have placed before his eyes the danger to the mission, and I have represented to him with many tears and begged His Excellency through my tears to reconsider the situation, which is clearly dangerous. Despite all this, His Excellency insisted on the three instructions that Fr. Vice-Provincial sent to Your Reverences in Beijing. Here there seems no way in which we can resist him. When he published the order forbidding the Chinese Rites, Father Vice-Provincial begged him in front of all to show him the alleged decree of the Holy Pontiff, and His Excellency replied that it was not necessary to show it. A few days later I made the same request to His Excellency in the presence of the same Fr. Vice- Provincial, and requested that at least he should give orders to hand over to me a document or authentication by which it could be made clear to everybody that I had requested His Excellency to show the decree of the Holy Pontiff, and again he would not listen to me. Then in our house I discussed and consulted with Fr. Vice-Provincial about the possibility of appealing against this order | to the Holy Pontiff. In the course of dealing with this matter I consulted some books and read texts which said that he had to show us his credentials in order to be believed and received as Visitor Apostolic to whom had been committed powers of acting etc. However, after examining the order closely and everything that would happen if we shut the door, I thought it better to obey immediately to the credit of the Company rather than to be obliged to do so under threat of sanctions without being heard or admitted, to the discredit of the Company. Furthermore, the same Lord Patriarch said to me firstly that the decree passed on 20 November 1704 was the same that had been drawn up in the time of Innocent XII.[^p334-11] And secondly he said if he had published the decree exactly as it had come from Rome it would have appeared as an affront to the Company; in acting as he did he had no other intention than to show his concern for the standing of the Company which he cherishes, and its reputation in the eyes of others, and he said much more of the same sort that I found myself unable to believe. My church in Nanjing has since the arrival of the Lord Patriarch <!-- PDF page 335 | book page 325 --> been out of bounds to Europeans, and neither the Lord Patriarch nor his clerics enter its portals because of the Jing Tian tablet. From the moment the decree was intimated to me I took down and carried out of the church the Jing Tian, and all the Fathers of these parts did the same. We were observed doing so in all those that were open.

### 10. They are asked to come to the rescue of a cause that is virtually lost

From everything I have described and said to you I can only warn you of the danger which threatens the missionaries and the mission. On the one hand is the will of the Emperor, on the other the will of the Lord Patriarch; both opposed to each other. I have no other recourse than to the Most Merciful God and prostrate before God I appeal to the Most Reverend Fathers of Beijing, whom I beg and implore by the wounds of Christ Crucified, to see if they can either directly or indirectly deliver us all from the danger in which we find ourselves. One way would be not to make petitions to anyone and to make our protests on receiving the piao of our subordination to the Holy Patriarch and other Superiors, if we do not find repugnant this condition that we impose on ourselves. I will see more clearly when I seek it in Beijing, or when the Emperor comes to these parts, but I have no time to write more on account of the couriers who are about to close the post. I ask Your Reverence to share this letter with all the Reverend Fathers, and the Lord Patriarch entrusts me with begging all the Reverend Fathers in his name to see whether in these circumstances they have some means of saving the mission, which is well on the way to perdition.

I recommend myself to your holy sacrifices.

Nanjing, on 17 February 1707.

Your Reverence’s Humble Servant in Christ,

Antonio da Silva.

From the content of the above letters it is quite clear that they were written in this way with the knowledge and at the instigation of the Lord Patriarch; that fact is proved from an authenticated account given by Most Rev. Fr. da Silva, preserved in the Archives in Beijing, and sent from Nanjing on 23 July 1707.[^p335-12] In it he writes: “So you can see clearly the danger to the mission and the missionaries. And the Lord Patriarch claims that the piao was invented in order to expel <!-- PDF page 336 | book page 326 | manuscript page 526 --> from China all the clerics, and he fears even for himself. He urged Father Vice- Provincial and me to write our letters to the Fathers of Beijing to come to aid of the mission which is lost, and that on the assumptions above they should see if they have some means of saving it which will save all the missionaries. We wrote these letters to Beijing and I will tell you the replies we received. The Father Vice-Provincial before sending the copies etc.” |

### 11. A note on the Lord Patriarch’s decree

This then is proof that the danger to the mission and to the missionaries was not unknown to the Lord Patriarch, but that he wished everyone to be involved in it, and that he presumed that some of them (the Beijing Jesuits) had the power to save themselves and all the others. In the meantime let the world judge, until such time as God is the judge, whether on the strength of this unjustified supposition the Patriarch’s decree should have been published in China pretending that it was in accordance with a Pontifical decision, while that decision on 7 February 170713 was not yet in the hands of the Lord Patriarch. And let the world judge whether he should have concealed the pontifical decision even if at that time it was in the hands of the Lord Patriarch as he wished the missionaries to say and believe at serious risk of exile and death.

### 12. It seems to have been produced specifically against the Jesuits

Moreover, once he recognized the danger, the Lord Patriarch before all others wished the Jesuits to be exposed to it. Of this design of his, Most Rev. Fr. da Silva writes in his account: “The Father Vice-Provincial before communicating the translations of the decree of the Lord Patriarch to the Fathers of the Vice-Province, arranged to go to Zhenjiang[^p336-14] (his church and ordinary residence) to prepare himself in his own house for what might happen. Whether wanting to get away from the Lord Patriarch or to appeal to the aforesaid Patriarch, he decided to go to wait for the Emperor with the Fathers who came to keep him company, having informed them of everything that had happened.”

### 13. A letter from Father Vice-Provincial

But let us listen to Father Vice-Provincial himself. On February 23rd he wrote as follows to Father José Soares together with the other Jesuits in Beijing: “And now I say farewell to Your Reverence before Heaven, trusting in the Mercy of God through the prayers and holy sacrifices of Your Reverence and the other Fathers with whom Your Reverence <!-- PDF page 337 | book page 327 | manuscript page 527 --> in his charity will share these my farewells, and begging them earnestly on my behalf to commend me to God. In the morning I leave to await the Emperor at Zhenjiang, and expect either death or at least some blows or exile on account of our decrees, which Your Reverence knows about, which I sent to Father Vice- Rector on the 18th of this month to communicate to all Your Reverences …”[^p337-15]

Also on March 1st he wrote to Father Kilian from Zhenjiang: “The many affairs that occupied me during the time I was in Nanjing prevented me from sending earlier the letters here enclosed to be delivered to Brother Paramino, which came to me for him when I was in Huaian, before I fell into the danger in which I now find myself and which places me in great need of the prayers and sacrifices of Your Reverences. In this situation I strongly recommend myself by way of farewell so that Our Lord God may dispose of me. Whether I live or die, I will be mindful of what I owe to Your Reverence’s affection, and to the zeal with which he has worked so hard in defence of the mission and of our Society; if I die I trust in the Divine Mercy to be able to thank you much better in Heaven …”[^p337-16]

He also wrote in a similar vein to Father Superior J.F. Gerbillon, Father Fridelli, etc., which I omit for the sake of brevity. But I will refer to one short one sent to Fr. Antoine Thomas on May 7th from his boat near Huaian, which he thought would be the site of his future suffering:

From Nanjing I wrote to Your Reverence and sent the decrees which the Lord Patriarch issued, and I informed you that I would leave there to await the Emperor’s pleasure. Now I write to you these instructions sent from Huaian through the post station there. When I was about to depart from Nanjing I requested the aforesaid Patriarch to come here to hold discussions with the Fathers who accompany His Majesty about the way to assist the mission. In this way I sought to do everything possible in a matter of such importance, while knowing full well how little I could hope for in the natural order of things from a gentile King who has already made up his mind. I decided to travel in this direction to meet up with the Fathers and to try | to see if it is possible for all the missionaries to receive their piao without undergoing any interrogation about the Chinese rites. I am fully conscious of how great my danger is in coming like this, but I trust in divine grace. I have no doubts that I will endure some sort of trouble and am prepared to give my life in obedience to and service of the Holy Church. I have left my papers and affairs in Zhenjiang in good order as best I could to meet anything that might happen. I commend myself to Your Reverences’ <!-- PDF page 338 | book page 328 --> blessing and holy sacrifices and those of the other Fathers, with all the efficacy that the predicament and danger I am in demands, and include you in mine and those of the Fathers of the South.

On the boat on its way to Huaian, 7 March 1707.

Your Reverence’s least servant, José Monteiro.[^p338-17]

It seems that at this point the reader would be inclined to expect that I would highly praise the courageous spirit of the Father Vice-Provincial together with his prompt obedience, and bring to a conclusion the whole story of this strife.[^p338-18] But by doing that I would have strayed too far from the affairs of Beijing to which I am now returning.

### 14. The Jesuits make a prudent decision to delay

We read carefully the letters from His Excellency and the ones from Very Rev. Frs. Antonio da Silva, the Vicar Apostolic, and Fr. José Monteiro, the Vice-Provincial, which had been written at Nanjing and sent to us towards the end of February. Nothing occurred to us at Beijing which took precedence over our decision taken long ago, which was that if in fact the Holy See had made that decision, we would take the oath to abide by the decision, and to entrust the fate of the mission to Divine Providence, with the certainty that we would suffer either exile or death in obedience to the commands of the Holy See.

However we did observe that there were many things which were demanded of us which in the text itself were not clear, and which were not fully compatible with one another. The result was that we were not able to send back an immediate response to Nanjing. We waited for some days to see if more recent letters might arrive and provide explanations, and above all it seemed best not to make any hasty decision since in fact it was being said that His Excellency was considering a letter to open up for us a way of acting with the Emperor in these very perilous circumstances. This seemed especially necessary since the Lord Patriarch was looking for help for the mission as it was on its way to destruction, and at the same time to secure his own safety and that of his associates.

<!-- PDF page 339 | book page 329 | manuscript page 528 -->

And as a result of this, it was concluded with sufficient certainty that His Excellency did not want to provoke a gentile monarch to open persecution of our holy religion, and that what he wanted to influence us to provide primarily was not an oath but our assistance. But this was absolutely impossible unless the Lord Patriarch made that concession to the Emperor which he only could provide. Finally, lest the rather long silence on our part should be regarded as a fault, on March 5th Fr. Antoine Thomas the Vice-Rector, in order to express summarily the vehemence of our distress, wrote to this effect to the Father Vice- Provincial:

### 15. The Jesuits did try to prevent the imperial decree to ask for the

How much did I learn from the letters sent to me on 17 February. Dear Jesus! The Chinese mission is sliding down into certain and imminent destruction. The Most piao Illustrious Legate is doing us the gravest injustice in thinking that the royal decree (regarding the piao) was brought about by us when, on the contrary, for several days we pleaded with all our might to prevent it, even to the point of enduring not a few rebukes. But much less are we able to prevent it from being put into effect. So in these circumstances it is useless for the missionaries to face the Emperor and to request the permits; it is more likely that they will provoke him. For to declare in his presence that they do not hold the opinions of the Bishop of Conon, while on the other hand they have signed the decree, would be nothing but deception, which when finally revealed would be the cause of absolutely total ruin, with the Emperor complaining that he had been hoodwinked. I remain here prostrate on my bed because of my illness, and grieving over | the many threatening things which cannot be prevented.

Then on March 7th Fr. Tomás Pereira wrote to Frs. Antoine da Silva and José Monteiro a letter for both, as follows:

### 16. Father Tomás Pereira gives an answer to some requests from certain Fathers

The Peace of Christ.

I reply to both of you Fathers with this brief and joint letter since my old weakness of the head, now all the more severe given the seriousness of the matter about which I write, does not permit me to write more. There are three principal points on which you request a response.

Firstly, Your Reverences state that I am suspected along with other Fathers of conceiving the plan, which the Emperor then agreed to, that all Europeans must seek a diploma by virtue of which they may remain in China preaching the gospel. Secondly, you write that the Lord Patriarch says it is in my hands and those of the Fathers of Beijing to remedy the ills that the same Most Excellent <!-- PDF page 340 | book page 330 --> Lord recognizes and admits will follow for the mission from the publication of the decree issued by His Excellency. Thirdly, Your Reverences, as do others of the Fathers, in order that no missionaries be thrown out of China, insist that all the missionaries must be treated with equal benevolence, and that the person of His Excellency should suffer no future interference from the Emperor.

I reply to the first: I do not doubt that I am under suspicion at the moment and that I was suspected of such previously I know by direct experience. Each of the Fathers must answer for himself. I cannot undo or disavow these suspicions which arose without any fault on my part beyond doing what Your Reverences demanded of me. But neither by this nor anything else without some miracle could I free myself from this charge in the eyes of those who suspect me without grounds. In this court I availed myself of my legal rights and requested to be examined; I was not heard. Of my own accord I offered to take an oath to absolve myself; I was denied this. I was both found guilty and sentenced:[^p340-19] as for the first, on grounds that I had already been heard; as far as the penalty is concerned, I accepted it though innocent (for the sake of preserving the peace and avoiding scandal),[^p340-20] contenting myself with giving my explanation in writing to my Legitimate Superior and to the Procurator.[^p340-21] However the writing was not thought worthy to be put into the hands or placed before the eyes of the Judge. I put all of this out of my mind, as God is my witness, dismissing all rancour and desire for vindication. And so in Tartary and in this court I did not cease placating the anger of the Emperor against the Holy Patriarch with all my strength and in every way I could. The witnesses to all this are the Fathers who were present; the eunuchs who many times rebuked me for my frequent appeals; the Emperor himself who forbade me to repeat these petitions, and in four months scarcely admitted me to his presence three times (whereas at other times he saw me several times in the space of a few days), lest I persist with tears and entreaties in opposing what he had determined. Among many witnesses let me single out one in particular, the Bishop of Beijing, who knows and can give evidence how many times on behalf of his being granted the piao, I applied kneeling, beating my head on the ground, not before the Emperor into <!-- PDF page 341 | book page 331 | manuscript page 529 --> whose presence I was not admitted but in the ante-room where all the Fathers congregated to perform these sad offices. I say no more since I don’t have time to excuse myself.

As for the second: Would that the belief of the Lord Patriarch were true. It is no more | within my power to prevent these evils than it is to convert the Emperor to the holy faith! If now there seems to be serious damage and bad effects through the publication of the decree, at least it should have been obvious at the time of publication. And if they were foreseen, it could have been prevented from being published. If it is granted with certitude that the Holy Pontiff has reached a determination in the form alleged,[^p341-22] there would be no Jesuit on earth[^p341-23] who would not rather die in the service of the Holy See than refuse to accept its decision and verdict.

### 17. There is no way of appeasing the Emperor if the Lord Patriarch’s decree is enforced

It was unnecessary to go further and impose under pain of an oath an obligation for the missionaries to say to an angry Emperor to his face: “Your Majesty says and the Chinese say that by the words Tian and Shangdi is meant the True God whom we adore. We say rather, on the contrary, that Your Majesty and the Chinese understand by them an idol. Your Majesty says that there is nothing wrong with the tablets and that nothing is sought before them according to the laws of the land; but we say that Your Majesty and the Chinese commit stupid errors in this rite and irrationally make supplications of your dead. Your Majesty says that the offering to Confucius is merely political and good and solely to arouse and preserve the memory of the Master. And we say that it is abominable and heinous, etc. This view, O Emperor, we derive from the Lord Patriarch of Antioch, to whom Your Majesty in Beijing both in word and writing explained the meaning of the rites and Chinese terms. Nevertheless we, rejecting your explanation, swear in the words he proposes, and declare that we will follow nothing that you have explained nor permit your subjects to do so.”

Your Reverences will say that there is no reason for us to present it to the Emperor so crudely and I say that, no matter how much better we do it, the responses which it is proposed we be obliged under oath to make will make such an impression on the mind of the Emperor, and that the effects which will follow will not be what the Europeans imagine but according to the Emperor’s mind and his acute understanding.

<!-- PDF page 342 | book page 332 | manuscript page 530 -->

I write like this on account of the sharp sorrow that pierces my heart, and out of the depths of the sorrow in which I am submerged at seeing the loss of this noble mission. If the Emperor were here at the Court, we might in that case at least once with tears and sighs have asked, sought, and supplicated, not for the abrogation of the laws of China on account of the decree of the Holy Patriarch but that he might allow us quietly to attempt to change the mind of the Holy Patriarch; or, taking pity on us, that he should allow us sufficient time for the Holy See to pay attention to our declarations, which were at sea on the way to Europe. But since His Majesty left this Court on the 22nd day of the first month,[^p342-24] there was no way of taking this route.

I reply to the third point. From what I have said above it should already be clear to Your Reverences how complicated are the matters that you propose to be requested. It is requested that we should secure that no missionary will be expelled. That is not too much to ask. But if you are also saying that we should request that those already expelled should be honourably recalled, who could persuade themselves that the Emperor after so many offenses would put up with those who would immediately, boldly and with European forwardness, propose the same propositions for which he had thrown out the Bishop of Conon? And that he should turn around and recall the same Lord Bishop whose life he graciously spared just a short time ago despite his having committed the crime of lèse majesté by issuing a decree to pull down his imperial yu bi?[^p342-25] | I do not know what more I can say except that with all humility, and as insistently as possible, I advise and beseech Your Reverences to leave China as quickly as possible since you have disrespectfully removed the Jing Tian etc. You have exposed not only yourselves but us to the anger of the Emperor, and since you have already done this, I do not see how I can appear before the Emperor and still hope for success or know what I can say to excuse ourselves.

I wish with all my heart that the Reverend Person of the Lord Patriarch should be safe and sound and I beg God continuously for this in my prayers. However, such being the state of affairs, and short of some miraculous intervention from Heaven, assuming that a pontifical decree has really appeared, I am prepared to swear by the word of the Lord Patriarch. Like the majority of the Fathers as best I can I am prepared for everything whether it is to be expulsion or death. For the time has now come when it will be made manifest to men and angels that I have served for thirty years under a gentile prince not for human advantage but only for the cause of holy religion. Not being able to write more,

### 18. Father Antoine Thomas writes to the Lord Patriarch a description of the sad state of the Chinese mission

### 19. A letter from the Beijing Jesuits to Father Vice-Provincial regarding the Lord Patriarch’s decree

Reverend in Christ, Father Vice-Provincial. As we prepare to send our answer with all due respect, before anything else we humbly declare that all of us have always regarded the sacred person of the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch and his commands and ordinances with the greatest veneration, and that we have never been lacking nor ever will be lacking in any form of obedient observance. And so we earnestly request of Your Reverence, that if it should happen that this letter appears to be somewhat cold, let him not attribute that to our feelings, or to mental confusion. For the nature of this most lamentable business forces us to write in this way but excludes circumlocutions of mere politeness and requires the bare truth.

<!-- PDF page 345 | book page 335 -->

We were unable to understand the letter from the Most Excellent and Most Rev. Lord Patriarch of 18 January of the current year which was sent to all the Fathers. In it, he made most vicious accusations and wanted to see us condemned for terrible things before our case is heard or we are formally charged. Now we are all the more astounded, because relying only on suspicion, without allowing time for explanation, and exercising the great powers which he has, he has published a regulation drawn up by himself, saying that it is in accordance with the mind of the Holy See and a decision made by His Holiness Our Lord Clement XI, and he wants us to take an oath to observe it while admitting that through it the entire mission will be destroyed.

In addition to all this, we simply cannot comprehend why he wants, and in regard to this makes a request to us, to provide a means of saving the Christian cause which is heading for destruction here in this empire. And this after he saw with his own eyes not only the risk of giving offence to the Emperor in the future, but not long ago had actually witnessed the result of the anger he has felt in the banishment of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon and of Fathers Mezzafalce and Guéty, in the arrest of Father Appiani etc., and in the threat made by the Emperor to banish in like manner all missionaries who have not left the rites of the empire intact and who refuse to give reasons by which they could prove that they are evil.

If that regulation is strictly in accord with the pontifical decision, and if it absolutely must be published then it must be observed faithfully; if not one jot nor tittle can be passed over in publishing it, then not one jot or one tittle should be omitted in putting it into practice. It is our duty, without reservation, with firm resolution, and with hearts committed to the service of the Faith and the Holy See to bear everything, both whatever we see has actually resulted in this empire and in the whole of the Orient, as well as those things which we cannot doubt will follow in the future. Indeed, in this regard the Jesuit Fathers will show themselves by no means lacking in the obedience towards the Holy See which they inherited from St. Ignatius; on the supposition that the decision of Clement XI and the regulation of our superior which has been published and communicated are genuine, they will abide by them faithfully, whatever is the result. For such is the nature of this storm that clerics, religious, and Jesuits alike share the danger; the same storms, the same fate, and the same death threaten all of us, including the Most Excellent Lord himself.

In all sincerity, in the presence of the heavenly court and the Pontifical court as well, we testified under oath in the year 1701 that the Chinese Church could not survive for long if the rites of the empire were abolished; at this moment we are able to declare under oath that now that the rites have been abolished by the regulation published by His Excellency under the penalty of excommunication, <!-- PDF page 346 | book page 336 | manuscript page 532 --> nothing remains which can save it. All that is left is that this destruction, along with the irreparable loss of so many myriads of souls, must be endured with patience, which should produce some fruit. If, therefore, at a time when the gentile ruler | was not persecuting the Church, he thought to provoke a persecution before there was, in accordance with due process, an examination of the case inside China, it should not be imputed to the Jesuits of Beijing if it is found not to be in their power to halt a mighty persecution which is descending like a flood.

### 20. The Lord Patriarch makes four demands which cannot be satisfied while his decree stands

Dear Father Vice-Provincial! Why do you either in your own name or in the name of the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch ask us for a remedy? What remedy? Where? When? For whom? And how is it possible? In your own letter you ask us: 1. To arrange that the missionaries will not be questioned with regard to those matters about which the Lord Patriarch has issued orders for such inflexible answers under pain of oath and excommunication. 2. To take precautions that no missionary will be ordered into exile from China. 3. That without discrimination and without previous questioning the royal permit should be given to everyone. 4. To arrange that the declaration prescribed by the Lord Patriarch for use in the act of receiving the permit should be favourably regarded by the Emperor. 5. To placate the Emperor’s feelings so the person of His Excellency may be free from any of the harassment which might be feared from a gentile ruler.

In all of this there are two suppositions, both of which we have proved to be false. 1. It is supposed, while the Most Excellent Patriarch’s regulation stands and is in force, that the mission and our holy religion can survive in China in the way they have so far stood. 2. It is taken for granted that the means are in the hands of the Jesuits of Beijing to carry out these requests. To this we reply:

If while the regulation stands with all its force, and is being enforced, our holy religion has been able to survive, then it has been able to survive because the regulation published as it has been, sworn to, and invested with ecclesiastical penalties is not known to the Emperor and the Chinese. This must be conceded by those who to save the mission are asking that as highest priority we are to take care that nothing should be done to bring to the attention of the Emperor the very stringent replies in the said regulation which are so strictly enjoined. Draw your conclusion from this, that according to us and to them too, our religion cannot survive if the regulation becomes widely known.

And it will, very soon, become public knowledge. For what is there which has so far remained secret in matters where His Excellency was involved with missionaries, converts, courtiers, and the Emperor? On the contrary the regulation <!-- PDF page 347 | book page 337 | manuscript page 533 --> must be considered to have already been made public, since Your Reverence states that it has already produced results that are public. For you write that in virtue of it, the imperial Jing Tian tablet has been removed, and you strongly advise that it should be similarly removed everywhere. By this action, which is not at all clandestine, the flanks of the mission have been exposed, ready to be pierced by the Enemy as often as it pleases him to make an accusation. Let us suppose, although we do not concede, that in China there will be no Satan whose ill-will is going to bring this about; but the regulation publicizes itself, and demands to be seen, because it bids us to preach from the rooftops the things which are enjoined in secret. For by virtue of the regulations, Christians must be taught that the tablets are evil, and the ceremonies on behalf of Confucius and the ancestors are evil, as are the terms Tian and Shangdi. In addition to this there are books which must be burned, and those who disobey the regulation are to be denied the sacraments. Will these matters, so numerous and so serious, escape the notice of the Emperor?

It is true that some time before, some people had taken down the Jing Tian tablet and some of the Chinese rites had been prohibited in one or another small group of the faithful without the Emperor coming to know of it or having cared about it. But we must distinguish between times. By this time, in the Court, this business has become the subject of noisy discussions. The teaching of Confucius has been publicly denounced as being opposed to the Law of Christ in many respects. The Most Illustrious Lord Charles Maigrot, Bishop of Conon, has been named to the Emperor as the patron of this cause, | as the one who is capable of giving an account of the things he has prohibited and it is known that he has made a request that they be prohibited by the Holy See. He has not provided any reasonable explanations, has offended the Emperor, has admitted his ignorance, and has been convicted of causing disturbances and violating the royal yubi.

As a result of these and similar events not unknown to His Excellency, the Emperor’s vigilance has been aroused and a justified (as he thinks) zeal for his own laws has provided him with weapons and with a thousand eyes on the lookout for transgressors.

Even if we were able to conceal this regulation, once it has been published, for one day or for one month from this Argus,[^p347-29] it will be impossible for us to conceal it for a whole year; in this way the last state will be worse than the first, when His Majesty not only considers that he was insulted through the ignorance <!-- PDF page 348 | book page 338 --> of foreigners but will think that he has been mocked by Ministers of the Gospel by such a crass deception. For while they are being sent to request the permit, they are being ordered, with what amounts to an insult, to teach what is contrary to the laws of the one granting the certificate.

From what has been said so far, it is clear how false it is to say that the regulation once published can be kept secret, and from what has been conceded, how false it is that the mission and our religion can survive if the regulation stands and has been published. What purpose therefore will those first four requests achieve? To work hard so that those who are to receive the certificate will not be questioned, and then after the certificate has been obtained by these irregular proceedings, to lodge an objection with His Chinese Majesty; during all this, to take an oath binding ourselves under pain of eternal damnation to think, teach, and answer contrary to the declarations of the Emperor in regard to Chinese terms and rites. This could serve no other purpose than to hasten the ruin of the mission and to shut the Holy Gospel out of China forever.

The fifth request, apart from being connected with what goes before as a cause to its effect, has its own special difficulties which cannot be avoided by any effort on our part, but can easily be avoided by the Most Illustrious and Most Excellent Lord Patriarch. Your Reverence gave us some hope of this by saying that His Excellency was giving thought to a letter to open up a way of providing some remedy. Meanwhile, the Emperor who set out from Beijing fifteen days ago already is daily getting further away from us and coming nearer to Nanjing. Hence even such a letter will perhaps be to no avail, unless it is sent to meet on the way the Jesuits who are accompanying His Majesty, in which case with the blessing of Heaven above it may bring about some result.

Your Reverence will object that in the past many requests have been made by us with happy results. For example the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing, at the insistence of Father Parrenin, was not summoned for an examination, and as a result of our requests he obtained the permit; from this it is inferred that also in this instance our mediation will be successful if we apply ourselves to it.

### 21. The source of the Emperor’s displeasure

To this we answer that the Emperor’s ears as a rule were not apt to take offence, but they were hardened after the first and second offence. Now they are quite closed, to the extent that he does not even admit us into his sight. For by this the Emperor is even more deeply hurt, that the more generously he has treated the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch at our requests, the more seriously he has felt embarrassed in the eyes of his own people, because after so many acts of kindness hitherto unheard of in China he has seen himself so spurned by foreigners that he, the legislator and <!-- PDF page 349 | book page 339 | manuscript page 534 --> the interpreter of the ancient laws, was roundly denied the right to interpret the Chinese rites and terms. | And under pressure of an oath, and the most serious of all the penalties, both missionaries and the subjects of his crown are forced to teach, to believe, and to answer to the contrary; this is at the very time, when out of his esteem for Europeans and with the intention of safeguarding the mission he himself has sent to the Supreme Pontiff his royal commands and explanations, guaranteed by his seals and with everything done in legal form. And he has left it entirely to the Pope to discern before God whether the explanation he has given of Chinese rites and terms is in agreement or disagreement with the Christian Law.

### 22. Why the Bishop of Beijing was treated less strictly by the Emperor

The case of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing is completely different. He was not summoned along with the others, on account of the deposition made by Fr. Guéty in Manchuria, but long afterwards; nor did they bring an accusation against him on account of something he had done. But they treated him simply as a witness of what had been done in the past. In addition to that, the Most Rev. Lord had in his favour the 20 years he had lived in China without any complaints to prejudice his good reputation. And to cap all this off, he gave satisfaction to the Emperor by testifying with his own hand that from the outset he had maintained that practice which had been in use from the times of Ricci, to the growth of our holy religion and the peace of the empire. And in this case, we should not fail to bear in mind that in the very act of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing receiving the permit, the Emperor through the mouth of his firstborn said to us: Let us realize that we are strictly warned that in the future we are not to dare to intercede for anyone else.

Finally, let Your Reverence be assured that here and now one thing alone is left to us: that however much we love the Chinese Church, and long to present it to Christ as his chosen spouse among the gentiles, we can only grieve for its destruction, interceding with our sacrifices, prayers, and every act of piety with the Divine Clemency, begging Him not to abandon China in such a desperate crisis—not only to clearly illuminate the mind of the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch to discern the truth but also to powerfully move his heart, so that in his mercy he may take pity on an infant Church which is on the point of perishing and not allow the power which has been given to him by the Holy See to become a force for destruction.

So that our requests may be granted, we cast ourselves down both before the Throne of God’s Mercy, and at the knees of the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch, beseeching him with burning tears not to permit the mission to be destroyed while he bases his opinion on mere suspicion on which not even a civil magistrate <!-- PDF page 350 | book page 340 | manuscript page 535 --> may proceed in a cause of far less consequence. Or if, in addition to his suspicions he has some foundation, let him condemn the guilty with a sentence that is just but without harming the innocent, since God, for the sake of the innocent, is accustomed to sparing even the guilty.

At the same time, in addition to our distress over the fate of the mission and the irreparable spiritual loss of souls, there was also the agitation of Doctor Borghese to contend with. When he was asked the reason for this, he replied that he was never able to give to his relatives in Europe an explanation as to why he allowed himself to be kept in this palace without employment. ### 26. He takes back and withdraws certain things he said against the Jesuits

There was certainly an abscess pouring such a noxious heat into a heart not rightly in control of itself; but then in the month of June, it was burst when in the presence of Father Buonaventura of the Order of Friars Minor he withdrew in writing certain very strong things he had written against us to the Lord Patriarch and to the Lord Bishop of Beijing, as will be related there.[^p353-31] In the meantime, lest the man’s impatience should cause him to act even more recklessly to his own and our dishonour, Father Antoine made several approaches to the mandarins Zhao and Henkama with earnest requests that if it were possible they would write to the Emperor a full account of some of our petitions for the peaceful dismissal of the doctor. They both said that for such a trivial matter they were not willing to expose themselves to the anger of His Majesty. Even more, they wanted the doctor to be told bluntly that if he presumed to leave Beijing, he would be pursued and brought back in chains as a fugitive. They strictly forbade his servant and others of our domestics to give any aid to the fugitive, under the customary penalties in China. Finally, they made this sole concession on about March 21st that they would accept one letter from Father [Antoine] to be sent to Father Parrenin so that this Father who was in the Emperor’s entourage, if an opportunity presented itself, might advise the Prince just how the doctor was obsessed with this urge to leave, and ask permission for him to depart.

The doctor was aware just how unremittingly the Father was working on his behalf, but by no means content with our zeal for his interests, wanted the Fathers themselves to put it in writing that the Emperor himself should make use of his services, and on this condition alone, he said he would remain quiet. He also wrote to the same effect to the Lord Patriarch, and sent a petition to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing to be received in his house in the city of Linqing at least for the time being. All these matters seem to be of minor moment, but it will not do any harm to have verified them with documents and answers.

<!-- PDF page 354 | book page 344 | manuscript page 538 -->

March 15th. The doctor came to the College and more insistently than usual pressed for his departure. Father Kilian, who was living there at the time on account of his work, told him not to disregard the advice of all the Jesuits. He answered that he could not remain any longer in a place where he was under suspicion. “Very well,” said the Jesuit, “from this very fact you should conclude that the advice given about avoiding the danger is valid, for if you were under suspicion by the Fathers, they would want you to depart as quickly as possible; but if they advise you not to depart even though they suspect you, that shows that the danger they foresee is greater than the danger they suspect they might experience through you.” He undertook to follow this advice, if [Father Kilian], on his part, promised that the Emperor would make use of his skill. The Father said that it certainly could be expected that the Emperor, after he returned, would make a decision. For then, in the 6th month, he would go to Manchuria and would not return until the end of the 9th month, when European ships were preparing to depart; so it could be presumed that he would either order the doctor to accompany him to Manchuria or to take advantage of the ships. He pressed for this to be given him in writing. And Father Kilian immediately wrote out a statement as follows: |

Doctor Borghese is making the request that I should testify in writing what my opinion is with regard to his departure or staying here. Wishing not to fail him in this most genuine request, I say: 1. It seems to be inadvisable and contrary to the experience and advice of all the Jesuits to risk headlong flight and thus offend the Emperor whose goodwill, not displeasure, is to be sought, if in fact Doctor Borghese intends to serve His Majesty for the good of religion. 2. It seems probable that the Emperor, after his return, will either make use of his services or send him away in peace.

I so testify. Kilian Stumpf. Beijing 15 March 1707.

That he communicated to the Lord Patriarch this really burning desire to leave, perhaps even before he mentioned it to us, is clear from an answer from the Most Excellent Lord written with his own hand at Beijing, 17 March 1707. A copy of it follows:

### 27. A letter from the Lord Patriarch to his doctor

Learn the language, and pray for permission to depart; that is the real way to succeed. Also, by remaining there at your leisure, you can be useful, as in fact you have already been of use to me. According to what appears to be the case and the information I have, I do not have to depart so soon. In so far as that goes, perhaps things are taking a different course. ### 28. The illness of Father Jean François Gerbillon, the Superior

On the same day, March 15th, Rev. Father Superior Jean François Gerbillon, who had contracted some disease, began to show symptoms of illness. That letter from which, just a few lines earlier, I quoted a brief extract forces me to write something more about him.

The previous year, Father Superior, after he had acted as interpreter on June 30th for the Most Excellent Lord in the valedictory audience with His Majesty and the Crown Prince, on the next day, July 1st, began to feel ill, suffering from a severe and continuous headache. For two months he tried both Chinese and European medicines administered sometimes by Brother Rodes[^p355-35] and sometimes by Doctor Borghese. Finally he seemed to have been cured with the pain in remission after it was found that pus had been formed under the scalp where the skin was healthy on the left towards the back part of the head. When the scalp was lanced by Brother Rodes, a considerable amount of matter was discharged during the period from September of the year 1706 to March of 1707. The Father, feeling somewhat better from the autumnal equinox until <!-- PDF page 356 | book page 346 | manuscript page 539 --> the spring equinox but with the wound still discharging, was able to resume work within limits. He did not however go to the palace except on those very sad days | in December and January when we decided to risk our lives for the general good of the mission and the particular good of the Bishop of Conon; Fathers Mezzafalce, Guéty, and Appiani; and the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing.

At length, either through excess of grief or with the change of the spring weather, the discharge ceased and the wound in the scalp closed up. A malignant fever followed with the whole of his chest turning purple with spots. For the first three days the disease was not diagnosed. On the fifth, Brother Rodes began to be really alarmed because he found that the pulse was intermittent and no medicine had any effect. The result was that the Brother too gave warning of the danger and asked for the Holy Sacraments to be administered to the patient. On the sixth, Doctor Borghese was summoned, and from the Palace at first two doctors were sent, and then another two. But the malignancy of the disease surpassed their skill and every kind of medication which was administered in large quantities.

### 29. His death on 25 March

The Father, finally, on the 25th at a quarter to three in the afternoon, duly fortified by all the Sacraments of the Church, with Father Antoine Thomas, Jean Régis, Antoine Provana, and Kilian Stumpf praying with him, and with Brother Rodes and Doctor Borghese also present, died peacefully. What added greatly to the grief felt by everyone was that God had called away from us this man whose interventions had long been so useful to the mission at a time when they seemed particularly necessary.

Fr. Kilian brought the news of this death so very sad to us to the Palace on the same day and was told to return on the following day so that the news could be formally announced by courier to the Prince and through him to the Emperor.

March 26th. Father Antoine Thomas, Tomás Pereira, Jean Régis, and Kilian Stumpf went together to the Palace. The word of the death of Fr. Gerbillon was announced by Fr. Pereira, who was in tears, and it was clear that all of us were deeply moved with grief and compassion. The mandarins immediately expressed the message for us who were present, in the Manchu language, and in appropriately sympathetic terms, and after it had been read aloud to us, and completely edited, they entrusted it to a courier. The Third Son, who in his father’s absence was steering the kingdom, wrote the same to His Majesty.

On the same day letters also had to be composed by Fr. Antoine Thomas. One of these was written to the Lord Patriarch and another to the Most Rev.

<!-- PDF page 357 | book page 347 | manuscript page 540 -->

Lord Bishop of Ascalon.[^p357-36] I find that both of them are dated March 25th. Since the death of Father Gerbillon caused no little activity and upheaval at the time in the evening when our letters were ordinarily sent to the office for the post, I do not know if those letters were delayed until the 26th.[^p357-37]

This is the first of them:

### 30. A letter from Father Antoine Thomas to the Lord Patriarch

Most Excellent Lord,

Our distress and anguish by which, in our solicitude for the mission which is on the road to ruin, we feel broken, compels me, even though I am weak and weighed down with sickness, of the spirit rather than of the body, to cry out to Your Excellency with all the strength I can muster to come to the rescue of the mission now involved in immediate danger of extinction. Please do not permit, that by one stroke all the hope of so many souls who are perishing should be extinguished, for we Jesuits here have no power to withstand the certain anger of the Emperor. The sole | remedy rests with the will of Your Excellency since it is you who could deign to suspend the obligation of your regulation, at least until the arrival of the response from His Holiness to the information which the Emperor sent according to the usages prescribed by law.

### 31. He asks him to defer the execution of his decree for a short time

Some time ago, we had already testified under oath before the supreme tribunal of the Holy See foreseeing the crisis which we are now experiencing. But it has turned out to be far worse after the events involving the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, whom Your Excellency introduced, who was with great kindness personally instructed by the Emperor regarding the legitimate meaning of the rites and expressions used in the empire, and who was convicted of ignorance in these matters. For under what circumstances would the Emperor not be offended when, right in his own empire, a decision has been promulgated in favour of the said Most Illustrious Bishop, at the very time he had made an appeal to the judgment of His Holiness following due process through the information he had sent to Rome? Your Excellency heard him complaining in a public audience that Europeans who do not understand the Chinese <!-- PDF page 358 | book page 348 --> rites and books have sent incorrect information to the Holy See, and he said this would be to the great detriment of the Holy Law if it were not faced up to. But how much more will he not complain when he hears that, without waiting for his information and the response from Rome, a decisive decree is being promulgated in China founded on the opinions of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, which he has condemned as being based on ignorance?

It is obvious to everyone and quite certain that the regulation once published cannot for long be enforced without causing trouble, unless the Emperor, who granted freedom for the Divine Law, wants it to be published as far as possible with minimal interference with the customs of the empire, and only where there is a special necessity. But how can he be convinced of any necessity to allow the implementation of the regulation when it is founded on information which he has declared erroneous, and is in contempt of his explanations and his personal judgment about the rites of his empire and expressions that are used. It would be supposing that the authority of a foreigner who did not know their meaning was to prevail, in this mere question of fact, over the royal authority as to what is to be done or what disregarded in the empire of which he is lord, legislator, and interpreter of the laws. In the meantime he intends to outlaw from China all of those who would abolish the Chinese rites following the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon.

### 32. The Jesuits are unjustly accused of being responsible for the royal edict

Your Excellency in your letter seems to attribute to the Jesuits both the banishment of the Most Illustrious Lord and the other misfortunes included in the royal decree. The truth is, and God is our witness as well as the courtiers who were present, that we did everything in our power to block the decree. Over several days we pleaded with the Emperor for mercy, asking him not to impose the obligation contained in this decree; yet some days later in the promulgated versions he added to it other things without our knowing anything at all about it. The Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon himself will witness to what we did on his behalf, for he was threatened with chains, prison, and even more serious things which were ordered by the deposition written with the Emperor’s brush.

However for us this burden would be light, and with patience we would suffer all these things being imputed to us, for some day God will demand an account of them, except for one thing, namely if people were convinced that the Jesuits are the cause of all this because of the assumption that it is in our power to change the mind of the Emperor in any way whatsoever we want according to our good pleasure; also, that we can allow the publication of the <!-- PDF page 359 | book page 349 | manuscript page 541 --> regulation without any danger. For this would have the effect on Your Excellency that you would plunge the Chinese mission into certain destruction and drive all the missionaries into exile, on the assumption that all these evils were to come about not as the necessary consequence of your regulation |, but from our ill will. But I believe that His Holiness, and anyone else whosoever in the whole world and the whole of posterity who are properly informed about the present circumstances, will judge otherwise.

This is especially the case since experience makes it sufficiently clear that in Europe itself, when even minor Christian princes suffer some offence within the bounds of their own dominions, whether at the hands of foreigners or even of powerful kings, they are not easily placated, even when the Supreme Pontiff intervenes as a peacemaker.

### 33. The difficulty in changing the Emperor’s decision

How much influence therefore will a few foreigners have in this kingdom of the Chinese to mitigate the anger of such a mighty Emperor after such and so great contempt shown in the face of such majesty! For after spurning the explanations sent by him to the Supreme Pontiff, a decree has been published against the Chinese rites relying on opposing opinions, which he declares to be clearly false and erroneous and full of ignorance of things Chinese. And in virtue of this decree, there is an order to remove the royal calligraphy irrespective of his declaration that their legitimate meaning is clearly in conformity with the Christian Law.

It must be recognized that the fire of persecution consuming the Church of God is not just something to be feared but something which has already begun. The Emperor’s feelings were hurt long ago; but now when contempt of the Emperor, which to him is most serious, has been added, we foresee that the ultimate destruction of the Church in China will shortly eventuate. Therefore, since the one remedy which can avert these evils is in Your Excellency’s hands alone, as I have explained above, prostrate at your feet I beseech you in tears, and I entreat you through the love of the Heart of Christ to deign to have pity on this infant Church so that it may not perish in its cradle, and to provide whatever help you can to it which is even now perishing. The eternal salvation of numberless souls is at stake here, and to avoid their eternal loss, if we think about it rightly, nothing must be left untried, how much less then this one remedy which remains in the hands of Your Excellency.

But even as I am in the act of writing this, my strength is failing on account of my illness, and forces me to finish even though I wish to write more on this extremely grave matter. Therefore all that remains is that I most humbly beg

<!-- PDF page 360 | book page 350 | manuscript page 542 -->

Your Excellency to forgive me if there should happen to be anything in which I have offended, and to impart your holy blessing.

Beijing. 25 March 1707

Your Excellency’s most humble servant in Christ,

Antoine Thomas.

### 34. A letter from the same Father Antoine Thomas to the Bishop of Ascalon

The following is another letter of the same day, written to the Bishop of Ascalon: Most Illustrious Lord. Through our grief at the imminent total ruin of the mission, we here do not so much live as die daily, since we have no remedy for this, the greatest possible evil. The outcome depends solely on the Most Illustrious Legate, for if he suspends the implementation of the decree until there is an answer from the Supreme Pontiff to the information sent to Rome by the Emperor through the Jesuits, the mission will be saved. Otherwise it is the end of it, and the response from His Holiness | will come too late, after its destruction. And now here in China we are close to seeing the Church in the same state as it is in Japan, and as the result of one over-hasty stroke something is happening which must be a source of grief for many centuries.

### 35. The Emperor has good reason to feel offended

For after the Emperor had found the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon guilty of ignorance of Chinese letters, in my own presence and the presence of others, and had deigned to send his explanations regarding the rites and Chinese terms to the Supreme Pontiff, how great is the displeasure which must be feared if the case is hastily decided in favour of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon on the question of fact without giving the Emperor a hearing! And if this is publicized through all the churches, and even the characters Jing Tian are taken down after he has explained them with so much solemnity as pertaining to the one and true God whom the Christians worship!

Who could hope that such a mighty Emperor would calmly submit to being slighted in this way, and in his own empire, after he had made so many concessions to the Christian religion and granted it liberty by a public decree? Especially since he has declared that he himself is not deciding nor touching <!-- PDF page 361 | book page 351 --> on the question of whether these are compatible with our religion, but rather that he wants to be heard in regard to the rites and terms of his own empire. These are very ancient rites which he does not want to allow to be abolished without the gravest of reasons, or without necessity.

I consider that this claim on the part of such a mighty prince is quite reasonable in a matter of such great moment for the preservation of the peace in his empire, lest the spread of the Christian religion cause disturbance among the people and sooner or later bring about its own ruin. I think that this would be the judgment of Rome and of the world in general and of all future ages, since in former times there are not lacking many unfortunate examples of haste which have proved fatal and are still the object of regret.

Truly, if the Most Illustrious Legate thinks otherwise, and there is no other remedy in our power, and we are forced by the necessity of obeying the Holy See, we will bow our heads to the ruin which is fast approaching, which he is accelerating and which we here must likewise be involved in. Full of grief, I am writing this to Your Most Illustrious Lordship and with tears which will soon have to shed for the destruction of the mission and the loss of innumerable souls.

### 36. It is not in the power of the Fathers to alleviate this

I am asking you to see if you are able to achieve anything with the Legate who has persuaded himself, as is clearly established in the letter recently sent to me, that it is in our power, even when the decree is promulgated and in force, to prevent the hostility of the Emperor which is threatening us and the banishment of the missionaries which must follow. But ignorance will be no excuse for this disastrous mistake and the others contrived against the Jesuits of Beijing, and will not wash away the tears for the China mission which has been destroyed; these will be all the more bitter the more the truth shines out afterwards and realization comes too late of a mistake for the consequences of which there is no remedy.

The grief of heart which weighs down on all of us here prevents me from writing more, as we place our hope in the help of God alone, for in his hands are the hearts of men and all human issues.

For one who loves this mission of ours dearly, this is enough. I commend myself to your most holy sacrifices and most humbly beg your holy blessing.

Beijing. 25 March. 1707.

Your Most Illustrious Lordship’s most humble servant.

Antoine Thomas.

### 37. St. Joseph, patron of the Chinese mission by the authority of the Supreme Pontiff

In addition to taking refuge in the mercy of God, we decided on a novena[^p362-39] both for ourselves and the Christians, in honour of St. Joseph who was appointed the Patron of the Chinese by the authority of the Supreme Pontiff, invoking his patronage with prayer, sacrifices, and other exercises of piety. We petitioned the Divine Providence to suggest to the Lord Patriarch and to his counsellors and to us his servants those means which he knew for certain would be efficacious to save the whole nation, one exceeding in power so greatly nearly all other nations. In the case of this nation, indiscreet zeal on the part of certain persons coupled with a senseless urge to engage in dispute, to the great loss of our holy religion and of the Blood of Christ, denies that there was ever in it any knowledge of God and consequently that there was ever the means of salvation—and, after doing that, endeavours to deprive them of the means of salvation by invoking papal authority.

### 38. The reasons why the Jesuits deferred the implementation of the Lord Patriarch’s decree for some time

The Beijing Jesuits did not take the oath during the whole of this month of March: 1. Because the Lord Patriarch and consequently all the superiors under him allowed them freedom either to take the oath and remain, or not to take the oath and to leave. But in such a difficult situation, there was need for time to deliberate as to what could be chosen without our departure offending the Emperor, or our persons and consciences being put under obligation without due reflection. 2. Because the oath was required from those accepting the piao; but if we were to be obliged to accept it or not had to be decided by the Emperor, who was not due to return until about the middle of June. If, moreover, during the journey he made any such decision, he would have to inform us beforehand. 3. The Lord Patriarch suggested the same in his private letter to Father Superior, which was written in French and in which he said that he supposed that a piao would not be necessary for us, and therefore he had not sent [the oath] to us. 4. Because the Lord Patriarch complained that the pontifical decree had been stolen from him a short time before by Father Manoel Ozorio,[^p363-40] and he would not state positively to anyone that he actually had it in his possession, much less show it to our Ordinary. 5. Because in fact we did not understand clearly to what we were swearing by invoking the name of God, when we took an oath on the Lord Patriarch’s regulation which was drawn up in vague, indeterminate, and ambiguous terms. 6. Because according to the wording of the regulation, things of this sort were presented as having to be held and taught, etc. as matters of faith; yet the foundation of this faith, the Apostolic Constitution, was not shown to anyone. 7. Because the oath also included a proposition in the regulation, namely that the Lord Patriarch of Antioch was in himself the spokesman for the Supreme Pontiff in virtue of his faculties, and no Roman Catholic may take an oath to <!-- PDF page 364 | book page 354 | manuscript page 544 --> this proposition unless it is explained according to the sound teaching of the Church. Further reasons will be submitted to His Holiness when he admits us and the Chinese Church as supplicants at his sacred feet. |

### 40. The Franciscan missionaries are presented to the Emperor and examined

March 9th. The above-mentioned Reverend Fathers were presented by the Jesuits according to the Prince’s instructions and well received by him; they even received gifts of fruit. Before the Prince came down to the questions, he first warned Father Parrenin that when he was acting as interpreter and mediator he should be very careful that nothing should come to the hands or the eyes of the Emperor that was not strictly according to the rules. Then he asked the Franciscans which one seemed to have had the right ideas and to have acted correctly with regard to the Chinese rites, the Bishop of Conon or Father Matteo Ricci? Fr. Frossoloni answered without committing himself, not wanting to prefer one over the other. The Prince made him twist and turn, and said at last: “This man of yours is not lacking in intelligence; but he cannot make up his mind, and refuses to give a straight answer.”

After the missionary Fathers had gone away after the examination, they gave this warning to the Jesuits. They said they were afraid that certain of the Europeans in Nanjing were causing a disturbance. They alleged that the reason for their fear was a letter written by the Abbate di S. Giorgio to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing in which he said that His Illustriousness was praying to God in order to gain the courage with which he would be able to confess the Holy Name in the presence of the Emperor; from this they deduced that the heart of this prelate was prepared to stir up some sort of trouble.

March 10th. All of them, together with the Most Illustrious Lord [Bishop] were conducted down to the bank of the river, whence they were taken on board the Prince’s boat, and on his orders were taken toward the Emperor’s boat where they immediately presented themselves while the Prince showed their gifts and answers to His Majesty. On his return, the Prince announced that his Father’s orders were that they were to climb up onto the bank and there wait on their knees, but the first in line should be the Lord Bishop and Fr. Castorano, who already had the residence permit. Then His Majesty would see them as he passed by and would give them a certificate on his return. Father Parrenin asked him humbly what the Fathers who lived elsewhere were to do in Linqing for two or more months. And the Prince replied: “Let them do what they like: those who live nearby can easily return, those who live further away can stay here, if that suits them.” Shortly afterwards | he said to the Jesuit in Manchu that he had shown the written applications of each of them to the Emperor, and that he added that Father Frossoloni had given such ambiguous answers that he was sufficiently convinced that in his heart he favoured the opinion of the Bishop of Conon.

### 41. Father Appiani is cleverly outsmarted by a Christian

These events which took place in Linqing were communicated to the Beijing Jesuits; this should be added, that today while the Prince was conducting the above mentioned Fathers towards the imperial boat he told them the following story. Information about Father Appiani came from the Governor of Sichuan to the effect that when he first arrived there he had taught that among the Christians everything should be held in common. After that, he gave a certain sum of money as a loan to one of the Christians. This was lost by the man when his business failed. When Father Appiani summoned him before the local governor to recover the money, he answered that he had been taught by Father Appiani that it had been ordained by the founders of the Christian Law that everything should be held in common, and so, in receiving money from Father Appiani he had received money which belonged to both of them, and consequently it should be judged that the ill-fortune of business should not result in more disadvantage to him than to Father Appiani. And the Viceroy, assuming that that this was what Father Appiani taught, dismissed both parties. When he heard this, the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing confirmed that this was indeed the case, stating that Father Appiani wished to establish in Sichuan the primitive practice of the Church which had been in use in the time of the Apostles, and through this, by a sinister stroke of fortune, had lost his money.

March 20th. Father Vice-Provincial met up with Fathers Bouvet, Parrenin, Jartoux, and Brother Paramino above Huaian. He was carrying some religious objects which he distributed to the Fathers as souvenirs of himself, just in case it should happen that the Emperor became angry towards him and should make some more serious decision about his life. With what feelings the Fathers looked on a man, who in the service of the Holy See and in obedience to the Lord Patriarch was longing for death, could not be described in the words of a dramatist. When from what he told them they first clearly understood the facts about the Patriarch’s decree, issued on 25 January and promulgated on February 7th, and about the declaration that had to be made when applying for the piao, under pain of ecclesiastical penalties, they expressed their feelings in sighs for some time while reflecting inwardly how unavoidably they were forced to co-operate either actively or passively in the destruction of the mission, the crisis of the missionaries, and the difficulties of His Excellency, since inescapably the negotiations with the Prince and the Emperor had to be conducted through them.

<!-- PDF page 367 | book page 357 | manuscript page 546 -->

### 42. The opinion of the Fathers in the Emperor’s retinue, consisting of six items

But since grief without action was getting nowhere, to get down to business, they prayed to God in whom alone was all their trust, and came to these conclusions:

1. They should act in such a way as to choose those plans which at least left some hope in the area of saving the mission. 2. Therefore they must avoid any possibility of alienating the Emperor. 3. It is certain that no one who gave answers to the Emperor following the views of the Bishop of Conon, as prescribed in the decree of the Lord Patriarch, would receive the permit. 4. That it seemed that if it should happen that all the missionaries are obliged to leave China, that they should depart before they would of necessity stir up the animosity of the Emperor. 5. Therefore it must be considered more advisable not to come to request the permit, since it is clear from the obligation rising from the decree that a permit cannot be obtained without that result. 6. It follows further from this that since without the most unseemly offence to the Emperor he cannot be told to his face that it is false that he and the Chinese, by the term Tian, understand | the true God, it would be more advisable for each of us to remain in his own church and there await the disposition of the Divine Mercy. It is this alone which has the power by some miraculous intervention to avert the lamentable destruction of the mission.

Father Vice-Provincial listened to advice such as this and resumed his journey so that he might dissuade other Jesuits, who were now under an obligation due to having taken an oath to observe the Patriarch’s regulation, from rashly presenting themselves to the Emperor, moved by a fancy for martyrdom as such unseemly answers were sure to provoke him.

### 43. The Lord Patriarch sets sail from Nanjing to Guangzhou

Up to this time, with regard to the present business, neither the Fathers who were in the Emperor’s entourage nor Father Vice-Provincial had received any answer from Beijing. And the Lord Patriarch set sail on this very day, March 20th, from Nanjing. For on the 17th, as is clear from his letter to Doctor Borghese which was quoted above, he began to load the boat; on the 18th he went on board; and on the 20th the anchor was raised, as Fr. da Silva described in this way in his account:[^p367-50] “The Lord Patriarch was very anxious to know the response and the outcome of the Fr. Vice-Provincial’s journey to Huaian, but did not take the time to wait for it since he seized the opportunity to embark on his journey to Guangzhou, conducted by the people of the zongdu and the Governor of Guangdong who had already arrived to accompany him, and had got hold of <!-- PDF page 368 | book page 358 | manuscript page 547 --> two large boats or zi chuan[^p368-51] for this purpose … Finally on 18 March the Lord Patriarch embarked, accompanied by 12 Europeans etc.”

March 21st. At the same time, the Emperor halted above Huaian, the abovementioned three Jesuits being with him.

March 22nd. He travelled by land to inspect a site selected for the excavation of a new channel for the river.

March 23rd. He returned, and with the wind preventing him from continuing his journey, he was forced to remain at anchor for two more days.

### 44. The Fathers consider that they should avoid the sight and the hostility of the Emperor

[March] 24th. Father Manoel de Sousa of our Society from the Church of Huaian informed Fathers Bouvet and Parrenin that a letter from the Beijing Jesuits had come to Father Vice-Provincial saying that in the ongoing destruction of the mission, which was being accelerated by the Lord Patriarch’s decree, the lesser of the evils seemed to be that the missionaries should take care not to provoke the Emperor to hostility by coming into his presence, but rather to go off quietly to Macau, there to wait for the outcome of the whole business and for the help of God. They replied that they also counselled the Father to leave Huaian and not to expose himself first to any danger by appearing before the Emperor. Therefore he was to go away either to Yangzhou or to Zhenjiang[^p368-52] and there to join up with Father Vice-Provincial; for this purpose they sent him a sum of money to help him in case he might have had to delay longer.

### 45. Father Manoel de Sousa abandons his church at Huaian and his mission

March 25th. He sent a servant to inform the Fathers that he had made arrangements for his house to meet any eventuality, and that after saying Mass, he consumed the Most Divine Host by way of Viaticum with the intention of leaving after that to go to Father Vice-Provincial to accept death with him. And he left his books, documents, and utensils in the care of a servant to dispose of them as it seemed good in the Lord.

[March] 27th. The Emperor arrived in Huaian. The Fathers went to the church with the intention of offering mass |, but Father Sousa had taken the chalice and vestments with him. So they collected the documents in a box and <!-- PDF page 369 | book page 359 --> taking them with them, returned to the boat; on the following days, the 28th and 29th, those events happened which Father Parrenin related in his letter to Father Kilian dated 29th March.[^p369-53]

### 46. Father Parrenin relates what happened on the journey from March 27th to the 29th

At a place 160 li from Yangzhou. 29 March 1707

Rev. Father,

Father Antoine Thomas wrote at length about the doctor, but said little about Father Superior. The note from Your Reverence left us almost stunned (Father Kilian had written about the dangerous illness of Father Superior on March 21st). I was thinking that nothing could be added to the grief we were feeling over the business of the Lord Patriarch; but shortly afterwards we experienced the contrary. Blessed be God who tests us in this way. He so arranges that everything turns out to His glory and our salvation. The Emperor had been informed through Zhao and Henkama of the Superior’s illness. I have not been able to find out what his answer was, but it would have been of little consolation to me. A courier arrived on March 27th before the boats got underway, and he was sent back again at about the same time as I received the letter and so I was not able, nor afterwards will I be able, to send an answer by the same courier who brought the letter. This evening another came, and if there is any news I will not be able to reply except through a second one because I have to deliver this letter of mine (to the men of the Palace) before a courier arrives, otherwise I will not catch him.

When Your Reverence wrote he would not have been able to know of the letter which I had written on 19 March through the royal courier to Father Superior, nor of the one which I sent by the ordinary courier from Huaian on the 22nd of the same month. It will arrive at about the same time as this one. There you will see what Father Vice-Provincial said to us. He met up with us three days before we arrived in Huaian. Among other things, he told us that he had come, on the advice of the Lord Patriarch, to consult with us as to what could be done at this critical moment when the mission was on the brink of ruin.

### 47. The Fathers decide that none of them will go out of their way to meet the Emperor unless summoned

There is no room here to report all the answers we gave, and which I have written already; I shall only say that taking everything into consideration we were unanimous in the opinion that none (of our missionaries) should go out of their way to meet the Emperor unless <!-- PDF page 370 | book page 360 | manuscript page 548 --> summoned; and that all who do not want to depart voluntarily should remain in their churches in which they will be able to be found as long as they are on the route to be taken by His Majesty.

The Father [Vice-Provincial] left immediately for his church at Zhenjiang to inform all the others of this. On the 22nd of this month, the Emperor travelled by land with a few men including Fathers Bouvet and Jartoux to inspect a place where there was to be excavated a channel for a new canal. He returned on the 23rd with the work still unfinished. He was far from pleased with the mandarins for reasons which are no concern of ours and which I have not the time to report. He wanted to set sail for Huaian at once, but with the winds being contrary, he was delayed for two days. In the intervening time, the two other Fathers and I changed plans for Fr. Manoel de Sousa (who was the missionary in Huaian and who, as a consequence, should have met the Emperor before anyone else). He had already taken the oath.[^p370-54] We wrote that he should go off as quickly as possible as far as Zhenjiang and wait there for more news from us. We adopted this plan for several reasons, but mainly so that the decree of the Lord Patriarch should not become known before we had other news from Beijing, Nanjing, etc.

On the 27th we reached Huaian. The Prince met me in the Palace and immediately asked me whether there was a church there and some Father. I replied in the affirmative. He asked why he did not put in an appearance. I replied that he had gone away towards the South. Charki (that is the name of a mandarin) immediately added: “Doubtlessly he has gone so that | he can present himself along with some others.” But the Prince made this contribution: “Has he run away?” I did not say anything but gave a weak smile. However, he immediately changed the subject. On the same day, when it was already late in the night, the Emperor asked the Prince whether there was a European in this city. The Prince told him what he had learned from me. He asked a second question as to the whereabouts of Duoluo (the Lord Patriarch). He answered that nothing was known about him for certain. Today the Emperor sent us tables furnished with fruit.

Yesterday evening, when I was going in the direction of the King’s boat to find out if there was any news, someone came to meet me and addressed me in the name of Zhang Charki to ask if I had heard anything of the Lord Patriarch. I went straightway to the Prince’s ship, and he admitted me after a long wait. I have my suspicions, he said, because the Fathers are not presenting themselves.

<!-- PDF page 371 | book page 361 -->

Perhaps the Lord Patriarch has summoned them, or they have gone off to bring him here. When we arrive in Yangzhou you are to go to Zhenjiang travelling day and night and immediately inform me what is going on and what news there is of the Patriarch. To us it seemed good that the Prince’s suspicions should grow gradually, to save him from a sudden burst of anger. And so I will go, but when I come back what answer am I to give him? Only the truth, and in the form I wrote about under March 19th.

### 48. Father Monteiro takes down the tablet at grave risk to the mission

We are very worried that the King may learn that Fr. Monteiro has taken down the Jing Tian inscription. It would have been better if he had gone away to Macau, or at least as far as Guangzhou. Now it is still possible for him to do this (go away) lest he causes trouble for others and himself as well. But in the meantime, perhaps, things may smoothed out. All of us are in agreement on this; but he, as I believe, will be brought round to this opinion only with difficulty, and in this way our religion will be destroyed all the more quickly, and indeed root and branch. We cannot foresee what the King is going to do. Let God, in whose hands are the hearts of kings, do what he thinks good, and turn it to the good of religion. I am very much afraid that at the very least he may send everyone to Guangzhou or Macau to take counsel together and come to a better understanding.

If the Emperor were willing to concede, at least to those who previously did not hold the opinion of the Bishop of Conon, permission to remain in their own churches without acting contrary to the imperial decrees, and there to await the return of our men, then there would still be some hope for us. For the Supreme Pontiff after seeing the imperial decrees and becoming better informed will perhaps come to a different decision.

Today we are very worried, and also very fearful as we wait for some news of Rev. Fr. Superior’s health; we consider it superfluous to commend him to Your Reverence’s care and charity which is very well known to us. Greetings to all the Fathers to whose prayers I earnestly commend myself.

Your Reverence’s most humble servant in Christ.

Dominique Parrenin, SJ

### 49. About the truth and authenticity of the acts narrated to this point[^p372-55]

I undertook the writing of this little work[^p372-56] in the month of November 1705, under instructions of Rev. Fr. Filippo Grimaldi, SJ, Visitor of Japan and China. And because I was appointed by him, according to the permission granted by the Supreme Pontiffs and the provisions of our Institute, Procurator and Apostolic Notary, I have noted down from day to day what I myself have done in the matters I have reported, and also what others have done in so far as I have reliable knowledge. I have shown my writings to my superiors, both in stages and then also when collected together in one volume at the one time, to be read, authorized and corrected. Finally, as regards this copy, which has been transcribed by another hand, I have caused it to be read again by Fr. Antoine Thomas and Fr. Ehrenbert Fridelli. And I myself have read it through in its entirety and with my own hand corrected at the foot of the pages the errors which I discovered.

In this document, the petitions, the Emperor’s decrees, the writings that came from the Lord Patriarch, letters, and other such things which are included conform to the originals, which for the most part have been sent to Rome and in some cases are kept in our College in Beijing.

This however is to be noted, that there is some slight difference from the records which were provided from the Palace and those sent by the Emperor Kangxi to the Supreme Pontiff, Clement XI. The cause of this diversity which does not touch on the substance of the matters and the records, is this. The Emperor, when he was about to send the records, first revised them himself and took pains to have some very minor things in some cases expressed more elegantly, in some more clearly, changing the wording but not altering the sense. And so I am attaching here the latest version of these records which were provided from the palace to my own account, as an authenticated complement to the records written by me.

What is recorded as historical fact from the accounts given by others is founded on the trustworthiness of those who made the reports. Afterwards they were collated by four readers who found nothing contrary to the truth in them.

I affix my signature: Father Kilian Stumpf, SJ

Assistant to the Vice-Provincial, Apostolic Notary.

Beijing, 25 October 1707
