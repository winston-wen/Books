February 2nd. From Macau, news was sent to the Beijing Jesuits that, at the urging of the Governor and the Viceroy of Guangdong, Fathers Gonzaga and Hinderer with Brother Vieira would set out as quickly as possible to present themselves before the Emperor as he had ordered. Likewise, taking advantage of this opportunity, it was communicated to them, along with details of the circumstances which confirmed their credibility, that finally in the month of December of the previous year, the letter from Father Thomas dated 20 April 1707 had been delivered to the Lord Patriarch,[^p70-1] as well as a document from Father Antonio da Silva in virtue of which he resigned from the office of Vicar Apostolic of the Province of Nanjing, which had been imposed on him in the month of November in the year 1705 by the Lord Patriarch under an order of obedience.[^p70-2] He resigned in accordance with a pastoral letter from the Primate of the Indies issued under pain of censures. This news from our people in Macau reached Beijing on 29 April 1708 along with the Fathers themselves. But up to today, August the 8th, on which I am writing this, no reply has been received from His Excellency the Lord Patriarch, either to Father Antoine Thomas or to Father da Silva.

### 1 Four Jesuits set out from Macau for Beijing

February 3rd. Fathers Luigi Gonzaga and Roman Hinderer and Brother Miguel Vieira began their journey from Macau to Beijing. Father Jan Bakowski, usually called Bonkouvski, a Pole by nationality but belonging to the Austrian Province, joined them.[^p70-3] He wanted to see if by the goodwill of the mandarins he might be able to penetrate the <!-- PDF page 71 | book page 62 --> court and then, after obtaining a permit, supply the place of a missionary in some church which had lost its pastor.

February 4th. The Most Illustrious Patriarch gave a reply to the Rev. Fr. Bernardino das Chagas, the Commissary of the Franciscans, to five questions proposed by him in legal form, as the reader saw above in an earlier place at the end of January of this year.

### 2 The five heads under which the Emperor ordered Father Tomás Pereira to be questioned

February 5th. While he was at his villa, the Emperor gave orders for the following questions to be put to Fr. Tomás Pereira through the eunuch named Zhang Qilin,[^p71-4] who is the president of the hall called the Yangxin dian.[^p71-5] 1. Did he have any news from Europe? The Father answered that there was none in the immediate past, and that the time when ships arrived had long since passed. 2. Whether in the present year 1708, news would come about Fr. Barros and Beauvollier?[^p71-6] He replied: it would depend on circumstances. Nothing could be affirmed or anticipated with certainty. 3. In what way is it to be expected that they will be well received by the Supreme Pontiff? Reply: As far as one may venture to guess, they will be well received. 4. What is the Pontiff’s disposition with regard to the controversies? Reply: It seems to him that His Holiness is disposed in accordance with the declaration submitted by the Emperor in the year 1700. 5. What is the situation if the Supreme Pontiff should decide to the contrary? Would not your labours and the good works of two hundred years turn out to have been in vain? Would he act in this way without consulting the European kings? Reply: in matters concerning the Divine Law the decision rests with him, and the kings do not meddle in his affairs; the Supreme Pontiff has learned and wise consultors, nevertheless the final decision is in his own hands. In this way Fr. Tomás Pereira made his deposition in the presence of Fr. Antoine Thomas, the Vice Rector, and at his orders a record was preserved in the archives.

February 6th. From Guangzhou Fr. Monteiro sent news that due to the hostility of the mandarins some churches were in danger, and he requested that a si wen[^p72-7] of the Mathematical Tribunal of Beijing should be sent there to protect the churches. His words[^p72-8] were: “Considering the very good effect produced by a wen from the qintian jian[^p72-9] could Your Reverence see if he could send another one to this province in case it should happen that the mandarins or the populace should occupy some churches? The zhifu of Shaojing has been ordered to knock down one belonging to the Augustinians in a village but has delayed doing so.” On this very point the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon and Rev. Fr. Hortiz had written in January, the former to ask for the same wen from the Tribunal; the latter merely to give an account of what was happening. On this same day, the 6th, the four Jesuits whom we had mentioned on the 3rd as having set out from Macau reached Guangzhou.

February 7th. Since again there was much discussion in the letters of different people regarding the excommunication which had been imposed by the Lord Patriarch on the three orders, ecclesiastical, civil, and military of Macau, and also on the Religious in | a certain college in particular and on the missionaries, once again in Beijing too questions were being asked as to why the Most Excellent Lord was proceeding in such a draconian manner. And from the testimony of many it was established that everyone was suspected by him of heretical perversity even if, based on some legal right which was more than just specious, they tried to depart from his regulation in order to save the mission in the present storm. And Father Castner gave fresh testimony about this matter, already mentioned to some extent under the month of May last; here it is recorded in full, as follows.[^p72-10]

### 3 The associates of the Lord Patriarch declare that through his decree a new article of faith has been established

In the name of the Lord Amen. When I, with Father Leopold Liebstain and Brother Franz Stadlin,[^p73-11] had been summoned to Beijing to the court by the Emperor of the Chinese, on the 17th day of September of the year 1707, we were passing through Shaozhou, a city of the province of Guangdong, and we paid a visit in their own house to Fathers Basset,[^p73-12] Lirot, and two other companions, clerics from their Paris Seminary who were there at that time. On the following day, the 18th, late in the morning, those two other clerics came to us on our boat, and when they heard mention of the decree of the Lord Patriarch of Antioch regarding the Chinese Rites, they declared in so many words that through that decree of the Lord Patriarch a new article of faith had been established. And when I objected that on this supposition not even the Supreme Pontiff could change anything in such a decree, they declared that that was actually the case.

I testify to this on the word of a priest, and Father Leopold will be able to give witness to the same for he was present during the whole discussion. Brother Franz, although present, did not understand what they were saying, because we were conversing in Latin.

Beijing. 12 February 1708.

Kaspar Castner, priest of the Society of Jesus.

That this corresponds with the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

### 5 Father Leopold Liebstain testifies that the same was heard by him from the associates of the Lord Patriarch

To these I add the testimony of Father Leopold Liebstain, who, as Father Castner declared above, was present.

In the name of the Lord. Amen.

I, the undersigned, testify on my word as a priest, that when I had been summoned to Beijing, together with Fr. Caspar Castner and Br. Franz Stadlin, we stopped at the city of Shaozhou in the province of Guangdong on Saturday 17 September 1707, and wishing on the Sunday which followed to celebrate the divine rites in the church of the reverend clerics, on the morning of the said Sunday, two of the reverend clerics from the Paris Seminary came to our boat. They were lodging at that time with Rev. Fr. Basset at the house of Rev. Fr. Lirot, who was the missionary at the church I have mentioned. These, in the name of the others, refused us permission to offer Mass (which we had requested on the previous day) saying that we were caught in a minor excommunication because we had been in communication with the Most Rev. Fr. Provincial of Japan, and with Fr. Porquet, a missionary in Guangzhou, who were Religious of the Society of Jesus and had been excommunicated. We countered by saying that no one fell under that censure unless he was in communication with someone who had struck a cleric and whose offence was well-known, or who was excommunicated by an express sentence which had passed against some particular person, and which had been publicly proclaimed or displayed in the recognized place. We knew that neither of these | were the case. And when they rejected this interpretation, we proved it from the Digest of Moral Theology.[^p74-15] In addition, disregarding the false assertion, we showed that anyone bound by a minor excommunication could be <!-- PDF page 75 | book page 66 --> absolved by any priest who had jurisdiction, and they had no grounds for claiming that this had not been granted to us.

Immediately afterwards, changing the subject to the decree of the Lord Patriarch of Antioch issued at Nanjing on 7 February 1707, they declared expressly that the said Most Illustrious Lord through that decree had established a new article of faith. In response, we argued as follows: Therefore, all the more so will the Pope be able to establish new articles of faith. Therefore, it follows that the Pope himself has the power to change anything in the said decree of the Lord Patriarch. They willingly consented to both of these propositions.

I am making this declaration on my word as a priest. At Beijing. 28 February

1708. Leopold Liebstain, of the Society of Jesus, Priest.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

### 6 Fathers Bakowski and Domingos de Britto with four others hasten on to Beijing. Why Fathers Porquet and Capacci remained in Guangzhou

On that same day, February 7th, and also on the 8th, the four above-mentioned Jesuits were despatched from Guangzhou by the Provincial Governor and the Provincial Military Commander on their journey to Beijing.[^p75-16] Permission had been given by the mandarins for Father Bakowski to come to the court at Beijing to receive the permit. But since none of them could speak Chinese, Fr. Domingos de Britto, who up till this time had been superior at the residence of the Provincial of Japan which was in Guangzhou, volunteered to go as interpreter and to support them on the journey. This suggestion was not displeasing to the Provincial Military Commander and he sent him on his way so that he could catch up with those who were already on the journey; through the same undertaking the Father also requested and afterwards received the permit. Two Jesuits remained in that city still lacking a permit, namely Fr. Porquet and Fr. Capacci; the former because he was wanting to achieve his dispatch by other means, the latter because he had many bodily infirmities and was also depressed in spirit and sought to take refuge from the present storm rather than, after receiving the Imperial permit, to have further fears of being excommunicated by the Patriarch.

February 9th. Those four Jesuits with the Brother set out on their journey with an understanding and with information different from that of the people then in Guangzhou. For some of the missionaries, through fear of censure and <!-- PDF page 76 | book page 67 | manuscript page 844 --> for the reasons enumerated by Rev. Fr. Hortiz in January, now considered that they could abandon the mission with a clear conscience. Others, moved by zeal for God and with desire for the salvation of souls, were again on fire with eagerness to receive the permit, certain that they would be recipients of the Lord Patriarch’s ire and abuse and the stripes which his sacred authority enabled him to inflict, but that by doing so they would not be driven out of the vineyard which, with the blessing of the Lord, they had cultivated into old age and even extreme old age.

### 7 How the Father Commissary of the Franciscans acted in Guangzhou

Nevertheless, what is extraordinary is that these men themselves had to endure the opposition of their own superior, the Rev. Fr. Commissary. Since he did not wish to accept the certificate for himself and was ready to accept banishment rather than to expose himself to the censure of the Lord Patriarch, he was demanding the same of his subjects, and was unwilling to give permission to go to Beijing unless they were bound by a new order from the Emperor, under threat of violence. As if there were need for any other pressure than the ipso facto penalty of exile for all who did not obtain the permit, or as if this punishment of banishment was to be considered as not having been seriously intended at the very time at which 24 missionaries as a result of it were already in exile. It was not that the Rev. Fr. Commissary did nothing at all. For as we saw above, in his own name and in that of his people, he consulted with the Lord Patriarch about requesting the permit and offered to go surety for his brothers to the mandarins if they would give them permission to go to the court. But the people in Guangzhou will write of these things to Rome with greater accuracy and they do not concern the Jesuits of Beijing except in so far as will be mentioned below under the 22nd of this month.

On this same February 9th, Fr. Müllener wrote from the city of Nanchang to Fr. Kaspar Castner of his decision to come to Beijing to request the permit. And he asked him, just as also on February 10th he had asked Fr. Parrenin in a letter he sent to intercept him, for instruction on certain matters in the same way as he had made a request at Guangzhou to Father Kilian on November 17th through Father Monteiro. | I consider that both letters should be transcribed, lest the truth about the whole affair of this reverend gentleman should remain somewhat obscured; especially since there is reason to fear lest a person who had the self-confidence to dare to dupe his escorts and the mandarins might also make game of the religious simplicity of the Jesuits if he considered it would be in his own interests or in the interests of religion (as they say).

### 8 A letter from Fr. Johannes Müllener to Fr. Castner

This is what the first letter says: Very Reverend Father. The Peace of Christ be with you.

I received in Guangzhou on 25 October the letter which Your Reverence brought from Europe from my kinsman, Rev. Fr. Martin Lohman. And so, I thank you for the favour you did for me, and I would have been very happy had I been able to greet in Guangzhou Your Reverence yourself together with your other companions from Germany who arrived at the same time. But what I was greatly looking for I hope will come about shortly at the Court, towards which I will make my journey immediately on the removal of the obstacles which are preventing me. These also will shortly be removed with the help of Rev. Fathers Simões and Foucquet, whom I met in this church of Nanchang fu in the province of Jiangxi.[^p77-17] They are caring for me with all possible kindness and are offering me a helping hand. What has happened is that the mandarins of the zongdu,[^p77-18] the fuyuan,[^p77-19] etc. of the province of Guangdong are sending me back accompanied by two escorts, and with a letter, to the church and city of Chongqing fu in the province of Sichuan. Since, however, on the way and here also I have heard that a buwen has arrived from the court giving freedom to Europeans to have access to the court, I am hoping that with the intervention of the above-mentioned Rev. Fathers I shall escape the hands of the escort and avoid that tiresome journey to the province of Sichuan and so come straight to the Court.[^p77-20]

But since I do not know whether the present situation at the Court will cause me trouble or favour me, I am therefore asking Your Paternity to deign to instruct me as to what I can do and should do, now and on my arrival. In addition, because I have heard from the Rev. Fathers Simões and Foucquet of the Society of Jesus that Rev. Fr. Appiani my colleague has arrived in Beijing and that the Rev. Fathers living at the Court are making great efforts to secure his freedom, I should commend this business very strongly to Your Reverence asking you to extend a helping hand to him.

### 9 He asks to be informed about various matters

God will certainly not fail to reward generously this charity of yours. In the meantime, I am eagerly awaiting a response from Your Paternity so that I may finally finish off his business of mine. This morning, February 9th, I personally approached the buzheng shi[^p78-21] who is very friendly towards Europeans and I explained my situation clearly to him. But just as I was convinced that the matter had been settled since I had taken up in my defence that new buwen which recently arrived in favour of religion, take note that the mandarin gave this answer: This new buwen is not from the Emperor nor does it encourage Europeans to come to the court, but it came only from a certain zongdu at the urging of Father Grimaldi.[^p78-22] And since that wenshu or letter which those two escorts of mine are bearing and which they exchange with the police in all the xian[^p78-23] was made also on an order from the zongdu and fuyuan of the province of Guangdong, for these reasons it is not fitting to change anything in it. But it is necessary for me to set out in the same way as I came, accompanied, that is, by the two escorts as far as the province of Sichuan and the city of Chongqing fu, which is a journey of at least two months from here. If in the meantime Your Reverence with the other Fathers would be able to arrange that the buwen which is for the provinces of Zhejiang and Fujian should also be recognized in the province of Sichuan and at the same time to add some recommendations for the fuyuan of the same province or for the daoye[^p78-24] of our city, whose name is Long Hongyi[^p78-25] and is under the red <!-- PDF page 79 | book page 70 | manuscript page 845 --> banner, commonly called the hongqi xia,[^p79-26] it would be of benefit to this mission which certainly is suffering acutely in many ways. Moreover, I have heard that the churches will be taken over by the mandarins and set aside as gong guan.[^p79-27] But I do not know whether they have yet been taken over. I am closing now and greet very warmly Rev. Fr. Grimaldi, Antoine Thomas, and the others living at the Court, and after I have kissed the anointed hands of the Reverend Fathers, I commend myself to their holy sacrifices.

Your Reverend Paternity’s most humble and obedient servant,

Johannes Müllener,[^p79-28] an unworthy priest of the Congregation of the Mission.

Gan chang fu.[^p79-29] | 9 February 1708.

### 10 The second letter from the same Johannes Müllener

The second letter, addressed to Fr. Parrenin, goes as follows:[^p79-31] Very Rev. Father. The Peace of Christ be with you.

When I reached this city of Nanchang fu, I found, together with Rev. Fr. Simões, Rev. Fr. Foucquet who had returned from the court.[^p79-32] On account of <!-- PDF page 80 | book page 71 --> a former friendship which we enjoyed on the Coromandel Coast when both of us were trying to reach China, he received me most kindly, and after a long and friendly conversation he gave me a letter to read, one which had been brought from the court on that same day. From it I was well informed of the zeal and love with which Your Paternity was endeavouring to come to the aid of Rev. Fr. Appiani, my colleague who is still under arrest. This was very good news for me, both because I heard that Rev. Fr. Appiani, who I thought was already dead, was still alive, then also because I realized that Your Rev. Paternity along with his colleagues were making great efforts to secure his freedom and release. And if they achieve this, they will be highly esteemed among men on account of their great reputation for charity which will not be without its reward from God.

Meanwhile I have arrived at this city on February 2nd, having been sent by the mandarins, namely the zongdu and fuyuan of the province of Guangdong in the company of two escorts who are to conduct me back to Chongqing fu in the province of Sichuan to my own church. But because while on my journey I heard about a buwen by which freedom is given to the missionaries to come to the Court in order to present themselves to the Emperor, I am hoping that with the assistance of Rev. Frs. Foucquet and Simões I may escape from the hands of these escorts and so avoid that tiresome journey as far as Sichuan, and be able to proceed directly to the Court. However, since I do not know whether the present situation at the Court will cause trouble or will favour my coming, therefore let me dare to make a request to Your Very Rev. Paternity to deign to inform me of this in a brief reply, and at the same time to give me some instruction as to what I can and ought to do on my arrival.

Yesterday, February 9th, in the morning, I went to visit the buzheng shi, who is very friendly to Europeans, and I explained my problem to him clearly but to no effect, for he replied that the buwen which I had referred to in support of my argument did not come from the Emperor, nor did it urge Europeans to come to the court, but was only something that came from a certain zongdu[^p80-33] and which had been prepared on the insistence of Father Grimaldi.

And since my wenshu,[^p80-34] which those two escorts of mine are bearing and which they exchange with the police from one xian to the next xian, has been <!-- PDF page 81 | book page 72 | manuscript page 846 --> prepared on the orders of the zongdu and fuyuan of the province of Guangdong, it is therefore not advisable to change anything; rather it is necessary to set out, in the same way as I arrived here, as far as the Sichuan Chongqing fu, which is a journey of a least of two months from here.

If in the meantime Your Reverence could arrange that the buwen which is for the provinces of Zhejiang and Fujian would also be accepted in the province of Sichuan and at the same time to attach recommendations for the fuyuan of the same province or for the daoye of our city named Long Hongyi, who is under the red banner commonly called the Hongqi, it would be to the advantage of this mission which certainly is experiencing exceedingly great suffering. ### 11 Comments on this letter

It is to be noted: 1. That what is said here by Fr. Müllener and others to be a buwen is not a buwen but a siwen; that name through lack of exactness and by extension has been adopted by them. Moreover, it was stated at length with regard to it in a document of the Mathematical Tribunal in October 1707 that nothing could be drawn from it which would imply that through it Europeans are given permission to go to the Court, much less that they on the strength of it could be forced to come. And the buzheng shi or Prefect of the Treasury explained this correctly to Father Müllener.

2. Father Müllener compounds his mistake by further asserting that it was said to have been composed by a certain zongdu, the Supreme | Military Prefect,[^p81-36] at the insistence of Father Grimaldi. For if it came from the court, how <!-- PDF page 82 | book page 73 --> was it composed in a province by some zongdu? However, it was composed by the Mathematical Tribunal and at the insistence of Father Grimaldi, it was received by the zongdu in Jiangnan who in turn showed it to the mandarins in Jiangxi, etc. who were under his control, and so caused it to be respected by them.

3. Father Müllener, in Nanchang fu, not unwisely grasped the opportunity to go directly to the Court, but at the same time he did not reveal the fact that he had decided to go from Guangzhou to Beijing on the advice of the Lord Patriarch and his supporters; for this was not when the idea first came into his head. But with the help of Fathers Simões and Foucquet he wanted to be freed from his escorts, who were not going to help him to go to the court. And the two Fathers in the simplicity and honesty of their hearts did everything for him that they could. Father Simões personally paid a visit to the mandarins who were not fully acquainted with the circumstances (for only Fr. Müllener fully understood it). They even granted what was being requested until it came to the order of the governor and of the zongdu of Guangdong.[^p82-37] Then they took a step back, saying they had no authority to go against them and that government procedures must be observed whereby, once someone had been entrusted to an escort to be conducted somewhere, the officials who encountered him on the way were to cause him to be taken on in the same way until that man had travelled from his starting point to his goal. And the records of every one of the zhixian as to how he had been passed on from hand to hand according to official procedures were to be preserved intact.[^p82-38]

4. Father Müllener, even though he was conducted by an escort, was not treated with disrespect. For he took four servants with him and abundant baggage, and with the help of the Jesuits he was admitted to discussions with the mandarins. Nor was he mistreated on the way as you may gather from that he was permitted by the escort to remain in Nanchang from February 2nd to the 10th and beyond that.

### 12 Father Müllener’s theology is not consistent within itself

5. It had not passed unnoticed that this priest, neither in Nanchang nor in Huangzhou nor in Wuchang fu etc., where he found Jesuit missionaries, had made his confession <!-- PDF page 83 | book page 74 | manuscript page 847 --> after a journey of two months.[^p83-39] And yet he celebrated Mass, as is established from the letters of different persons; but when Father Hervieu, a Jesuit, wished to make his confession to him when he was passing through, he would not admit him to the sacrament alleging as the reason that he held him to be excommunicated because he disobeyed the Patriarch’s regulation. Nevertheless, he had remained for ten days with the Jesuits in Nanchang, eating and drinking what they had, without the worm of excommunication gnawing at his inner vitals. Something similar happened in Shaozhou when the Rev. Frs. Basset, Lirot, Balluère, and Henri were there. When Father Castner and Father Liebstain had freely brought them with charity and much labour on their part and danger, the annual contribution of money from the Paris Seminary, they accepted it courteously but declared it was their duty to say that the Fathers were regarded by them as excommunicated and they refused them permission to say Mass in their church on the following day and even to hear Mass. Who could understand this strict theology? They communicate with the Jesuits while they are receiving money or food; they spurn them after they have accepted these things. They have no scruple about putting in their treasure-chest[^p83-40] the silver which was delivered by excommunicated hands, nor in taking food offered free of charge by the Jesuits, for they know that what goes into the stomach or is put into the purse does not contaminate the soul.

6. The Jesuits of Beijing had not replied up until now to the letter sent by Father Monteiro from Guangzhou on 17 November 1707, nor to this one sent from Nanchang by Fr. Müllener. Not to the former one, because the request that was made was couched in the most general terms without being carefully prepared; namely that it was to be explained to Fr. Müllener what were the wishes of the Emperor with regard to his person in particular, that is whether he ought to come to the court or to leave China—as if the Emperor of China would know anything at all about Father Müllener, or as if anything about him could be referred to the Emperor in such circumstances! Did he expect us to refer to the merits of his colleague Fr. Appiani, who had just been imprisoned by the Emperor in the Palace? | Fr. Müllener knew well enough what the Emperor wanted done by everyone and had practical experience at Guangzhou of so many who had been banished etc. Why would he still want to send the Jesuits to the Emperor with an unusual question of that kind? If the Beijing Jesuits had proposed something like that to the Emperor, it would have done nothing for

<!-- PDF page 84 | book page 75 -->

Father Appiani except that both the latter and the former could rightly have had cause for complaint about us.

Nor did we reply to the second letter, for as a rule, letters take almost a month to come from Nanchang to Beijing almost every month, and so what this Father wrote on February 10th could be in Beijing by March 10th. But where Father Müllener would be then, he did not state, nor was it known by what route he would travel. Above all though, the later part of the letter cancelled the earlier part by saying that now he was not permitted to go directly to the court.

7. Fr. Müllener confirmed the existence of a rumour which, as we mentioned under the previous year, had been circulating around Guangzhou regarding Fr. Appiani, whom they regarded there not so much as already dead but destined for death by the hand of Kangxi. The foundation for this was better known to them than to us. Granted however, that Father Müllener was convinced that his colleague was either dead or condemned to death with the knowledge and consent of the Emperor, what itch to come, I ask you, took hold of the man? Finally in April, it will be revealed more clearly from the testimony of the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing what Fr. Müllener was turning over in his mind when he made great efforts to come to Beijing, even when he was a fugitive. By relating the facts of the case, I will explain his motives.

February 11th. Shortly before eleven o’clock in the morning Fr. Appiani sent a note to ask for books of meditations for Lent, adding that he had something to communicate to us relating to the sacred person of the Lord Patriarch, even though in fact he knew that we regarded him as a servant of the Holy See, and hence beyond reproach. However, I have recorded his statement under the day it was nominally written and not here because today nothing else serious enough happened to need to be noted.

### 13 The concern which the Jesuits felt for Father Appiani

Father Kilian had made arrangements with one of his students, a Christian whose name was Ignatius Cui, so that when the man was working his shift in the glass factory either in the daytime or during the night, he was to take the opportunity to speak to Father Appiani and to ask whether he needed anything, or if he could be helped in any way.[^p84-41] He asked for only a few things which were small, and which were provided. But, on this particular night, the priest instructed the young man who was twenty-four years old not to be remiss in his duty of Christian <!-- PDF page 85 | book page 76 | manuscript page 848 --> charity, and he on the following day, February 12th, in the morning said to the Jesuit that Father Appiani had greeted him in the Chinese fashion, inquiring after his health and praising his charity in providing what was necessary. He asked what the future held for him, whether the Jesuits felt sorry for him, and whether they were able to make petitions on bended knee on his behalf before the Prince and the Emperor so that he could be released, or at least be allowed to transfer to our residence. ### 14 Some Muscovites come to Beijing and are visited by the Jesuits

On February 13th and the following day, Father Parrenin and Father Kilian, at the orders of the Crown Prince, paid a visit with his chamberlain to the Muscovites who <!-- PDF page 86 | book page 77 --> had arrived not long before. He was very concerned for the spiritual welfare of the Muscovites who lived in Beijing. He encouraged them to go to confession and to be faithful to the other practices of religion. ### 19 A letter from the Bishop of Ascalon to the Lord Patriarch

### 20 On what conditions may the permit be granted to the missionaries so that they can remain in China

Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lord: While the edict regarding the Chinese Rites which emanated from Your Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship on 25 January 1707 remains in force, it would appear that for Chinese missionaries to present themselves before the Manchu-Chinese Emperor or his delegated judges to obtain the permit to remain in China requires very great boldness combined with serious imprudence. For such a permit is definitely not granted (whatever a false rumour without foundation may imply) except under the condition, either expressed or tacit, of not observing the rules of the said edict, at least until Our Most Holy Father, after hearing the information sent to him by the Emperor himself, shall have decided otherwise. Nevertheless, <!-- PDF page 94 | book page 85 | manuscript page 852 --> since Your Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship has urged both orally and in writing some of the Spanish missionaries to present themselves to the Emperor to request the said permit, and moreover has boldly written the same to Alvaro de Benavente, the Bishop of Ascalon and Vicar Apostolic of the province of Jiangxi, saying that you are sure that he will not leave China because of the love he should feel towards his province, for these reasons the said Bishop, who is a debtor to everyone, considers that the case should be brought up for examination again. Indeed it is his desire, not only to carry out the commands of Your Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship but also to remain faithful to your counsels while maintaining his freedom of conscience.

For it is true moreover that on the one hand it is obviously and undoubtedly certain from various decrees issued by the Emperor and from several witnesses beyond reproach that the penalty of capital punishment is imposed on those who teach against the rites in question, and on the other hand, in the beforementioned regulation there are some things so difficult to understand that they are not perfectly intelligible unless Your Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship provides a clearer explanation. And so it is necessary | to consult you to avoid going ahead blindly in a matter of such great moment; and, even more, in the present crisis when we have become a spectacle for the whole Christian world, it is not right to proceed without due caution in a matter on which, speaking in human terms, the salvation of the other world, that of the gentiles, depends.

In the present situation this is even more important, since for a long time now the Emperor has not been unaware of the dignity and office of a bishop, and, what is even more perilous, he is perfectly well aware that before the publication of the edict of Your Most Excellent and Most Reverend Lordship he was holding and defending in writing before the Apostolic See, or rather had held and had defended, that his declarations regarding the Chinese rites were correct and conformable with the Chinese canonical books. A prince, and one so mighty and so proud, cannot but be amazed and saddened that a bishop should spurn his opinion and that of experts on the authority of one man who is totally unfamiliar with Chinese affairs.

And in fact that bishop, when questioned about changing his opinion, can give no clear reason for maintaining it, except one founded on the authority of the said edict and of a decree of the Apostolic See on which it is said to rest. On account of this, the obligation and necessity of obtaining a sound and correct understanding of the implications of such a decree is all the more pressing for the bishop, otherwise he lays himself open to the obvious danger of lying to the grave disgrace of the whole church of China and of the Apostolic See—not to mention the loss of his own head.

<!-- PDF page 95 | book page 86 -->

Therefore, disregarding the other regulations prescribed in the above-mentioned edict because they do not come into question in so far as they do not pertain to the rites which are in dispute, which have provoked the persecution and involve the threat of capital punishment, the principal difficulty concerns only these three propositions contained in the edict, namely:

### 21 The three queries or doubts proposed to the Lord Patriarch

1. The rites called ji for Confucius and the deceased ancestors cannot licitly be celebrated by us or permitted for followers of the Divine Law.

2. The use of the tablets of the deceased ancestors according to Chinese custom cannot be lawfully permitted.

3. Shangdi or Tian are not the God of Christians.

From these arise the following questions: Firstly, it is mentioned in the aforementioned edict that a decree was issued on 20 November 1704. In that, were the three above propositions or their equivalent defined ex cathedra by His Holiness Our Lord Pope Clement xi, thereby binding the Church of God with that infallibility which he possesses, so that to assert or to maintain in practice the opposite to each individual one of them is in every case an error against the Catholic Faith?

In addition, since the Emperor or his delegates may not only question the missionaries about the practices which are forbidden by the Christian religion but also about those which it allows in the said rites, it is not desirable to hurt his feelings by remaining silent about those aspects which are capable of conciliating him and making him well-disposed, and only to speak about the matters which provoke him to opposition against the Divine Law and which are prohibited in the above-mentioned edict of Your Most Illustrious and Most Rev. Lordship. I refer to such things as are in the decree of Pope Alexander vii of happy memory, issued in the year 1656, which would have the effect mentioned.

And so I put the second question: Is that decree of Alexander vii clearly and expressly revoked through the above-mentioned decision of the 20 November 1704 with regard to those things which it permits in the cult of Confucius and the deceased ancestors?

To these questions the same Bishop of Ascalon humbly but with insistence entreats Your Most Illustrious and Most Rev. Lordship to deign to give him an answer as soon as possible and to fully inform him and the other missionaries in the present and pressing crisis, so that they will be able to proceed absolutely free from error in a matter of such great moment.

### 24 Six Franciscans request from the Viceroy of Guangzhou permission to travel to Beijing to receive the Piao

Very Rev. Fr. Manuel Osório, On 22 February of this year 1708 the undersigned Religious of St. Francis, missionaries in China, offered a petition at the office of the Governor of Guangzhou, asking his permission to go to Beijing to present ourselves to request the Emperor to grant us a permit to remain in China and not be expelled from the kingdom for lack of it, according to the order which the same Emperor has issued.[^p99-77] We dispatched our petition to receive the necessary approval of the Viceroy through the ancha shi, and this mandarin handed it over to his inferior officers;[^p99-78] just when it was about to be sent on there came advice from the mandarins saying that in order for this permission to be granted, approval of our persons was necessary from a Xiyang ren (a European) called Li Guozheng, without whose approbation permission would not be granted.[^p99-79] This was the order that had come from Beijing sent by the eldest son of the Emperor, the Da Wang ye who had been appointed by the Emperor as judge to examine the European missionaries who were preaching in his kingdom, interrogating each of them (before giving them the permit) whether they were determined to follow the practices which the first missionaries, the founders of the mission, Rev. Fr. Matteo Ricci and others who had followed his teaching, had established.

This corresponds with the text of an authenticated transcript, for the original is retained in the College of Macau.[^p99-80] I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary

I have said that this petition presented by the six Franciscans seems to be extraordinary, and that the reply from the mandarins is even more extraordinary. <!-- PDF page 100 | book page 91 | manuscript page 855 --> For neither were the Jesuits able to understand why and how the said six Religious decided to made a such a petition, nor were the Rev. Franciscan Fathers able to understand why they were ordered to obtain a reference, or as they call it here, approval, from a member of our Society, without which they were not to receive permission from the mandarins to take themselves to Beijing to present themselves to the Emperor there in order to receive the certificate. And the Jesuits too were amazed at this and both parties had more than a few reasons for their amazement.

1. Some time earlier, these Rev. Fathers had received a report from their Brothers of the province of Shandong, | and the writer of the report, Fr. Antonio Frusinoni, had confirmed it in their presence. And already they had heard of the benign interpretation given by the Bishop of Beijing of his own promise and the promise which the Franciscans who were living in the province of Shandong had given in the presence of His Majesty. However, they were not satisfied with this, so that then, while accepting as fact what they were told had been done by those already mentioned, they sought permission from the mandarins to go and request the permit.

But afterwards, disregarding the report from their own brothers in Shandong as if it did not exist, on two occasions and in due form they put the question to the Lord Patriarch whether they could go to Beijing to obtain permission from the Emperor to remain in China without the stigma of great imprudence and rashness. And they received no definite answer. Finally, they were eager to begin the journey and they presented a written petition to the Viceroy asking not to be hindered, in full knowledge that in Beijing where they were going, candidates for the permit would be questioned as to whether they were prepared to follow the former practices according to the Emperor’s declaration, against which the decree of the Patriarch commands them to bear public witness.

They expressed it this way in their letter already cited: An order to this effect has come from Beijing, sent by the firstborn son of the Emperor, the Great Prince, who is the judge appointed by the Emperor to examine European missionaries who are preaching in his kingdom by questioning them individually before he hands over the permit, as to whether they have determined to follow the practices established by the first founders of the mission, Fr. Matteo Ricci and others who were his followers. Certainly, it is extraordinary that they were not afraid of the censures of the Lord Patriarch, were not affected by the example of their own Brothers, and were not forbidden to engage in such a risky undertaking by their own commissary in the present situation, and when he is avoiding a similar fate for himself.

### 25 A rumour that a Jesuit has been put in charge of giving approval for those who seek a permit

2. No one could understand what advantage there would be for the Jesuits in this arrangement, that one of them by examination would select those suitable or not suitable to receive the permit, or what in the circumstances comes to the same thing, to remain in China. If the Jesuits had thought it conferred any benefit on themselves, they would have been able to organize it long before, when all was going well and without the necessity of bringing down on themselves the odium that would follow from it. Why then would they do this at this time when the mission is in ruins, and when they themselves in fear and trembling are waiting for the reply from the Holy Pontiff which, if it is not favourable to the declarations of the Emperor, will mean that they also, without hope of redress, will be banished with all the others. It did not seem credible that the Jesuits would have arranged this to attract the attention, the pens, and the censures of the Patriarch’s party without any advantage for themselves. Nevertheless, credence had to be given to the mandarins who were saying that there was such an order from the Court. Hence firstly the amazement of everybody, and then some broke out into criticisms as if to say that with considerable inconvenience to Religious the Jesuits were laying claim to some sort of higher authority. Others were delighted and sang paeans as now having hold of definite reasons for condemning the Jesuits to be cast out into the outer darkness for the worst possible crime.

We who were far away writing from Beijing could only with difficultly come to a vivid realization of the grief of some and the delight of others at such an unusual response from the mandarins; only through letters by which some have communicated their joy to their friends did we know that it has been said openly in Guangzhou by the Patriarch’s party that this happened as the result of a miracle, so that the Lord Patriarch is able to claim a solid reason for fulminating his rightful revenge on the Beijing Jesuits. And, in fact, if an examination of this kind had been arranged by the Beijing Jesuits or any Jesuits at all, they would certainly have made themselves liable to punishment both by the Pope’s representative and the Holy See. In what way will this determination be tested? It is held with certainty that the order was sent to Guangzhou. The author of the order is known, but the occasion or cause of the order is not known. But finally in May the outcome would be known. May I leave it to the reader to judge for himself when he sees what is there. |
