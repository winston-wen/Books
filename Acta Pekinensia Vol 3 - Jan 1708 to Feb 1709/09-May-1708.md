This month did not produce many matters, but rather a diversity of issues requiring greater discernment; therefore, those concerning Beijing will be dealt with first, and then those concerning Guangzhou will be reported.

### 1 The Fathers brought from Guangzhou are presented at the villa of the Emperor

May 1st. The four Fathers and Brother Vieira, who had been brought from Guangzhou, attended the villa of the Emperor, but nothing was done concerning them, the Prince being occupied in the city about some matter of which I know nothing.

May 2nd. They returned to the same place, they were presented, and on the following days Fr. Gonzaga with Frs. Parrenin and Castner were occupied in setting some instruments in order and in sketching out a brief topography. At first the Prince showed himself inclined to keep Fr. Gonzaga, and, since he had not appointed Fr. Hinderer to the topographical project, there was a hope that this Father would be released for the work of the mission as he himself wished and had requested.

On about May 5th a petition was made for the first time that he be permitted to leave the court and go elsewhere. Moreover, about the same time it happened that, as indicated earlier in a letter of 26 April from our men, and now through the direct testimony and request of Fr. De Britto,[^p191-1] a remedy was sought for the problem of our church in Xinhui xian,[^p191-2] which had been converted by the mandarins into a school and had the sacred images taken away. As Fr. Monteiro explained in a letter of 30 May: “In regard to the matter of the Church of Xinhui nothing has succeeded. After Fr. Osório had made his representation they placed there a tablet of Confucius and an idol called either Lao Jun[^p191-3] or Liu Shen, etc.”[^p191-4] We proposed points that might move the Emperor, and, among others, that we had originally established that church to keep in good order <!-- PDF page 192 | book page 183 --> the tomb of St. Francis Xavier, which was situated in a deserted place where none of our Fathers could live, and it seemed that there was only one way of taking care of this, namely that some one of our Fathers should stay in the neighbouring town of Xinhui xian, so as to guard the place, under the protection of the mandarins. This idea was acceptable to the Prince, and he promised to bring it to the attention of the Emperor; and when he asked who had previously taken care of the tomb and the church since Father de Britto was then absent from the town, Father Castner who was present replied that the place had previously been entrusted to his care (as, in fact, it had been thus entrusted for some time, before this Father went to Europe).[^p192-5] The prince told all of this to his Father.

### 2 The Emperor orders that the church in Xinhui be restored to the Fathers

And His Majesty decreed that the church be restored, adding the reason that it is near the tomb of a saintly xian man, who deserved to be defended against any injury. For this purpose he designated, of his own accord, Fr. Roman Hinderer as a companion of Fr. Castner in the journey, and his disciple in scientific matters, and now his successor in this concern. See a copy of this royal decree under 16th May, or at the end of the same month.

The said decision of the Emperor was certainly full of benevolence. In one respect however it was extraordinary, and in two respects not completely adequate. It was extraordinary in that the Emperor in his decree accepted for the holy Xavier the title of “saint,” and still more extraordinary in that the Prince, as if constituted a judge in matters concerning the Europeans, twice ordered that the title be omitted, and yet, when he was correcting with his own hand the text of the decree written out by the secretary, he allowed this word to escape his attention, badly erased as it had previously been by his own brush.[^p192-6]

### 3 The prudent goodwill of the Emperor towards the Europeans

This benefit was not adequate, since it did not by a single word touch upon the invasion by the mandarin, and still less did the Emperor reprehend him, and since by the plenitude of his power he placed Fr. Roman Hinderer in that place, when someone from Guangzhou could easily supply there and Fr. Roman could be occupied more fruitfully elsewhere. Firstly, we worked upon the Emperor in patient silence, knowing him from of old, to see whether perhaps he wished to favour the Europeans, but not to appear to favour them by showing little esteem for his own people, or causing them damage. Thus, he often said to us that it would not promote our cause if Chinese people were punished on our account, no matter how far they were out of line, since the punishment of one Chinese person would arouse the hatred of thousands against the Europeans; and this would be more damaging to us than punishment of the offender which, however deserved, would not promote our cause. | Secondly, we tried to make an application on about May 10th, pointing out to the Prince how much we needed Fr. Roman elsewhere, and how, without difficulty, one of the invalids could be appointed guardian of the tomb. To this he replied first that these entreaties should first be proposed by all of us together.

### 4 The Prince receives the Fathers arrogantly

May 11th. We all came and knelt before him; he ridiculed us and sent us away, telling us to be content with the previous decision of his Father.

May 15th and 16th. Residence permits were obtained for Fathers de Britto and Bakowski without any further formality than that when they were interrogated, they replied in the same way that the others had done, and used the same form of written petition that the others had presented except that Father Bakowski, having only recently come to China, was not able to write that in the past he had followed the practice of Matteo Ricci.

May 17th. Frs. De Britto, Bakowski, and Hinderer left Beijing for Huguang, and from there they turned toward Guilin, where Father Bakowski was to set up a mission. Fr. De Britto was to remain with him for some months, so that after he had been instructed somewhat in Chinese matters, he could return to Guangzhou and Fr. Hinderer could come to take possession of the church in Xinhui, which was to be restored, as well as taking the responsibility of caring for the tomb of St. Xavier.

May 18th. Letters arrived from the Guangzhou Fathers, which had been written in February and the beginning of March, and which we already discussed each in its place. It was regrettable that they had not been brought earlier, at least those which contained more definite news of the destruction of the <!-- PDF page 194 | book page 185 --> church of the Most Reverend Augustinian Fathers.[^p194-7] If at the appropriate time we had had grounds for bringing this matter forward, we could have done so in the presence of the Emperor on the occasion when we brought to his attention the matter of our own church in Xinhui; but the Most Reverend Augustinian Fathers had not written until March 10th, and the letter was delivered at the end of May, as will be said in due course.

May 21st. In the Villa, the Most Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial, Fr. Parrenin, and Fr. Kilian, having entered the gardens, implored the Prince that, in favour of the missionaries who had already been graciously supplied with permits, he would deign to restrain the vexatious activities of the officials. These were not decreasing as time went on, but increasing, as we had learnt from the letters written to us and discussed under April. The Prince said he would look at the decree that had previously been drawn up but twice on the same day the mandarin Zhang Charki,[^p194-8] when we were present, prevented its being openly produced. However, our fortunes changed, and later the Prince asked whether there was any other business remaining, and as there was none, the Secretary brought out the said decree.

### 5 And shows that his attitude is unfriendly towards them

The Prince read it, and, in a rage, snatched up a brush, crossing out some things, adding others, and this in a way that was very bad for our holy religion. For in the end he caused to be renewed the shameful decrees directed against five Jesuits and others, and what was worst of all, expressing himself in the Manchu language which generally leaves no room for ambiguity, he was guilty of a harmful ambiguity when speaking of those five for, although according to the Emperor’s decree of the previous year published in Yangzhou, it was said that, for the present, the (Christian) Law could not be preached by them (the five exiles), the Prince wrote that, for the present, their Law could not be preached. These words could have had very adverse consequences in the hands of hostile officials if they understood them in an all-embracing sense and extended their meaning to all Europeans, as if it were decreed by royal edict that, for the present, until the replies come from Rome, the Christian Law could not be preached by any of the Europeans, or even by any of the Chinese. The Fathers then present took <!-- PDF page 195 | book page 186 | manuscript page 907 --> this badly, but since the Prince had written this boldly with his own hand, it was not a suitable time to respond; they judged it sufficient to wait for a more suitable occasion on the following day, and in the meantime to pray earnestly to God that he might graciously soften the heart of the Prince and instruct him.

### 6 Three of our Fathers are sent by the Emperor to Tartary

Not long afterwards the Prince went up to the Emperor. The latter began to speak about the Europeans, and ordered that three of them be sent to | Tartary, so that they might make a map of the whole area of that land according to the norms of geography. He wished this whole work to be done with public authority, and through the accustomed ministries. Hence first of all the commission was made known to the Ministry of Rites, that this ministry might advise the Bureau of Mathematics, which was subject to it, concerning the choice from its staff of men suitable for the task of conducting this expedition with the Europeans. Nothing of this was said by the Prince to any of our Fathers on this day, or for some days subsequently, perhaps because he was little pleased that some honour in state affairs should seem to accrue to us. However, this very silence of the Prince had the good result that both bodies, which had no foreknowledge either of the number or the names of Europeans skilled in geography, were compelled to seek advice repeatedly both from us and others and finally from the Emperor, who at the beginning of June designated Fathers Joachim Bouvet, Jean Régis, and Pierre Jartoux.[^p195-9]

May 24th. Father Kilian replied to the letter of the Rev. Fr. Friar Buonaventura da Roma, which had arrived in Beijing on the May 18th, concerning an appeal for help, namely that he might be permitted to leave Guangzhou and go to the province of Jiangxi. His reply was as follows:

### 7 Letter of Fr. Kilian to Father Buonaventura

I have understood what Your Very Reverend Paternity wrote to Fr. Gonzaga. Indeed, I appreciated as well as understood how appropriate it was that those things were sent in a letter to the said Father, and I hope that I will understand even more through the same means. I sympathise with Your Paternity, and further sympathise with the souls who are fearful that Your Paternity’s spiritual ministry will, as it were, suffer <!-- PDF page 196 | book page 187 --> shipwreck in the storm. My own obligation, and that of all the Fathers, is to attempt, in the best way possible, to bring it about that the Governor of Guangzhou[^p196-10] does not continue in this most hostile fashion to obstruct your work of providing spiritual help to so many souls. We desire with our whole heart that this may be achieved, and we will work with all our strength to bring it about. But if a good result is delayed in coming, this is to be ascribed, not to us, but to the evil times. Almost daily, many requests present themselves (to be brought before the Emperor); however, there is no daily access to him to make those requests.

### 8 The Prince has been appointed by the Emperor to hear cases involving Europeans

At the same time that Fr. De Britto proposed action here in the matter of the restoration to God of the Church in Xinhui xian, which had been taken by the mandarin as property of the crown, there arrived in Beijing the lamentations of the Fathers in Huguang concerning the troubles which they were there enduring because of the ill-conceived plan of Fr. Müllener to either flee or go on a journey. Also, there was the case of Fr. Baborier sent back to his zongdu by order of the Libu etc. Is there any need to give the details? The difficulty in this is that the Emperor has quite definitely appointed the Prince as the one to hear the cases involving Europeans, and he either does not receive them, or having received them does not refer them on. There are so many misfortunes which the missionaries, even those equipped with a royal piao, are compelled by the mandarins to endure, which we have presented to him in writing; yet he has discussed with the Emperor only the alienation of the church in Xinhui xian, perpetrated by the magistrate, since he knew that a little later Fr. De Britto with others were to be admitted into the presence of His Majesty, where, given the opportunity, it could perhaps happen that he would bring this complaint directly to the Emperor. The Prince remained silent about the other matters, knowing that our access to His Majesty had been cut off. I would like Your Reverend Paternity to note how charmingly some write, both from Macau and from Guangzhou, to those who are experiencing this, praising the kindness of the Emperor and telling them that they are inundated with favours and exaggerate the Emperor’s anger. If they believe that we in Beijing abound in favours, then they must base this belief on the fact that the Emperor has forbidden us <!-- PDF page 197 | book page 188 | manuscript page 908 --> to speak of the Lord Patriarch, and for almost a whole year, whether at home or on his journeys, has admitted scarcely any of our Fathers into his presence.

That this is in accordance with the copy of the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary

### 9 A letter of the Bishop of Beijing to Fr. Vice-Provincial

May 26th. The Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing wrote to Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial with great concern about the approval or disapproval of what the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch did on the Coast of India.[^p197-11] The Most Illustrious Bishop had written to him about this matter on December 27th, 1707, as follows:[^p197-12] “From Guangzhou someone has written to me in the following words: ‘In Rome the absolution given by the Illustrious Monsignor the Archbishop of Goa[^p197-13] of the censures imposed by Monsignor the Patriarch has been declared null.’ And from Pondicherry an informant of mine had written on April 30th this year: ‘The Lord Primate recently sent the decree of the Holy Congregation which approved the pastoral letter in which he annuls what Monsignor the Patriarch did in this Coast.’ Truly I cannot reconcile these two contradictory statements. Your Very Reverend Paternity will have had letters from Goa with clear information as to whether it is true that Monsignor the Primate received a decree from Rome confirming his own because this new one would be more favourable to our | activities.” He said the same to Fr. Bouvet when he wrote: “If what they tell me from Pondicherry is true there is hope that what was done in China will not be approved, and the mission will be saved.”

In the letter of this day the Lord Bishop retracted his position in these words: “I wrote to Your Very Reverend Paternity some months ago the news I received from Guangzhou and from Pondicherry regarding the actions of Monsignor the Patriarch at the Coast and considering that the one from Pondicherry was more likely to be true, and that belief was confirmed by Rev. Fr. Castner. But I received, contrary to my expectation, a copy of the briefs of His Holiness to Their Excellencies the Archbishop of Goa and the Bishop of San Tomé in which His Holiness has decreed quite the contrary. Enclosed I send a copy so that you can see more clearly. I trust that the said document will be communicated to the other Fathers, especially to Fr. Joachim Bouvet, for I wrote also to the said Father about the same matter and, having written something materially contrary to the truth, I have an obligation to write this time what is the truth.”

<!-- PDF page 198 | book page 189 -->

From this retraction there was not derived nor should there have been any comfort for the Fathers in Beijing, since it was about matters different from what concerned them; except that they should have noticed that the Most Illustrious Bishop first conceived the hope that the Chinese mission would be preserved safe if Rome abrogated what was done by the Lord Patriarch on the Coast of India, but now had abandoned the hope that the mission could be saved once he believed that what was done by the Patriarch has been ratified by Rome. They should further have noticed that he accepted more readily the truth of the alleged contents of briefs sent to him by the patriarchal party than a decree of the Sacred Congregation sent by the Primate of the Indies. But we have no interest in such matters, leaving them to those to whom they apply.

So far, I have been dealing mainly with affairs of Beijing.

Let us now deal with the affairs of Guangzhou.

### 10 About Father Osório the Jesuit, the one who approves candidates for the permit in Guangzhou

Under the month of April, before its penultimate day, it was mentioned that the mandarins required of those seeking to go to Beijing to get a residence permit an affidavit, testimonial, or approval, to be given by a certain European called Li Guozheng. Six Reverend Franciscan Fathers successfully requested such approval from Fr. Osório, sj, whose name matched “Li Guozheng” even if the Chinese characters differed. This resulted in a variety of interpretations: some were not pleased, because in this way they seemed to be subject to one Jesuit; others were joyful, and the motive of their joy was scarcely believable. Father Monteiro wrote about this on May 3rd, in these words:[^p198-14] “I don’t know whether it was the Governor here who declared that Fr. Osório was this Li Guozheng whom the Prince, using an erroneous character, ordered to be the arbitrator of those who can go to request the piao. There are rumours that if someone decides to ask to go there, they should seek out this Li Guozheng and if they should find him, they will then have a good chance of success. We know nothing about this and yet this doesn’t stop people saying that God has worked miracles in support of the Lord Patriarch and his people.”

The followers of the Patriarch explained the miracle, and the joy that the miracle produced, in this way: that they now at last had a clear and irrefutable argument by which to persuade the whole of Europe that the Jesuits had brought it about that other Religious, and the secular clergy, and the apostolic vicars, and the Lord Patriarch himself, an apostolic visitor with the power of a <!-- PDF page 199 | book page 190 | manuscript page 909 --> legate a latere—that all of these be subject to themselves. This whole reasoning process, since it is without a foundation, does not require any refutation. Why didn’t they say this when, long ago, our Fathers gave testimony of good character on behalf of the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing, his secretary, the Franciscan Fathers in Shandong, etc; and further added petitions that they might be granted residence permits? Indeed, the Most Illustrious Lord, in thanking us for our intervention, did not hesitate to acknowledge that he was able to remain in China now because of our efforts.

But let me describe what actually happened: I leave aside that miracle, and demonstrate that what Fr. Osório did was appropriate and just, since he wished to be of assistance to those who, for the welfare of the mission, without offense to any living being, asked for his testimonial. And this is what, personally and on his own initiative, Fr. Osório, who was sick, wrote by the hand of an amanuensis to Fr. Tomás Pereira on 5th May:[^p199-15]

For six months now I have been laid up with the gout which has been burdening me this year more than ever. Six Religious of St. Francis and also Fr. Capacci[^p199-16] of our Society have been begging me to go guarantor for them so the mandarins of Guangzhou will allow them to go to Beijing to request the piao. I decided to give them the said testimonial for the reasons that I gave in my letter to Fr. Kilian, which he will communicate to Your Reverence just as I presented them to him in the letter I wrote to him. I send Your Reverence a document written in Chinese script signed by the six Religious in which they requested me to be guarantor for them to go to request the piao. I send also a written text in which I give the testimonial sought by the six Franciscan Religious. In addition, I send a document, signed by Fr. Capachi in which he seeks a testimonial to go to request the piao in Beijing. I gave him the affidavit in the same way that I gave it to the friars.

That this agrees with the original text, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary

### 11 The letter of Fr. Osório to Fr. Kilian

Since Fr. Tomás Pereira has been close to you for many years, he will have informed Your Reverence of what has happened. A rumour has spread here that a packet of letters sent from Beijing has been lost on the way from Guangzhou to Macau. There arrived here an order from the eldest son of the King in which it says that all Europeans must take for guarantor a certain Li Guozheng if they want to go to Beijing to request a piao etc. Li Guozheng is written in Chinese characters, as Your Reverence can see in the margin of this sheet under No.1.[^p200-18] The mandarins made inquiries at all the churches for the European Li Guozheng. Not finding this European in Guangzhou they sent to Macau to inquire, including at this college. But this testimony, given by Father Osório, had no effect on the mandarins, in so far as we in Beijing know today, 1 September 1708,[^p201-25] and as may be gathered from the letter of Father Monteiro from Guangzhou, dated 12 May, which reads:[^p201-26] “Here we have been able to gather no news concerning those <!-- PDF page 202 | book page 193 | manuscript page 910 --> who have petitioned to go to request the piao, and now that I have obtained the order of the buzheng shi they are awaiting a reply from the da wangye as to who this Li Guozheng is. Over this the business has been delayed for many months to the delight and triumph of the patriarchal party.”

And again on 13th of the same month:[^p202-27] “Today, May 13th, we received a message about the Bishop of Ascalon and I sent to the zongdu and the Governor in the hope that they might know who this Li Guozheng might be, since the character for Li is either as it was received from the former, or was written down wrongly by the latter. When Fr. Osório was asked, he replied that he was not the person in question, and in the Churches, they said they had nobody of that name. The good Bishop is quite upset about not being able to go to Manila and Rome etc.”

These things are confirmed by the letter of the Lord Bishop of Ascalon to Father Kilian, from Guangzhou, dated 13th May 1708. I reproduce this in full, also for other reasons.

### 14 A letter of the Bishop of Ascalon to Fr. Kilian

I received Your Paternity’s letter of December 1707 and deliberately delayed the task of answering it. Firstly, because nothing Your Paternity adduces to prove the innocence of Yourself and the other Fathers as regards the petition offered to the Emperor on my behalf requires any response; for, from other letters written by me in other contexts, it is quite clear that I never had any doubts about your good faith, or that of your fellow Jesuits, nor will I have any doubts unless there is some new and very grave reason for them. Secondly because I did not venture to disturb Your Paternity on account of conjectures, however certain they might seem to be, and I was awaiting an occasion when I might be able to instruct Your Paternity with all clarity. For I now discover by experience what I wrote to Your Paternity, namely that the mandarins prevent, by every means possible, my journey out of China. For when nearly three months ago I sought from the Governor |, that is the fuyuan,[^p202-28] permission to return to Europe on the ships from Manila, since I was neither able to go to Beijing, nor did I wish to receive a permit,[^p202-29] after a thousand evasions on the part of various officials of the zongdu and the buzheng shi, etc. the request was utterly refused. The Governor said that it was necessary to wait until it became clearer who Li Guozheng was, and then he could be questioned and the matter decided. And so the ships from Manila, which weighed anchor today, are sailing away without me, and I remain, exposed to <!-- PDF page 203 | book page 194 --> many more hardships than before. So the position is that it is quite impossible for me to appear in person before His Holiness. May it please Your Paternity to make my position known, so that, without any further delay, he may realise in what great danger I am detained; since I am still hoping that not only Your Paternity, but also the Fathers Superior, may at last lend their ears to what now for a long time, and more than once, I have written, namely that you and they should make efforts to obtain from the Emperor an express command, with no letter changed even by a marginal note, to the effect that the mandarins are no longer to detain me, but are to let me go, or even expel me. For the mandarins want to give me permission to go to the Court, but are not willing to permit me to go away, and in the meantime, I suffer molestations from their inferiors. I do not venture to be troublesome to Reverend Father Antoine Thomas; he has in fact not responded with a single word to any of my letters,[^p203-30] so that I have no hope of help from him. However, I do not wish for that reason to make an end of friendship with him, but I commend myself heartily to him, together with the rest of his fellow Jesuits. Farewell. Guangzhou, 13 May 1708.

I testify that this is in accordance with the original.[^p203-31]

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary

### 15 A letter of Fr. Monteiro to Fr. Kilian

The matter is set out most clearly in a letter of Fr. Monteiro to Fr. Kilian, dated May 22nd.[^p203-32] It reads: “There has been great disagreement amongst the mandarins over granting permission to those Franciscan Fathers, as Your Reverence will see in the Chinese document that I send to you. So, it is to be hoped that the zongdu or the fuyuan will resolve the matter of the jiangwen[^p203-33] of the buzheng shi. They sent the promises of the six Franciscans to Fr. Tomás Pereira together with the affidavit of Fr. Osório. It was hoped that Fr. Osório would be recognized as Li Guozheng so that he could seek permission from the Governor for Fr. Capacci, but since he was not so recognized and the question of who he might be was referred to the Prince, I fear that the Father will not be able to go etc.”

<!-- PDF page 204 | book page 195 -->

From what has been said, therefore, it is clear that, firstly, the mandarins have always found some pretext or other to prevent Europeans from going to Beijing to receive a permit, and in April they alleged that they had a positive order from the Prince, that only those for whom a European named Li Guozheng has given an affidavit were to be allowed to come to the Court. Secondly, in all the churches of Guangzhou, even those of the Jesuits, the response has been that there is no European with a name expressed by those characters. Thirdly, Father Osório further denied in Macau that these characters signify him. Fourthly, nevertheless Fr. Osório did what he could to help the missionaries, and gave an affidavit to them in accordance with his conscience, even though this might mean accepting the troubles which he knew could follow for him, for his fellow Jesuits, and even for the whole Society. Fifthly, the mandarins did not accept the testimony of Fr. Osório, but made it an issue, writing to Prince in Beijing, so that after three or four months, when they had received a reply, it might finally become clear who this Li Guozheng is.

### 16 Hatred of the Jesuits, unjustly kindled and fanned into flame because of what Fr. Osório did

Hence it is clear that the Jesuits had no part in this train of events. If they had ambitiously sought to be judges with regard to the petitioning of the Emperor for patents and his granting of them, why did they not accept when it was offered to them? Why did they openly reject that any of their members had the name indicated by these characters? Why did Fr. Osório publicly declare that although in Chinese he is called Li Guozheng, his name is not written with those Chinese characters? So where is the miracle?

Please note, reader, that this matter, from its very nature, was such that the followers of the Patriarch hoped they could move decisively against the Jesuits by means of a dilemma. For either they, or one of them, was going to assume the authority to grant a testimonial, approval or affidavit to Religious etc. who wished to obtain a permit, or he, or they, would or were going to refuse to do so. In the first case, the followers of the Patriarch were prepared to say that this was clearly an unholy sin against the sacred authority of the Lord Patriarch; in the second case they would declare it to be evident that the Jesuits were preventing the missionaries from being granted permits and remaining in China. However, truth opened a way for the Jesuits: while those in Beijing were unaware of the Prince having written anything or, much more importantly, of what the mandarins were saying had been written by the Prince, nevertheless their brothers in Guangzhou and Macau rejected assuming an authority they suspected to be false, but granted to those missionaries who requested it what charity demanded. Since it did not work out as they thought, the Jesuits could not be accused by the patriarchal party. Nevertheless the supporters of the Patriarch <!-- PDF page 205 | book page 196 | manuscript page 911 --> took pleasure even in this, and fashioned in their interest a new miracle, and at the same time formulated a new argument against the Jesuits—in their interest, I say, since they think that they too are secure in Guangzhou for four months longer, until a response comes from the Court; against the Jesuits, since from this very delay they pretend that the Emperor | does not wish to expel anyone, but that he will, at least quietly, grant permission for everyone to remain, without a permit, until the reply arrives from Rome. And indeed, this very thing is known to be desired by the Jesuits, since they laboured with all their strength to bring it about, before the need to petition for a permit was made public; this need was, for a time, not made public in the thirteen provinces due to the request of the Jesuits, until after the reverend clerics S. Giorgio, Hervé, Montigny, Le Breton, and Carvalho, etc. gave cause for its publication.

To the material from Guangzhou may be added the letter of the Rev. Fr. Tomás Hortiz, Vice-Provincial of the Augustinians, to Father Tomás Pereira, dated 10 March 1708.[^p205-34] It reads:

### 17 A letter from Fr. Tomás Hortiz to Fr. Tomás Pereira

Very Reverend Father, The Grace of Christ be with you. I have not written to Your Very Reverend Paternity not from any want of goodwill and affection but in order not to bother Your Very Reverend Paternity with my worries. However, now that I have an opportunity to send a message to Beijing, I write these few words to greet Your Paternity and beg news of your health, and to express the hope that God will grant you many more years for the good of this mission. At the same time, I want to inform Your Paternity of the many afflictions which the mandarins have imposed on me without cause, taking us prisoners, as I have already written to Fr. Antoine Thomas, for no other reason than that the fu yuan sent orders to make a list of the names of those who resided in Shaojing fu. The zhifu proceeded to seize the altar and images of the church in Dongzhou[^p205-35] and burned a biao[^p205-36] on which there was a brief notice of the Law of God. And because he was of a mind to annoy me, he then persuaded some of the Christians to apostatise and he told the gentiles that if they wanted to complain that this church was there, they should present a petition against it for him to dismantle it. But because the lawabiding gentiles could not find it appropriate to present a petition, he instructed the district magistrate to order the leading citizens of Dingzhou to present such a petition, and below I give the terms in which they presented it. The only <!-- PDF page 206 | book page 197 --> reasons they gave in it were that they said that twice every month the Christians gather to recite together and they keep no registers of their members, so it is difficult to distinguish the good from the bad. This will become clear from the Chinese original which I attach. In it they request that the zhixian should inquire into the matter. He gave his judgment that within three days anybody who disagreed should present their objections. He was immediately presented with a petition requesting a delay until he should have read the imperial decree. He then decreed that he had already been informed by the zhifu and decided to refer it to the zhixian to carry it out. A second petition was presented to the zhixian referring to the zhi of the Emperor which permitted the churches, and the gaoshi of the Jingtian jian which enjoins its observance.[^p206-37] The response was to give twenty blows to the servant of the Church, which knocked him down, and order him to pull it down until it was knocked flat. Finally, the church was demolished with such enthusiasm that not a stone was left upon stone. I have briefly described this in order to beg Your Very Reverend Paternity to graciously favour us in this matter by supporting in all possible ways the rebuilding of the said church. Any expenses you incur in this undertaking I will deposit here in Guangzhou, where Your Paternity can send for reimbursement. I am not unaware of the little that at present Your Very Reverend Paternity is able to do at the Court but I hope that God will give you a suitable occasion and that Your Paternity will take advantage of it. Of the five of our Religious remaining in China, I have stationed one of our men in Macau, namely Fr. Fray Francisco Fontanilla. Another, Fr. Fray Juan de Rivera, is blind.[^p206-38] The remaining three of us[^p206-39] will have to leave eventually since we are prevented from going for the piao. This is completely unsatisfactory and it will not help us to get one since it will just multiply our problems. All we can do is stay on here telling the mandarins that it is only until the Reverend Fathers come from Rome, and there is nothing else we can do that will help, except to beg Your Very Reverend Paternities to get permission for us to stay with our churches. But if we should be obliged to leave China Your Reverence will not be able to secure the restoration of the said church because when we have to leave there will not be enough silver here in Guangzhou to rebuild it. So, it seems to me you should keep in mind that it is a common cause and a very serious one. I finish with this. May God preserve

<!-- PDF page 207 | book page 198 | manuscript page 912 -->

Your Very Reverend Paternity to whose sacrifices I recommend myself and to whom as a suppliant I commit myself in matters of such seriousness. Guangzhou, 10 March 1708.[^p207-40]

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.
