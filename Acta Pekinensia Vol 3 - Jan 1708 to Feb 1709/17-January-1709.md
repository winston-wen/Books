We began this year grieving over the death of Father Tomás Pereira, whose funeral we decided should be held on January 8th.

### 1 The mourning ceremonies in the Chinese manner used at Fr. Pereira’s funeral

On the first day of the month and the year, three eunuch chamberlains sent obituaries to the tomb of Fr. Pereira. They were written in white on a black background on a fabric shot with gold, almost in the manner in which philosophical sayings are printed on silk and hung from gold embossed rollers to adorn a lycaeum.[^p561-1] There was this difference, however, that those are usually confined to a surface of two cubits, whereas these were resplendent on a far wider field, more than ten ells broad.

On [January] 2nd. The Mathematical Tribunal also made a solemn presentation in honour of Fr. Pereira, offering a silken panegyric not unlike that of the eunuchs.[^p561-2] And on the subsequent days his friends of every rank would come to mourn, including among their number the minister Zhang,[^p561-3] who, in the name of himself and his two colleagues (that is to say, his two Chinese colleagues: the other three ministers are Manchus),[^p561-4] hung an elegy inscribed on damask above the bier.

On [January] 3rd. A special ceremony in his memory, which deserves a place in these chronicles, was performed by a nephew of the Emperor, the son of his second eldest brother,[^p561-5] a man of about 30 years of age. He sent his servants <!-- PDF page 562 | book page 553 | manuscript page 1102 --> with the Master of the Palace who performed the customary rites, called diao[^p562-6] in Chinese, before the coffin of the deceased, with the usual offerings of food, which they then served to us to eat. At the wish of the Prince, they made us sit down in the same hall where the open bier was being kept, and invited us to the meal, serving it with their own hands. This will seem strange in Europe, but is not for that reason superstitious. Rather it makes clearer the purpose of the offerings of meats and pastries and fruit at the tomb, which is to show how the memory of the deceased lives on in his friends and to express care for the living who are in attendance on the dead. Just as drink is offered elsewhere, so also at the death of Fr. Pereira on December 24th the Emperor sent the eunuch Zhang and the official Zhao with a serving of cha, as if to swallow our sorrows. If a European still says that his scruples are not assuaged and is still nervous in case the deceased is taken for an idol and that the offerings are idolatrous sacrifices, with due respect I suggest that he test the origin of his own spirit which he too easily assumes to be a good spirit if he is more scrupulous about other people’s behaviour than about his own. For what kind of delicacy of conscience do you have if, before you have understood the nature of some rite, you condemn in your own mind those who practise it? You do not know whether you are more learned than them, but you certainly know, or you certainly ought to presume, that others have spent much more effort than you have in trying to reach an accurate understanding of the matter and the form of that rite. So, either suspend your judgement, or come to China so that you can learn at first hand about what you now judge in ignorance. I believe that you, whom I am addressing, are judging as a private individual, not as a duly appointed judge, for it is incumbent on a true judge to assemble the evidence before he delivers his judgement.

Since mention has been made of Fr. Pereira’s funeral at the beginning of this month, it will be best not to leave an interval of a few days before describing his burial; so, I shall add the rest here, and, when this topic is completed, resume the narrative of other events in due order of dates.[^p562-7]

[January] 8th the official Zhao with others from the Yangxin dian and the Wuying dian brought an address from the Emperor which His Majesty graciously gave as a posthumous honour for the Father. |

### 2 The funeral panegyric in honour of Fr. Pereira composed by the Emperor

The shangyu,[^p563-8] or Words of the Emperor, as a panegyric for Fr. Tomás Pereira: I, the Emperor, have considered the fact that Xu Risheng[^p563-9] [the Chinese name for Fr. Tomás Pereira], with the intentions of an upright and religious man, came here from a very distant land to offer me attentive, faithful, and lifelong service, not only through the sciences of music and mathematics, in which he was wonderfully adept, but also in the construction of every kind of ingenious device perfectly adapted not simply to give pleasure but also to be very useful. I have considered also his uprightness of heart, his frankness of soul, his truthfulness in speech, his dedication to the tasks I set him, and his sincere faithfulness based on love in which, from the beginning to his end, I have observed him to have followed a constant course. For all these virtues I often praised him while he lived. Hearing by chance that he was taken ill I wished through my physicians to free him from his sickness, but the news came unexpectedly that he had departed this life. I cannot but sorrow at his passing. So now for his funeral I donate two hundred ounces of silver and ten rolls of Chinese silk, that at least in this way I may bear witness to the love I have for him, and how much I shall miss this vassal from abroad.[^p563-10]

The 47th year of Kangxi, the 11th month, 27th day.[^p563-11]

This piece of Chinese eloquence may seem to the European orator somewhat unadorned. But its worth is to be judged by its author: it is a greater thing to be called a poet by a prince than if a commoner thinks you are an Apollo, a far greater compliment to be called faithful by an Emperor than to be described as an Achates by a companion.[^p563-12] Imperial compositions of this kind are extremely prestigious documents in China, and have proved of great benefit to both missionaries and Christians when they are printed and sent round all the provinces, or when inscribed upon a sepulchre.

### 3 An argument about the route to be taken for Fr. Pereira’s funeral

January 8th. There was a controversy about the route to be taken by Fr. Pereira’s funeral procession. The point of disagreement arose because there was a shrine to an idol along the Royal Way[^p564-13] in which and in front of which at that time the imperial coat of arms was displayed along with a tablet with his name on it, and prayers were being offered in many shrines for him because he was ill at the time. The Chinese laws lay it down that wherever the Emperor is deemed to be present, as when the tablet with his name is exposed and his arms are displayed outside to indicate this, nobody is permitted to pass by on horseback. This is observed even in front of the south gate of the Imperial Palace, despite the fact that it is almost always closed and the Emperor’s residence inside is at least three stadia distant, and similarly for the gates of each of the Princes. The instigator of the controversy was Fr. Luigi Gonzaga, who had not yet been in China even for a full year. There were two others as well who thought it better to take another route, though from a different motive. They did not believe it was wrong, given the circumstances, to dismount at the gates of the shrine, but they hesitated about whether all the Christians had been sufficiently instructed to understand, without mistake or scandal, that one might be dismounting not to the idol but in accordance with a civil honour paid to the Emperor. But Father Gonzaga denied that the Emperor could be honoured in that place without the honour redounding to the idol as well. He was so convinced of this that he wished to persuade everyone else as well, and so the most junior of all the Jesuits in China challenged the most senior of them all, Fr. Filippo Grimaldi, a missionary of 40 years’ experience, proposing that both of them should write down their reasons, and then descend into the lists. There is no point in citing Fr. Gonzaga’s reasons here, for at that time he did not know what they were himself, but the zeal that seized him persuaded him that he was defending what he took to be the truth. I do not say these things | in any way to insult the Father; but I was proved right by the outcome. When the Visitor two or three days later told him to produce his reasons in writing, he asked for a postponement, which lasted for more than a month before he could put any of his reasoning down on paper.

### 4 Fr. Gonzaga’s unfortunate dispute with the other Fathers

His main reason was, as he put it, that the Fathers had long held that it was not permissible, and that was why in such circumstances none of our people had ever dismounted from his horse. He sought to prove that claim from the more recent example of the funeral of Fr. Jean-François Gerbillon. When prayers were being <!-- PDF page 565 | book page 556 --> offered by order of the Emperor in the same square, albeit in a different shrine, the Fathers accompanying the funeral procession left the house on foot until they had passed that shrine. However, his reasoning was mistaken, since none of the Fathers orally or in writing had ever taught that it was wrong to do in Beijing what Elisha had allowed Naaman to do in the temple of Remnon.[^p565-14] He was also mistaken in the example he alleged in support: the situation was different, for on that occasion the name of the Emperor was not displayed nor was he deemed to be present; it was simply that at the Emperor’s command prayers were offered for one of the queens. It was by that order that people were asked to dismount, just as formerly in Beijing there were shrines before which by imperial statute one always dismounts. In such cases, where the emperor is not deemed to be present but an idol is, we neither dismounted nor rode past, but made a detour because the honour was being paid to the idol and not the emperor.

That is all that needs to be said about the controversy. However, the contrary case as drawn up by Fr. Gonzaga was sent to Rome together with the resolution of the problem. This was not by way of an accusation—why should zeal be accused even if it is misguided?—but so that it will be clear in future where the truth lay, and no further opportunity would be given for any Navarretelike fairy-tales. Nevertheless, even on this occasion we did not in fact dismount, because when the prayers were over, the arms and name of the Emperor were taken down that very morning.

### 5 The pomp of a funeral full of piety and a credit to our religion

January 10th. The funeral was conducted with a religious dignity which provided a marvellous spectacle. Two mandarins, sent by the Emperor for this, led the way, and the eulogy given by the Emperor and inscribed on silk was carried along in the procession. Then, as is customary, came the processional cross with candles; sacred statues on their biers, accompanied by acolytes, thurifers, and then almost two thousand Christians dressed in white as a sign of mourning. Those among them who were close to the cross and the statues went in twos with lighted candles to the place of burial, about one French league from the College. There, the sepulchre was blessed, and the remainder of the Christian ceremony completed. Everyone was weeping with such sorrow that the representatives of the Emperor and the princes said that while they had taken great pleasure in seeing a funeral procession so full of dignity, nothing had <!-- PDF page 566 | book page 557 | manuscript page 1104 --> amazed them so much as a crowd of so many people weeping with all their hearts though none of those present was related to the deceased by blood or by marriage. More powerful men could indeed muster such a number of relatives and slaves for a funeral service, but they had witnessed nobody, not even a prince, cause such an enormous crowd to pour out sincere tears such as had been shed from their hearts by so many Christians in memory of the deceased.

Such is the state of our times that there will be those in Europe who will disapprove of such expense being incurred for the burial of a poor Religious who would have been buried from the Roman College for three scudi or less. Nonetheless, a funeral procession in China is a solemn and public gathering, and it provides both an argument in favour of the Christian Law and an encouragement to live in a Christian manner. It removes the slur which is attached to the Law of the Europeans[^p566-15] (which is how they describe our Holy Religion), that it has no greater respect for the dead than for a donkey which is being buried; it teaches people that there are other rites in addition to Chinese rites, | and of greater solemnity than the Chinese; and so people are encouraged to leave aside the Devil’s pomps and the hellish trivialities of the bonzes and to embrace a religion in which even the external ceremonies breathe holiness.

January 12th. Laying our grief aside we went to the Emperor’s villa and, with the normal ceremonies of respect, we gave thanks for the many kindnesses bestowed by the Emperor on the late Fr. Pereira. The Emperor was still convalescing, and in very fragile health. He asked us for some remedy to aid his recovery, as I will have occasion to say again on several occasions in the days to come.

### 6 A letter from Fr. Juan Fernández Serrano about the mandarins who are harassing his church

I must also tell Your Very Reverend Paternity how in two Christian communities in the prefecture of Jinan, the zhixian (district magistrates) are persecuting the Christians. They have flogged many people, and put others in jiahao (cangues).[^p567-18] The xianguan (prefect of the city) threatened a scholar that if he did not apostatise from the xie jiao,[^p567-19] evil law (which is how he describes our Holy Law), he would lose his degree. All this information comes from Christians who have come from these townships, and from letters sent by others. The mandarin demanded that the materials with which Fray Bernardo wanted to build a church should instead be used to build a miao.[^p567-20] We did not want to trust these reports from Christians and trouble you until it became clear that it was essential. A priest went to those towns— actually it was Fr. Francisco de S. José—so that if he found out that these reports were true, he could see whether it was possible to sort things out with the mandarins; and if he could do nothing, he would write a complete account for Your Very Reverend Paternities.[^p567-21] I beg your Very Reverend Paternity to use your influence to intervene. I do realise that the reputation of Europeans at court here is not what it was, and that you Reverend Fathers cannot do all you would like for the good of the mission and to reassure the missionaries. But I trust in God that if you talk to our friend Zhao Chang, he will find some way to restrain the mandarins of the abovementioned towns, who after all are in contravention of the latest decree of the Libu. Your Very Reverend Paternity must forgive me for troubling you, and accept cordial remembrances from Fathers Diego de S. Rosa and Francisco de la Concepción. I await your Very Reverend Paternity’s orders, and pray God to grant me happy years to come.

Jining zhou, 3 January 1709.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

### 7 Fr. Monteiro’s letter to Fr. Kilian about Fr. Visdelou

Also, on the 3rd. Fr. Monteiro wrote from Guangzhou to Fr. Kilian Stumpf, saying that the news from Beijing about the deposition of the Crown Prince had reached them. On the basis of these reports, Fr. Visdelou tried to defend something that was contrary to the Emperor’s declaration issued in 1700 and 1706 that nothing was asked for from deceased relatives. Here are Fr. Monteiro’s words: |

### 8 Fr. Visdelou cites Chinese writings to reject the Rites

It has come to my attention that our Most Reverend Visdelou (named a bishop by the Lord Patriarch) has translated into French some Chinese gazettes giving notice there (namely in Beijing) of the removal and imprisonment of the Crown Prince, news received with great applause in the French factory, and which no doubt will spread throughout the world reprinted many times by the same. He explains or rather translates that passage in which the Emperor says, using Tian Di for Heaven and Earth, that he prays to Heaven and Earth. Another printed report about the pardon granted in the name of the deceased empress[^p568-22] says the same thing, that he gaoji Tiandi[^p568-23] and the ancestors; a little further on he says Suoyi nian an zang yu fu gui jin min ye,[^p568-24] in which he confidently hopes that the ancestors will help or console the people. Your Reverence in his zeal should collect any gazettes that have appeared about this matter and closely examine the terms used and what they mean, and whether in the pardon mentioning the deceased Empress there are the characters zang yu fu gui[^p568-25] and what they mean in this context, and how they agree with the Emperor’s declaration that they never pray to nor expect any help from the ancestors. The reason is that this translation of the Most Reverend Visdelou seems very convincing and will appear so to the general public. I recommend myself etc. Guangzhou, 3 January 1710.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

### 9 May missionaries, if the Emperor allows it, remain in China and not preach the Gospel?

Also, on the 3rd. The same Fr. Monteiro wrote to Fr. Visitor[^p569-26] a letter which I will include presently, about a fresh controversy: whether it is permitted to promise the Emperor to remain in China even while not preaching the Gospel until the Supreme Pontiff’s determination comes back to China concerning the Emperor’s dossier, which was sent by the hands of Fathers Barros and Beauvollier? Something has already been said about this under the years 1707 and 1708.

Here is what Fr. Monteiro says:

In regard to the promise not to preach, thanks be to God, the mandarins have never obliged us to observe it, and this creates a further difficulty which on one occasion led Your Reverence to say that the positive precept to preach the Gospel includes another negative one that we are always obliged never to place an obstacle in the way or make promises against it, and this irrespective of to whom the promise is made, whether gentile or Christian, tyrant or not. Any prohibition, suspension, or censure of the Church in such a matter, which arises in one case but not the other, is always exclusive of cases of necessity in which exclusion applies in the case of Christians. But in an absolute promise made to a gentile such an exclusion is not present unless I expressly include it. Here however we have not made such a promise and we carry out our ministry by virtue of the pastoral appeal of the Archbishop and because the patriarchal decree was never sufficiently published, nor do we see it being followed etc.

### 10 A refutation of Fr. Monteiro’s view

Whatever one might think of the subtleties of his argument concerning the double command involved in the words of Christ, “Preach the Gospel,”[^p569-27] it is certain that it is irrelevant to the present case in China, and I do not know of anything in any mission which is strictly parallel to our situation. One must point out that it is not the Emperor who has forbidden anyone to preach the Gospel; it is the Commissary’s decree which the Emperor will not allow to be preached until he himself has been given a hearing in Rome on a matter which is his proper concern. Indeed, in so far as it depends upon the Emperor, he does wish the Gospel to be preached, and, if one thinks about it, in so far as it is in his power he actually requires it to be preached, for he is preventing the preachers from leaving China, whereas if they do they will no longer be preaching, and perhaps will have ceased preaching <!-- PDF page 570 | book page 561 | manuscript page 1106 --> once and for all. He persuades and invites them not to heed the Commissary’s prohibition, and that they should give themselves to the preaching of the Gospel as they used to do, in accordance with the permission given by Alexander vii which so far has never been withdrawn by any other pope, and which is beyond any doubt of greater weight and validity than any commissarial prohibition; of course this is only until Clement xi, the Commissary’s Superior, makes his decision once he has received valid information, | which is much stronger than any mere opinion.

### 11 It is the Lord Patriarch, not the Emperor, who is blocking the preaching of the Gospel in China

So it is the Commissary[^p570-28] who is preventing the preaching of the Gospel, who at this moment would rather not have the Gospel preached on the substantials of our Holy Faith than that his own decree should not be preached before the Emperor’s information can do its work. The only thing which the Emperor requires of the missionaries is that they should remain in China and not spurn His Majesty’s goodwill towards the Gospel, even if the Lord Commissary suspends them from preaching. He protests that any obstacle is not being put there by him but by the Commissary, who illegally refuses to listen to His Majesty in his own kingdom, and would like to block him from getting an effective hearing from the Commissary’s own superior. What this amounts to is a coup whereby he has snatched away from his superior the power to give judgement. On the other side, the Emperor puts it to the missionaries that he himself should by decree exile everyone who prefers to go along with the Commissary’s coup d’état rather than to obey the laws of the Emperor and the Supreme Pontiff Clement xi, and the authority of Alexander vii. There is the case: it is the Vicar of Christ in whose hands judgement lies.

January 8th. Someone from the College in Macau sent Fr. Visitor a letter he had written saying that the Captain General claims to have looked after the Lord Patriarch, because, in accordance with an ordinance of the Viceroy in Goa, the military guard was to be considered removed from the door of his Most Illustrious and Reverend Lordship; the soldiers who remained were left there <!-- PDF page 571 | book page 562 --> solely as a mark of honour and protection; they had no intention of preventing anyone who wished to go in or out or wanted to be admitted to any form of communication with His Lordship.

### 12 The guard stationed outside the Lord Patriarch’s residence

The Lord Patriarch refused to accept this freedom, as was confirmed by what Fr. João Mourão wrote to Fr. Kilian on the same January 8th: “The Lord Patriarch is continuing to complain to us that he is being held a prisoner.” He goes on to add some news to be passed on to Fr. Visitor that a rumour has come via Manila to the effect that the Lord Patriarch has been made a cardinal. At this, several of our people, in particular Amiani,[^p571-29] Capelli,[^p571-30] and Messari[^p571-31] felt prompted to try to ensure that all the guards who had been left at the door of the Patriarch’s palace, no matter under what pretext, should be removed. When Fr. Capelli proposed this, the Captain General consented but on condition that the Lord Patriarch agree to revoke anything he had done which infringed the patronage of the Portuguese crown.

### 13 The rumour about the Lord Patriarch being given a cardinalate

On his own initiative Fr. Capelli accompanied by Fr. Messari went to the Lord Patriarch’s residence, intending to communicate the Captain General’s resolution. They were received by Fr. Astudilho, a Dominican who lived with the Lord Patriarch as his interpreter, and by the Most Reverend Fr. Candela, the Lord Patriarch’s chancellor. However, they announced that the Lord Patriarch had rejected any congratulations about the cardinalate, and, so far as the Captain General’s proposal went, he had no intention of putting his freedom up for sale; so, the Fathers had been told to go back home without being admitted to see the Patriarch at all. The same writer then gives an account of the efforts of these three, and in particular Fr. Capelli, with the permission of Fr. Provincial Francisco Pinto, going round from house to house and appearing before the magistrates advocating the complete removal of the guards. To no avail: the magistrates thought that they themselves had behaved correctly and deferentially in the Lord Patriarch’s interest; in their own, they had acted prudently by paying out of their own funds for the honour guard at the gates of the patriarchal <!-- PDF page 572 | book page 563 | manuscript page 1107 --> palace, rather than expose His Illustrious Lordship and their city to more serious troubles, about which they were apprehensive, and not without reason.[^p572-32]

### 14 The reason why the authorities in Macau decided that the guard on the Patriarch’s residence should not be removed

They argued as follows: The person of the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch has been committed to us by the Emperor, to be kept under guard until such time as the Emperor is officially told that he has had his hearing in Rome and the decision of the Supreme Pontiff has arrived. This very guard was frequently praised by the Emperor in rather commendatory terms, and not only has the Viceroy in Canton several times called on | the city to give an account of the Lord Patriarch’s guard, but he himself has, through the mandarins, sent soldiers who keep a watch from a ship on an entrance by which a skiff might enter the Patriarch’s palace.[^p572-33]

So, if we remove the guard completely, they will require that an account of this decision too will have to be written and sent to the Emperor, and besides, the mandarins will then station a Chinese guard, thus giving offence to the citizens of Macau and to the Patriarch’s people, which will have far worse consequences in the long-term both for His Illustrious Lordship and for the city.[^p572-34]

### 15 The Beijing Jesuits did not object to the Lord Patriarch being set free

I would add just one comment, that a short while before this, Fr. Carlo Amiani, full of pity for the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch, wrote to Beijing to Fr. Gonzaga saying that at the order of the Viceroy of East India full liberty had been granted to the Lord Patriarch, but that the Jesuits in Beijing had prevented this reasonable command of the Viceroy from being carried out. Father Gonzaga commented that Fr. Amiani out of concern for the Lord Patriarch had overstepped the mark, and wrote back to him proving that before that letter of Fr. Amiani had arrived in Beijing no news of the Viceroy’s order had reached, or could have reached us, and so any such assumption that the Beijing Jesuits objected to the Lord Patriarch being freed was pure fantasy. But who can tie down the fantasies of dreamers, the mouths of gossipers, the pens of writers? In this world all such things have to be permitted as long as we are Jesuits, humble members of a militant and suffering Church, just as every human being has to allow rains, winds, hailstorms, and the winds of hurricanes which it is beyond human powers to subdue. It should be enough for the exiled children of Adam that they know how to protect their skins from the powers of the air which without the decree of the omniscient God can do us no harm.

### 17 A letter from the Bishop of Ascalon to Fr. Kilian saying how he is doing in Macau

I have received a couple of letters from Your Reverence, dated October 4th and October 19th. Your kindness towards me gives me pleasure, | and I confess that I am not up to thanking you properly, still less paying back my debts. Here I am in Macau, ready, God willing, to leave for Luzon in April, and then to transfer and go on to Europe. But if I am compelled to stop because of my health or some other obstacle, there too I shall not fail the mission: I have various contacts, some of them powerful. As I write, I am being pressed from all sides and dragged into many different things, which is why I cannot do Your Paternity justice.

Concerning the doubts about the episcopal consecration,[^p574-35] I am taking a line which is different from yours, and it is this: Two dispensations are required, the <!-- PDF page 575 | book page 566 --> first for the election, and if there is a defect here, then there can be no consecration. The second is for a consecration done by just a single bishop (since without a dispensation from the Pope there can be no valid ceremony with just one bishop, as is generally agreed by all authorities, and there is little doubt that this does bear on validity). According to the law, then, there are three necessary conditions, the imposition of hands being the matter of the sacrament. So, if the Pope did not grant or it is not clear that he has granted the first dispensation, then there is no second one either. It would be pointless to get a dispensation so that a person who has not been validly elected could be consecrated by just one bishop. What does not exist cannot have any properties, and the secondary follows upon what is primary. For that reason I said that I am being exposed to the danger of—rather, I should have said to actual—nullity. I think there is no easy way round this point. It is astonishing that the Father who has been chosen, or someone else who supports him, has not written a single word to lessen such a serious scandal. But nowadays there is no other law than “That’s what the Lord (Patriarch, etc.) wants.” I place great reliance on Your Paternity’s promise of trying to do something on my behalf with the Emperor, but, as I wrote, it will be better for me to say nothing, in case he or one of his agents should make inquiries about me by name. These negotiations in the Court keep us in a state of worry and anxiety, and they are among the most serious which can happen in the kingdom. So, we all commend the affair to God. Here, comparatively speaking, things are as disturbed as they could be, with the Portuguese fighting on one side and the Lord Patriarch and a not inconsiderable number of missionaries on the other. I got here because I had been granted a safe conduct, and am unwilling to meddle in things which are not my business either personally or relating to my mission. The Portuguese approve of my proposal, and so I am well accepted by them, but I am afraid there might be a lot of trouble from the Lord Patriarch, who loses no opportunity of harassing me. ### 18 Public prayers for the Emperor’s health

Whether the Lord Bishop had begun any inquiry among our Christians or not, we neither know, nor have we asked. It was all a long time ago. So, I want to describe in more detail what is and was done at those ceremonies, so that in future generations someone might speak up for us one day.

### 20 The Christian ceremonies normally used together with the non-superstitious Chinese ones

When the Mathematical Tribunal, in which some of the officials are gentiles and some Christians, decide by a majority vote to hold the prayers for the Emperor or any other public necessity in our church, it then sends a deputation to ask if we would welcome them. They assure us that they all wish to exclude anything which we think incompatible with our Christian religion. They promise not to address their prayers for the Emperor’s health to any other than the one to whom we address petitions in our way; the only difference is that we call him the Lord of Heaven, while they term him Heaven or Supreme King.[^p577-39] From their books and ours it has for more than a century been clear that they are in fact the same God. They offer funds and anything else which might be needed. This proposal is no sooner heard <!-- PDF page 578 | book page 569 | manuscript page 1110 --> than praised. Their request is granted, and they are informed that because they recognize the same God, we are pleased to share these things with them, and that we have no difficulty about following them in the other things as well. There are different ways to serve the God whom they adore as Lord of Heaven, and who has inscribed his commands in the heart of every human being. Spiritual worship having been promised, the funds they offered are not accepted, and it is left to them to look after what is done outside the church—such as Chinese inscriptions, banners, the imperial arms, flute-players and timpanists, and guards at the door. We at our own expense decorate the things inside: altars, sacred images, etc. They add an elegant little table on which is placed a tablet inscribed in gold letters Huangdi wansui wanwansui,[^p578-40] “May the august Emperor be granted a thousand times ten thousand years.” On the appointed day, this little table is put inside the church to one side outside the chancelscreen, and then, at the agreed hour, the mandarins gather; music is heard in the street outside the doors and an orchestra plays inside the church, where the Fathers are waiting in fine vestments. The mandarins come in, form up in lines, and stand erect. The priest, vested in a surplice, stole, and a Chinese biretta enters, accompanied by the acolytes and thurifers; as he genuflects at the foot of the altar, the other priests and the mandarins prostrate themselves to reverence God in the sacred image with the customary beating of their heads. Then everyone kneels | and litanies and other prayers are sung by the Christians, interspersed by musical interludes. The priest goes up to the altar, incenses it, and then recites the prayers of the Church invoking the most Holy Trinity, and those to the patron saints for the conversion of the nations, etc. There is another adoration before the sacred image, the priest comes down once more, and the other priests move back. Then the little table of the Emperor is moved from the side of the nave into the middle, and before it the mandarins prostrate themselves nine times. Then the Christians sing aloud the rosary of the Blessed Virgin antiphonally, with musical episodes between the prayers. And so, with singing and playing musical instruments a large part of the day is spent, without a hint of anything slipping in which might smack of paganism.

### 21 What happens when the royal banners are unfurled in front of the doors

There are two further things to be noted about this service. First, while the prayers are being recited outside the doors, the royal banners are unfurled, glowing with golden dragons. While these are displayed, nobody is <!-- PDF page 579 | book page 570 --> allowed to go past on horseback, cart, or sedan chair under pain of a flogging, which would be inflicted without further ado by a soldier or an attendant of the Tribunal. This honour, dismounting and so on, is not offered to a sacred image, but to the Emperor’s banners and tablets. Nor can a lower tribunal give orders to a higher one, or to anyone which is not subject to it, to make them dismount in front of the Jesuit church. It is a matter of imperial law that on every occasion on which, on the authority of even the lowest tribunal, the imperial arms and tablet is displayed, nobody of any rank whatsoever, be they magistrate, prince, son of the Emperor, or even the Crown Prince, is permitted to go past other than humbly on foot.

### 22 Tablets of the living are not superstitious

The second point is that even living persons in China have their tablets. The first of these is that of the Emperor, to whom honour is due by the law of the land. But there is nothing superstitious about it, nor is the spirit of the Emperor thought to reside in the tablet, which is no more than a conventional symbol, just as the Pietà statue stands for a real person. We do not keep or display the Emperor’s tablet in any of our churches anywhere in China except for a prayer service such as I have just described. It is a lie, pure and simple, which some people got from Navarrete who in turn got it from a letter (forged or genuine?) in which Fr. Francisco de la Madre de Dios[^p579-41] states that in our burial chapel in Beijing we incense such a tablet every day, and that one is brazenly displayed in all our churches. ### 24 A remedy proposed to the Emperor

We made excuses, | saying that we were hardly qualified to give medical advice, but we went on to suggest some European wine which has often produced very good results in cases of heart palpitations and depressive melancholy. As soon as the mandarin heard that I had arrived, he sent a chairen[^p589-57] to tell me that I must not sleep there that night, but should leave at once, | and refused to see me. He ordered the gates of the town to be locked and that they should absolutely not let me in. His order was carried out in such a way that I and those who were with me could not get in because of the precautions he had taken. The mandarin issued a further order to the inn-keeper that he must not allow me to sleep in his house that night, and that if he did let me do so he would get thirty lashes. The innkeeper, prostrate at my feet and in tears, begged me to have pity on him and leave. I replied, telling him not to be afraid that any harm would come to him on my account, for I had not come to cause trouble to anyone, but rather to do business concerning my Holy Law. And so, as the sun was going down, with myself and my people very cold and uncomfortable, I went on my way. These two mandarins must have given a secret order in all their jurisdiction to prevent anyone being converted to our Holy Law by threatening any such person with being flogged to death. I had this information from gentiles in the different places where I stopped on my way back on a journey of more than 600 leagues.

### 35 Three mistakes in this letter

Note the mistakes in this letter:

It is dated 20th January, which it should not have been. For the Very Reverend Father says above that the buzheng shi, or Royal Treasurer, had not accepted a judicial delation, because, as he says, the seals were closed. Whether they had been closed or not, the whole populace would know. There was no need for the Treasurer to announce this because the day on which seals are closed not just in the metropolises but in all the cities and government offices is published in advance throughout China to ensure that they are closed on the same day all through the kingdom. But the seals are closed not more than 13 days before the Chinese New Year, and often fewer. The Chinese New Year fell on February 10th, and the difference between that and what he says was the closing of the seals according to the buzheng shi is 21 or 22 days, <!-- PDF page 591 | book page 582 | manuscript page 1117 --> which is impossible. It is still credible that the Reverend Father wrote his letter when the seals had been opened, and the Postal Couriers were starting up again, which would have been on February 26th.[^p591-62]

The second mistake was not to give the names of the mandarins, nor what kind of mandarins they were: Tartars or Chinese, civilians or military?

The third mistake was that the Reverend Fathers have started a legal process there, and then recommended us to follow it up. But it is an extremely serious mistake in China for a missionary to accuse a mandarin to other mandarins. It is like accusing a Jew before a rabbi. The result of this miscalculation is that the business is going to be both long and difficult.

### 39 The Emperor wishes to nominate his Second Son as heir but the continue to nominate the Eighth

January 24th. On this and the preceding days, the Emperor on several occasions sounded out the gelao[^p594-70] about who should be declared heir to the throne, not gelao bothering to conceal that his own desire was that the recently deposed Second-born Son, the first and only child of his legitimate wife, should be restored. They replied that in their view it was the Eighth Son who should be raised to the succession. They were ordered to think again about the matter, and come back. Ma,[^p594-71] a Tartar gelao, came back to report that votes had been cast by the college of gelao in a written secret ballot; when the ballot papers were opened, nothing else was found among the votes of the Tartars except the word tschakuntshu, which is the character ba in Chinese, and the word both in Tartar and Chinese means “eighth.” The Emperor was not pleased to hear this, especially because in various ways he had already praised the deposed Prince, and the Prince, with his father’s knowledge, had met with the gelao and spoken in a conciliatory manner, telling them not to be afraid that he might at some time take his revenge on any of them who had previously agreed to his deposition. On this very point he had given a sworn promise to offer an amnesty. But the Emperor, sensing how difficult it was to disabuse the gelao of their impressions, did not push the matter any further. He remarked that his health was improved, that he was in the prime of life, and so there was no need to name a son as successor. Meanwhile, the gelao might like to consider what would be best for the Empire, while he would devote himself to ensuring that the deposed Prince furthered his education, and resumed a morally acceptable lifestyle. Shortly afterwards he lost his temper with the <!-- PDF page 595 | book page 586 | manuscript page 1119 --> gelao Ma, and as the rumour goes, felled him with a punch, and while he was lying there had him carried to some shrine or other where he was kept, as in a kind of honourable prison. It is quite certain that he was imprisoned, but nobody was co-opted in his place. On the following day, twenty senior mandarins presented dossiers to the Emperor begging to be released from their offices, some pleading age, others physical infirmity.

### 40 The Emperor celebrates a wedding banquet

[January] 27th. There was a wedding banquet in the Emperor’s Villa, because His Majesty was giving away[^p595-72] his daughter in marriage. On this festive occasion, the wishes of the Emperor concerning the restitution of the deposed Prince became even more evident. | When he was asked how the Main Hall was to be laid out, he gave orders for three tables and three thrones to be made ready, one for himself, one for the Empress Mother, and one for the deposed Prince. Not that he, or the Empress Dowager, or the Prince were going to process into the banquet, but that it should be clear to everyone in what position the Prince was henceforth to be held at court. Nor would I wish anyone to raise an eyebrow when I said that a table had been placed there for the Empress Mother, on the grounds that Chinese women do not appear at public banquets. Though this is indeed the Chinese custom, it is not the ancient Tartar custom, which this Emperor Kangxi observed until about the year 1690, sometimes inviting his empress wife so that they could sit together while he gave orders to the Poyambis—the domestic marshals—about domestic affairs, and what was and was not to be done. Further, one must realise that at this kind of wedding banquet, nobody goes into the room where the Emperor is feasting apart from the eunuchs and one or other of the Poyambis whom the Emperor might invite;

<!-- PDF page 596 | book page 587 -->

even less open to others is the place where the bridal couple dine. The mandarins remain in halls where they sit on the floor on their cushions and eat off low tables at which they enjoy the feast. Particularly noteworthy is that on such an occasion even the greatest mandarins in the Palace serve the less important ones,[^p596-73] who at that time are regarded as being guests and are served with food and wine. It is ordained by custom that anyone who belongs to the household on such days plays the part of a waiter, showing the splendour of his master or displaying that even he in his own person is one who serves.
