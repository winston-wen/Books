### 1 Certain matters regarding Fr. Appiani

At the start of this month, I think it would be a good idea to make a few preliminary remarks about Rev. Fr. Ludovico Appiani, and to put together a brief outline of our dealings with one another during this month. To be sure, they are hardly weighty matters, nor particularly newsworthy. But these are uncertain times, and people’s opinions are always liable to change, so I have decided to give an account of them, so as at least not to give any enemies we might one day have when I or His Reverence are dead room for making things up to suit themselves. You should know, then, that the notes I wrote to him together with his replies or requests are preserved in our archive. We had a mutual agreement that every time I wrote to him, he would return my original handwritten document to me, and, with one exception, His Lordship has so far returned them all, and often has added at the bottom his own reply.

Early in July, when the hot weather in Beijing begins in earnest, Fr. Appiani had requested that his window could be unsealed. I obtained permission for it to be done and myself did what was needed to the window frame, and provided a small curtain. In return for this kindness, which really was no trouble, he thanked me on the 28th or 29th of August, writing on a slip of paper concealed inside a book, Instructions for Priests by Toledo,[^p394-1] which he sent me, as follows:

### 2 He thanks Fr. Kilian and asks for books

Most Reverend Father

May God bestow on you every spiritual gift in return for the kindness you have shown me. I do not have a chance to thank you at this moment. May God make up for what I, your most humble servant, am unable to do. If it would not be too much trouble to you to send me some books dealing with either scholastic or controversial theology, I would be extremely grateful. That way I could do something useful with my time, and could more easily put up with the heaviness of my weary body. If there are no books I can have, I shall pursue my life in patience, that I may eventually possess my soul.

<!-- PDF page 395 | book page 386 -->

I would enjoy [Robert] Bellarmine’s Controversies,[^p395-2] and Lessius’s Opuscula on the divine attributes, on grace, etc.[^p395-3] I would particularly like Gregory of Valencia’s four volume Commentary on St. Thomas Aquinas,[^p395-4] or Conink,[^p395-5] or anyone else who comes to hand. My best regards and heavenly blessing to all the Fathers and Brothers. If any of the Reverend Fathers were to have the works of St. Teresa, or her life in Italian, French, Spanish, or Portuguese, that would be a great boon for me, if it were convenient.

To this I replied on 30th August, though my reply was not delivered until September 1st, as follows:

Most Reverend Father,

I would like to assist your Most Reverend Lordship to the very best of my ability. Here in Beijing, I neither have nor use any other theologians apart from St. Thomas with the commentaries of Cappone,[^p395-6] Cajetan,[^p395-7] and Platel.[^p395-8] Fr. Van Hamme took them with him a few months ago when he was leaving for Zhenjiang to save that and the neighbouring missions from gentile persecution. Of the famous aforementioned Doctor all that I still have is Contra Gentiles Books i–iv in Sylvester’s commentary.[^p395-9] As for moral theology, the only one I have with me <!-- PDF page 396 | book page 387 | manuscript page 1018 --> is Layman;[^p396-10] on scripture, nothing besides Pereira’s Disputationes in Genesim.[^p396-11] Among the spiritual writers, the main one I have here in the house is Bucelin,[^p396-12] and of him just the six volumes of his Spiritual Exercises of St. Ignatius. Should Your Reverence want any of these, do let me know, and I shall send them. All the other fathers of this house are in Tartary, and I would not presume to take anything from their rooms when they are not there, either for my own use or for others.

On September 1st, Fr. Appiani wrote back:

I would never doubt Your Reverence’s readiness to perform a service to me, so I am perhaps overbold in approaching you. If Your Reverence could bear to do without Pereira and one of the volumes of Bucelin I would willingly transform your loss into riches for me. As soon as I have finished them, they will make their way back to their master leaving behind only the fruit which they have conferred on their reader, and bearing a load of gratitude as a fair return to the benefactor; and, with the expectation of reading the whole of Bucelin from whom, God willing, I hope to draw the spirit of the root and the tree. God grant that these moments of time should profit me | even though many would count them as nothing but loss to me. But, if I may, I would dare to quote the Apostle, that I have counted all things as loss if only I can gain Christ;[^p396-13] nor do I count my life more valuable than myself, if only I can finish my course according to his good pleasure, who has called me into his admirable light. I beg for <!-- PDF page 397 | book page 388 --> the assistance of your prayers and sacrifices, and I hope that what seems beyond my power will nevertheless come to pass. I humbly kiss your kindly and holy hands.

### 3 Fr. Kilian sends the books to Fr. Appiani

I sent the books on September 3rd, or perhaps the 2nd, together with these few words:

Here are the books you asked for! If Your Most Reverend Lordship needs anything else, I will do my best to get it for you.

Fr. Appiani replied:

If Your Reverence could possibly find me a breviary or a book with the readings for the second nocturne of the Office of Our Lady of Mercy whose feast is on September 24th, I could copy them out, and let you have it back the same day. Please remember me in your Holy Masses that I may each day receive spiritually what I am not allowed to receive bodily. It has happened as God willed it to happen, and I shall bear God’s anger, since I have sinned against him.

On the 4th of September, I sent the breviary, with the following note:

I am sending you a breviary, so that you can copy the readings you were asking for. I would ask you to return it, since I too received it as a loan. No European news has yet arrived here. If I hear any cheerful news regarding politics or Church affairs, I shall let you know.

On the 5th of September, Fr. Appiani sent back the breviary together with my note, to which he had added:

Dear Reverend Father,

I am keeping my promise and, as you asked, am sending you back the breviary. I have copied what I needed, and I want to thank you most sincerely for the kindness with which it was brought to me. No news that I am waiting for could be sweeter to hear than that peace should come about through the power of God, and that, although I am imprisoned, as God wills, within these walls, I might hear that the Emperor has granted other missionaries permission to preach the Word of God in freedom. This is the one thing which would relieve the tedium of my time here, which might perhaps be easier to bear and less unpleasant if I could spend my time alone without the presence of guards. Yet not my will, but God’s will be done. I would with pleasure hear of peace between kings rather than of wars and victories; but I would be especially happy to know of the good health of our Pope, if anything can relieve the tedium without arousing those bodily disturbances which, I admit, are not as yet dead in me. There is one thing which I will bring to Your Reverence’s attention, <!-- PDF page 398 | book page 389 | manuscript page 1019 --> for I have no servant nor resource: the bed on which I sleep is made of clay, and has been split on every side by the heat of the fire to which it was subjected in the previous months. If I may put it like this, when the fire is set underneath, I often feel as if I am some Vulcan sitting in the smoke. The result is that I spent the winter months very miserably because I was breathing in the sulphurous coal fumes which surrounded me on all sides. The people in charge of this place don’t bother about anything except to force unpleasant things upon one (mind you, I am not complaining), with the exception of some whose voice carries no weight. If there were any way of making this bed out of bricks and doing it properly, it would be less uncomfortable. But if that is not allowed, I shall just have to be patient, and all the more so the more I am filled with sulphurous breath and cannot get through the day with meditation or reading. At the moment I am not able to meet any expenses. Perhaps God will allow me time in which to repay. May Your Reverence consider what the service of God requires; it will be enough for me to wait and find out God’s good pleasure.

### 4 The Chinese Hypocaust[^p398-14]

In this connection one has to know that in the northern provinces of China, they build a kind of oblong furnace inside their houses as a way of coping with the rigours of the winter nights. It is raised about two feet above the ground and has a smooth surface, much as in Germany where in a regular guardhouse such a bed is prepared for the soldiers to sleep on. So, this Chinese furnace is in effect a hypocaust, in the strict etymological sense of that word.[^p398-15] In the humbler dwellings, the surface of the furnace is laid with ordinary crude bricks. Since however they have many cracks and are rather inaccurately fitted together, the whole surface is then covered with packed clay. | Over time this develops cracks, which are open to the fire and smoke in the hollow space below. It is this kind of bed that Fr. Appiani is talking of having repaired, and would like others to pay for. I did so from my own funds in October, which is actually the usual time for repairing furnaces. It was helpful of Fr. Appiani to think of reminding me in good time.

### 5 Some letters of Fr. Kilian to Fr. Appiani, and from Fr. Appiani to Fr. Kilian

On September 6th I wrote to Fr. Appiani:

We have heard news that the good Fr. Bouvet together with his horse had a dangerous fall more than two hundred leagues from here, and because he had been <!-- PDF page 399 | book page 390 --> injured, he is being brought back to Beijing.[^p399-16] The rest of his party are continuing their journey. I commend them all to Your Lordship’s devotion.

Fr. Appiani replied:

If God hears the prayers of sinners, then I most willingly offer to God my small mite of prayers and petitions coming from this cold place for the health of a labourer endowed with so many virtues as is Fr. Bouvet. I will pray even more fervently if I can because of the great benefit which such ministers confer upon this poorest of missions. But since I rightly have little confidence in my own prayers, I join them with due affection to the burning prayers of Your Reverence and the other Fathers, so that when God looks upon yours, He may not despise mine either. Amen.

Since the days as the autumn equinox approached were getting shorter, and so in the mornings and evenings before and after sunset there were some hours which were just empty and useless for Fr. Appiani as he sat in darkness, he requested that our Brother Bernard Rhod,[^p399-17] who looked after things in our house, should provide some candles for him. The brother did not dare give them to him, since the servants told him that in accordance with the custom of the country there are some specific items which may not be given to those in custody, such as knives, ropes, wine, and, among other things, candles. I was afraid that Fr. Appiani would not take this refusal at all well; so, I had some bought for him, and ordered that they should be given to the guards with the request that, if the regulations allowed, they would let Lord Appiani have them, but if not, they should keep them for their own use. I continued to do this until the time came when permission was given by the Ministry to provide oil so that the guards could have light during the night.

On September 22nd Fr. Appiani wrote to me as follows:

First of all, I am sending back the Bucelin volume. May God grant that from the feast which he has prepared and made ready for ascetics, one little morsel may enter the substance of my soul, so that nourished by this feast it may be freed from the salty infection with which it is afflicted.[^p399-18] I hope for the most <!-- PDF page 400 | book page 391 | manuscript page 1020 --> fertile outcome from the supreme Giver of good things if the prayers of Your Reverence reach him, since in return for your generosity God will grant you no small reward, as tomorrow’s gospel clearly says. I shall rejoice from my heart if I hear better news of Your Reverence’s health than what I have received so far. If I may say so, I would make bold to tell you to put a limit on the work you do. In this volume I came across two pictures which I am keeping to put into the later volumes as bookmarks and reminders.

Thus, he writes. At this time, I came down with some sickness or other, with the result that we were less in touch with one another. But the reader may be sure that what I have attributed above to Fr. Appiani was written by him exactly as it appears, and if it is at times obscure or over-involved, I did not think it should be altered, so that everything would correspond exactly with the original autograph versions, as I hereby witness that it does. Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

Now I turn to other things which took place in this month and which directly or indirectly are related to the Acts and the Beijing Fathers connection with the mission.

### 6 Harassment of the Fathers by Chinese officials

On September 1st, Fr. Dominique Parrenin, the Superior of our residence within the Saffron Wall, who at that time was staying in the Emperor’s summer quarters, provided Fr. Kilian who had inquired of him in August an explanation for the harassment which our French Fathers (Baborier in the province of Shaanxi, Noëlas[^p400-19] in Huguang, and D’Entrecolles in Jiangxi) had had to endure at the hands of the officials following their acceptance of the permit. The explanation was that there was a widespread | story that some missionaries who had been expelled by the Emperor had gone into hiding inside China, and that a search was underway throughout all the provinces for Fr. Cróquer.[^p400-20] The officials called in all the permits for scrutiny, and those belonging to the above Fathers were found to contain a mistake to do with their names. The mandarins seized their chance to harass them with summonses, detentions, and other inconveniences. In addition, Fr. Noëlas had still more serious difficulties to deal with, because at that very time Rev. Fr. Müllener, a diocesan priest and missionary <!-- PDF page 401 | book page 392 --> from Propaganda, who was being sent by the officials of Canton to the province of Sichuan, evaded the Tribunal’s agents who were escorting him.[^p401-21] As for Fr. Baborier, Parrenin wrote that he had been forced to leave Hanzhong to go to Xi’an fu, a round trip of forty days and more, to show his diploma to the zongdu, or Chief Military Prefect.[^p401-22] This man recognized the seal of the Marshal of the Royal Household in the capital, but was still unwilling to release the Father until he had consulted the Ministry of Rites in Beijing about the genuineness of the permit.

### 7 A mistake about the Fathers’ names was the cause of the harassment and danger

When the matter did come before the Ministry, it was found that the Father’s name had been entered in the lists as Bu Jianian instead of simply Bu Jia as it should have been.[^p401-23] So the Ministry ordered an answer to be sent that nobody of that name was known to the Ministry. Fr. Parrenin prevented this from happening, since he had already been informed about the scribal error.

However, Fr. Noëlas was summoned from Anluo shi, where his mission was, to the provincial capital, Wuchang fu, where he had been accused by minor officials of trying to pass off someone else’s permit, which was assumed to belong to one of those who had been expelled the previous year. Like it or not, the Father had to remain in the provincial capital where they intended to force him by yet another stratagem to hand over his permit and have it sent to the officials in Anluo shi, and through them to the provincial governor. However, the Father persistently refused to hand it over. He stayed in Wuchang fu for several months, and all he would say was that if his permit had to be shown to the provincial governor, he would hand it to him with his own hands to be examined; that is just what he did. In the end the whole case was dropped, and the Father was sent back to his church with his honour intact. Rev. Fr. Müllener’s praiseworthy decision was a great help in all this, for he was led by <!-- PDF page 402 | book page 393 | manuscript page 1021 --> remorse to return to Wuchang fu and present himself to the mandarins from whose custody he had absconded.

As for Fr. D’Entrecolles, that a mistake had been made about his name came to the notice of the Prince, who accordingly reprimanded the li bu (Ministry of Rites), whose officials were extremely upset by the reprimand. It might seem in Europe that to get a single letter wrong is a trivial mistake, but in China it could be enough to cost a magistrate his job, or at least a whole year’s salary. So, the li bu apologised to the Prince in person, but it made every effort to hunt down the source of the mistake. They discovered that the Prince’s secretary had written the name in Manchu; it had then been put into Chinese phonetically, not according to meaning. It had been written as hong in Manchu, and this had appeared in the permit as the character 弘 whereas in the records of the Ministry of Rites it appeared as the character 洪;[^p402-24] these two characters are very different despite the fact that they are pronounced in exactly the same way.

Also, on September 1st. A similar explanation for the cases of harassment is given by Fr. Antonio de Magalhães writing from Songjiang to Very Rev. Fr. Provincial, José Soares, as follows:

Firstly, When Fr. Giovanni Laureati, my predecessor, had come to this house, on the 24th of the said first month, I received an order from the zhifu and from the buzheng shi of Suzhou[^p402-25] which enjoined that by order of the zongdu they were to inspect our piaos to see whether or not they were genuine. We immediately presented them to the zhifu. When they had seen them, we went away thinking that the matter was all over. On the fourth of the second month Fr. Laureati left for Fujian. Then on the 8th I received another order that our piaos must be sent to Suzhou to be given to the zongdu. When I replied that the said Fr. Laureati could not go since he was no longer in this city, there then came another order addressed to the members of this church saying that they should not have allowed the wretched Father to escape since he was no longer around and must be regarded as a fugitive. Then, two or three days later, there came yet another | order for me to be taken to Suzhou unless the said Father should make an appearance. This caused me a great deal of trouble, and also to the Christians, since to escape this, it was necessary to go to the yamen of the mandarins where we had to listen to much bad-mouthing of the Lord Patriarch and our Holy Law; after having such a long peace, we now have nothing but trouble. My piao was eventually taken to Suzhou by Fr. Manuel Mendes along with his own and, so far as I can see, I will have not a few further difficulties over it.

### 8 The neophytes argue about the Lord Patriarch’s regulations; fewer baptisms

Secondly, as for liking the regulations[^p403-26] that the Lord Patriarch has published, many Christians have grown lukewarm, and do nothing but complain about them because they do not believe they perform superstitious rites or that their intention is superstitious.

Thirdly, the number of those coming to receive baptism is much less than in previous years. There have been several attacks on our churches, much as happened in the time of Fr. Laureati, even by the very same people or at least I suspect they are. I constantly recommend to my Christians to pray without cease for the peace of the mission because I know this is the way to remedy the situation.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

This has already been noted last year in its proper place, and will soon come up again on the 8th of this month, in the letter of the same Father.

### 9 The missionary called Ai

On September 2nd Fr. Barelli replied to Fr. Kilian’s question: who on earth might be this missionary called Ai, for whom there was a search all over China last year? It was known that Fr. Cróquer had broken his journey for some time and for that reason had been pursued by the officials and sent away under escort by their agents, but the name didn’t match. Since the Father’s reply is brief, I shall give it in its entirety:

Fr. Contancin, who joins me in sending Your Reverence our best wishes for your health and for the good of this mission, sent me these two documents for Your Reverence; if you need any more assistance from him, he is entirely at your disposal. I know nothing at all about the return (or rather turning back) of Fr. Cróquer.[^p403-27] A man called Ai was indeed being hunted in this city, and a lot of <!-- PDF page 404 | book page 395 | manuscript page 1022 --> trouble he caused me last year; he is Ai Yuhan,[^p404-28] otherwise known as Rev. Fr. Fray Juan Astudillo of the Order of Preachers.[^p404-29] I commend myself, etc.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

The two documents which Fr. Barelli mentions here are given below: one of them deals with the circumstances surrounding the events in Hangzhou, when the Emperor granted permits to 3 Jesuits but refused them to 8 Dominicans and 3 clerics. Fr. Kilian had sought an explanation of these events; the other has to do with a case from His Lordship the Bishop of Conon, which Fr. ### 10 The good offices which the Beijing Fathers performed for the Dominican Fathers

Here is the first one:

In my short account of the events which took place when we received the Emperor’s permit in the city of Hangzhou, I think I covered the main points of interest, but I think there are one or two other things I should also mention. First, I witness to the fact that in the city of Hangzhou I heard Rev. Fr. Bouvet more than once urge us warmly with the following charitable words: “Fathers! Let us do everything we can for these eleven missionaries, and make every effort to treat them well. Doing so will be most pleasing to God, and in this way, we may bring his favour upon the affairs of our mission.” And in fact, to say nothing of all the other things I saw, heard, and witnessed, when the Eldest Prince asked whether there were any other missionaries, and to what order they belonged, and whether the Dominican fathers wish to accept the Emperor’s diploma, Fr. Bouvet at once fell to his knees and said that all eleven missionaries eagerly wished for that permit. The Prince replied in a very firm tone: “It’s no use; so far as those belonging to the order of St. Peter[^p404-30] are concerned, the matter has already been dealt with; they will not receive a diploma. It has been decided. That’s that.” From that time forward our Fathers did not dare to query any of those points, but they still strongly argued on behalf of the Dominican Fathers. Fr. Jartoux said many things in their favour, and praised their order at length. That, in my opinion, is why the Prince sent Fr. Bouvet and Fr. Jartoux | to see whether there might be some justification for giving them the permit. For instance, was there not perhaps someone among them who had kept to <!-- PDF page 405 | book page 396 --> the practice of the old missionaries? Wasn’t there anyone who wished to follow that practice in future, or at least to remain in his own church following the religious life in private?

Secondly, I testify that as soon as we appeared before the Emperor’s Eldest Son, the Prince, speaking of us to Fr. Bouvet, asked: “What about these? What do they think?” Fr. Bouvet replied: “Prince, they are of the same mind as those who were in the city of Suzhou.” Whereupon the Prince asked us about the practice we used to observe.

### 11 Two of our people appeal against the regulations of the Lord Patriarch

Thirdly, I testify that I was forced into the position of leaving China, remaining but without preaching the Gospel, or of appealing against the regulations of the Patriarch of Antioch. Since the first two were fraught with difficulties, and went quite counter to the good of souls, whereas on the other side there were many legitimate reasons for appealing against the regulations of the Most Illustrious Patriarch of Antioch, I followed this last path. At this moment I am so far from regretting that choice that I have often in the past thanked God, thank him now, and will continue to thank him for the fact that I accepted that good advice.

In the city of Shaojing in the province of Zhejiang, 27 August 1708, Cyr Contancin, sj.

To which Fr. Barelli added in his own hand: I testify to the same, Hangzhou, capital of the province of Zhejiang, 1 October 1708, Agostino Barelli, sj.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

### 12 The ignorance of Bishop Maigrot

On this day, 19 August 1708, Thadeus Zhou,[^p405-31] one of the prefects of the church in the city of Tingzhou[^p405-32] in the province of Fujian, told me that when Bishop Maigrot was staying in the city for a few days in the year of the Lord 1704, he wished to offer Mass in the chapel dedicated to the Blessed Virgin, which is inside our house. But first, he wanted to have removed the tablet headed by the four characters 敬迓[^p405-33] 天庥, Jing ya <!-- PDF page 406 | book page 397 --> tian xiu, which mean “With reverence approach the Heavenly Protectress.” In the presence of many Christians, two scholars of the rank called by the Chinese xiucai[^p406-34] came up to him and asked His Lordship why he wished this tablet to be removed. When His Lordship gave the inscription an interpretation that was quite wrong, the literati pointed out that His Lordship had not understood the inscription very well, and that the meaning he gave it bore no relation to the characters, so there was no need to take it down. They themselves explained it to him in a Catholic sense, with the result that Bishop Maigrot had no further objection to offer, just slightly nodding his head without saying a word.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

### 13 The Lord Patriarch issues a decree against two of our people

On September 5th, Fr. da Silva from Nanjing sent a reply to the inquiries which Rev. Fr. Visitor had made on 7 August 1708 about the decree which the Lord Patriarch had issued against Fathers Grimaldi and Pereira in 1706.[^p406-35] The gist of the reply is contained in the following words:

I should add that when the Lord Patriarch was in Nanjing, I sought him out in his room in the Dongtang (the church which at the time the Lord Patriarch had bought from the Franciscans)[^p406-36] and requested him to revoke the decree against the two Fathers. He answered that such a petition was the responsibility of the Vice-Provincial, who was the Superior. On another occasion when Fr. Monteiro the Vice-Provincial spoke to him about the same matter he clearly replied that it was necessary first for the two Fathers, Grimaldi and Pereira, to humiliate themselves by requesting a revocation in writing, and that then he <!-- PDF page 407 | book page 398 | manuscript page 1023 --> would consider whether he had it in his heart to exercise mercy. As soon as we heard this response, we completely gave up this business since we had nothing more to say about the matter.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

On September 6th, something new and unexpected happened for the Jesuits in Beijing! A letter turned up from two Capuchin Fathers | written in Lhasa in the kingdom of the Dalai Lama on 11 January 1708 to a missionary in Xi’an fu.[^p407-37] It should be pointed out firstly that it was not the original but a copy which arrived in Beijing; secondly, it did not bear the address of any missionary to whom it was being sent—it was addressed simply to the missionary of the place whom an Armenian merchant based in Lhasa had met there, according to what the Fathers wrote. Thirdly, it is however absolutely clear that this letter was delivered to the Franciscan missionaries in Xi’an fu rather than to Fr. Domenge, sj,[^p407-38] who also lives there, as anyone reading it will easily discover. Fourthly, a second letter later arrived from the same people. Both letters were written in Latin, and I give them here word for word, straightforwardly, and without omission or alteration.

### 14 The letter from the Capuchin missionaries written from Lhasa in the Kingdom of Tibet

The first reads as follows:

Reverend and most esteemed Father, It happens that some people who have arrived here in Lhasa from Xining[^p407-39] have brought us news of your stay in that place, and that you will arrive there as soon as possible to build a church, and so will be our neighbour. So, although we are totally ignorant of your Christian name, your family name, and to what nation Your Reverence belongs, we still thought it an excellent idea to convey to you our most dutiful respects, since we are cut off from all other ways of communicating and deprived of any advice or dealings with Christians and Europeans. It will be a great consolation to us if

<!-- PDF page 408 | book page 399 -->

Your Reverence could establish some link with us. Very Reverend Father, we were six missionaries chosen by the Sacred Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith to proclaim the most holy gospel of our Lord Jesus Christ in the kingdoms of Tibet.[^p408-40] Four of us were Italians and two Frenchmen.[^p408-41] After two of us had died on the journey, one Italian and one Frenchman, and another Italian returned home, those who were left, two Italians and one Frenchman, continued our journey through the Indies. In the kingdom of Bengal, we left behind one of our number who was ill. After unbelievable hardships, illnesses, crises, and sufferings endured throughout a very rough journey over difficult roads, through mountains I would have called all but impassable, travelling on foot from Patna to Lhasa because of shortage of money, I myself and a Frenchman called Fr. François-Marie de Tours arrived here in Lhasa on the very day of Pentecost last year, 1707.[^p408-42] Having finally reached this place, we decided to stay here in order to learn the language of this country, and, if Almighty God[^p408-43] should grant us his favours for the future, we can set ourselves to preach the Gospel. What we do not know for sure is whether we will be able to remain in this country for any length of time, since we are almost bereft of all our funds because of the unspeakable costs of such a long and difficult journey. We do not have enough left to support ourselves for another year. So, if no other funds reach us in good time, I think that we shall, after all our great labours and calamities, be compelled to abandon the mission and all that God has done up until this time.

### 15 They request some financial assistance, and letters of recommendation to the King

Meanwhile, here we are in Lhasa, dressed in our own Capuchin habits as we have been since we first arrived. So far nobody has made any adverse comment on this, nor asked any question about the reason why we might have come. Rather, we are held in reasonably good esteem by everyone, because, although we live in poverty without any pomp or luxury, which in any case would be impossible because our money has already been spent, nevertheless we can practice medicine, and even in our destitution we are still able to offer it free to everyone, and for this reason they treat us with some affection and equal respect. Everyone calls us and thinks of us as European Lamas. So, Most Reverend Father, if God, in his Divine Providence (which is our only hope) comes to our aid and so disposes that we are to remain here, we beg and entreat Your Reverence that if you are in any position to help us in deed or with your advice, not to delay, and in particular to help us with a letter of recommendation to the king here and the Great Lama. We are aware how well Your Reverence has been accepted and how high you stand in the estimation of the Emperor and the dignitaries of China. So, if through Your Reverence’s offices, we could have some letter of recommendation from the Emperor himself | to this king and the Great Lama, asking him to grant us a site on which we could build a chapel, I think it would be greatly to God’s glory. Meanwhile I ask Your Reverence graciously to accept our humble respects which in this letter I and my companion offer you, humbly commending ourselves and our mission to your holy sacrifices and prayers. ### 17 Observations on this letter

1. The original letter from the beginning had no seal. 2. These two Fathers still write in a very confused manner about the kingdom, the city, the palace, and the king of the place where they hope to preach the Gospel. This should be put down to their being just beginners in the language, and to the short time they have been in the country.

3. They say that they have several times been invited by the king and have spoken with him. Here their lack of experience is clear: to begin with, that country does not have a king, but a ruler whom they believe to be the Living God, and whose rule is not on the model of kings, but rather cares for spiritual things <!-- PDF page 412 | book page 403 --> in an ecclesiastical way. Temporal matters are entrusted to some governor; in addition, there is a war minister. The ruler himself remains in the Holy of Holies, so to speak, and he is honoured as a god not only by his own subjects but by everyone in the length and breadth of Tartary, even by those who at present are in power in China. Nobody is admitted to see or converse with him who has not first adored him as a god. It is safe to assume that had he summoned these Capuchin Fathers he would not have dispensed them from this act of worship, and if they were summoned by anyone called “Great” by that people, they must have been summoned by the governor of temporal affairs, or by the war minister, whose dignity they had not yet learned to distinguish from that of a king.

### 18 About the Dalai Lama or God Incarnate and Lord of Lhasa

In any case, at that time there was no such Lord or God Incarnate in that country, for the Emperor Kangxi had ordered that he be brought to Beijing; but when he had passed through the Great Wall he died from the journey, a year and more before the Reverend Fathers reached Lhasa.[^p412-49] That God incarnate is called the Dalai Lama,[^p412-50] the Governor for Temporal Affairs is called the Depa,[^p412-51] and the War Minister is the Dalai Khan;[^p412-52] these are their fixed official titles.

4. It is no less impossible to obtain the help and referrals which the Reverend Fathers want the Beijing Jesuits to procure from the Tartaro-Chinese Emperor than as it is to preach the gospel in that kingdom. How could a pseudo-god tolerate that the true God should be preached, thereby annihilating his own divinity? Or how could an Antichrist allow the preaching of Christ, involving the extermination of his own professed divinity? I do not wish to debate the point, for I am well aware that where human means fall short the powers of the Providence of God are more than sufficient to vanquish his enemy whose strength is in arms, and overturn all his vessels so that they yield the rich spoils of the Faith for the poor Capuchin Fathers. As for the state of affairs which makes it impossible for the Beijing Jesuits to provide the help they are looking for, a number of things must be kept in mind, otherwise Europe might allow the <!-- PDF page 413 | book page 404 | manuscript page 1026 --> calumnious inference to be made that they who had begun the work of spreading the Gospel out of envy pretended they were unable to help their fellows and support them when they were in danger. |

### 19 Who are entitled to be called lamas in Western Tartary?

In Fr. Athanasius Kircher’s China Illustrated,[^p413-53] the reader may see what kind of men are the lamas, the popes of Tartary; their hierarchy; and the cycles of re-incarnation involving their head, the Dalai Lama. There the reader may also learn that when the Tartars defeated China in the war, the lamas came in along with the Tartars themselves; friends, therefore, but friends to be feared, since the whole of the more western part of Tartary regards their nod as a command from Heaven. To such a degree is this true that if the Dalai Lama were to give it, that very day hundreds and hundreds of thousands of men would take up arms against China. For this reason, the Eastern Tartars who are masters of China today have allowed the lamas to wear the yellow robe which during the Yuan dynasty was granted to them. They have honoured the lamas not just with the imperial colour which blazes out from their robes; they have enriched them with many monasteries and rents. About 28 years ago it happened that a certain huductu, or archpope,[^p413-54] came to Beijing with several lamas saying that he had been sent from Lhasa by the congregation of huductus to obtain permission from His Majesty to solicit a certain sum in alms from among the Tartars in China to pay for a large and indeed quite extraordinary sacrifice which was to be conducted there. They claimed that the Dalai Lama had given his consent since it was for something which fell under the responsibilities he bore on behalf of the human race. The Dalai Lama had decided to return to heaven in order to wipe away the wearinesses of his life on earth with invisible delights. He would renew his youth with a second incarnation to be announced to the world. However, <!-- PDF page 414 | book page 405 --> since there was good reason to be fearful that the absence of their God could bring down nothing but evil, he had decided that the archpopes and the entire order of lamas should prepare a sacrifice in the odour of sweetness, so that, refreshed, he could put up with remaining on earth for some years longer. The Emperor, who was still only a youth, heard this, but was sharp enough in mind to smell fraud. Still, he preferred to lose money rather than to annoy the lamas, so he gave permission to collect the alms. He contributed ten thousand ounces of silver himself, and the dowager empress, his grandmother,[^p414-55] gave a thousand ounces of gold. More and more lamas flocked to Beijing, and many abandoned monasteries belonging to the bonzes[^p414-56] were assigned to the lamas. I do not know who made the suggestion, but it was judged necessary to elect one of the lamas from those in Beijing to deal with all business matters regarding the lamas. The Emperor chose one, giving him wide powers over all the monasteries, and gave him a seal investing him with his powers. This head pope[^p414-57] himself became a rebel not long afterwards, supporting an armed rebellion against the Emperor, as I shall presently describe.

### 20 A quarrel between two Tartar princes. The loser flees to the Emperor[^p414-58]

It happened that two Tartar princes led their followers onto the battlefield where they engaged, and one prince killed the other. The dead man’s brother had become a lama as a young child, and was now also an archpope.

<!-- PDF page 415 | book page 406 | manuscript page 1027 -->

Now he laid down his mitre[^p415-59] and robe, and set out on a soldier’s career, not without success. He routed his brother’s killer, recovered the hereditary lands of his father, and drove the enemy out of his own. The man died shortly afterwards, leaving a pupil in the tutelage of the other archpope of the lamas. The ex-lama, having achieved victory, and now wishing to extend his power more widely, tried to tie people to himself, sometimes by fear, sometimes by friendship, offering them the hope of conquering China if they remained faithful to him. At the same time the other lama, tutor of the pupil, fled to the Emperor, asking for his patronage and also for permission to purchase arms with which to protect the infant prince who had been unjustly expelled. The Emperor reflected that now there were two lamas who had a pretext for openly raising an army, and it might turn out that, once they had armed and gained the loyalty of the remaining Tartar princes, they would suddenly join their by then extremely formidable forces and invade the empire. So, in order to safeguard his own position, he accepted the lama who was seeking to be his client once he had sent a modest army to his aid, appointing his own brother as commander in chief, and appointed to his staff the Eldest Prince, whose mother was a concubine, so that from his early youth he could learn the rudiments of warfare at first hand. |

The expedition did not turn out terribly well. There was a battle; neither side gained all it wanted. The client survived, but the ex-lama was victorious in the field, increased his reputation and his forces, and grew so powerful that the Emperor decided that he ought to take the field in person against this enemy. While he was assembling his soldiers, and slowly collecting all the supplies required to lead an army into such remote parts, the archpope who had his seal of approval hurriedly left Beijing and joined up with the ex-lama to become his future personal advisor. Eventually a double army left Beijing with the Emperor; a third army had already been formed in Tartary from the Tartar group known as Kalkases, or Halhazes.[^p415-60] The Emperor left the capital before the snows had melted, and with an immense effort on the part of both cavalry and foot-soldiers they travelled three hundred French leagues to catch up with the ex-lama. Such a feat took him completely by surprise and he fled, only to meet with the other army. He was wounded, and in the following year, having given up all hope of recovering his former glory, swallowed poison and died.

<!-- PDF page 416 | book page 407 -->

### 21 The good consequence of the Tartar War

In the years that followed, the victorious Emperor took the defeated leader’s son, who was hardly more than a child, from the Muslim client prince and demanded that his daughter, who was in Lhasa and betrothed, should be handed over to him by the Depa, the Governor for Temporal Affairs; finally, he apprehended the lama who was the seal-bearer. The son and daughter had been born to the ex-lama before he gave up the habit of the order; although lamas are not permitted to marry, the older ones are allowed to maintain concubines, not in the monasteries but in tents in the countryside, with due care for decorum. The Emperor spared their lives, but the rebel archpope was taken to a military parade-ground in Beijing and all the lamas in the city were forced to watch the spectacle of him being slowly hacked to pieces and killed.

Immediately after that, the Emperor, accompanied by large forces, went to Tartary on the pretext of going hunting, but in fact in order to entice their desert princes to swear allegiance to him. He publicised the event quite effectively by sending ambassadors everywhere and by making speeches himself, armed with breastplate and helmet. This summer, all the princes of those peoples assembled from every direction. A special ambassador even came from Lhasa to the Emperor, one of the highest archpopes. Unbelievably, at his approach the Emperor actually left his tent in procession and welcomed the man with astonishing graciousness. He, in order to explain the purpose of his coming, said: “The Dalai Lama, soothed by that great sacrifice and by the kindness of His Majesty, has already postponed his return to heaven for sixteen years altogether; but, now that peace has been restored to Tartary, and seeing that the wisdom of the Emperor has bound all the princes to him in friendship, there is no reason for him to delay his ascension to the upper realms. But so as not to deprive the Emperor altogether of his assistance, he sends his portrait for the Emperor to have in place of himself, as an immediate source of help. It is not to be removed from its wrapping until his own departure has been confirmed. It would therefore be just and good if the Emperor took care not to break the seal of the wrapping until eight moons have passed, after which the Dalai Lama would impart his blessing from heaven.” His Majesty accepted the gift, and sent the ambassador on his way, accompanied by dignitaries to escort him with honour as far as the borders of Tibet.

Hardly two days after the archpope ambassador had left the Emperor’s camp, His Majesty’s scouts, who had previously been sent on a very long journey towards Samarkand, came back. Asked what intelligence they brought, they replied that 40 days previously they had been in the camp at Chagar-arptan belonging to a relative of the ex-lama who had poisoned himself two years before. A single archpope sent from Lhasa had arrived there, and came in, solemnly <!-- PDF page 417 | book page 408 | manuscript page 1028 --> dressed, and with a great display of mourners announced the death of the Dalai Lama. The Emperor gnashed his teeth with rage at the realisation of how badly he had been deceived. He summoned the princes of Tartary who were guests in the camp and ordered the scouts to relay the same message to them. He denounced the deceptions of the lamas, and before he did anything else sent his speediest horsemen to bring back the archpope who was hastening on his way home. Then, turning to the princes, he said, “Since it is now clear that the Dalai Lama is dead, what is to prevent me opening the lama’s wrapping to see if there is yet another hidden | layer of deception?” The package was brought, the seal broken, a great deal of paper unfolded, and behold, in the middle of it was found a small clay image, with the head severed from the body. Angrily, the Emperor threw it away, swearing that he would never have anything to do with this monstrous headless corpse.

### 22 How the Tartars found a new god to replace the previous one who had died

The ambassador, dragged back from his homeward journey, readily confessed to the deception, begging pardon for himself on the grounds that the senate of archpopes, and in particular the Depa,[^p417-61] had compelled him to do it. The Emperor did not want to cause any more pain or upset to the Tartary princes, since they are deeply devoted to such men, regarding them as living gods; neither did he anticipate any further trouble from the wretched lama. But he did have him questioned in detail, and he admitted that it was the lamas’ custom to designate one as Dalai Lama to be adored by all as a true, living god. When he dies, the custom is to conceal his death while a male child is selected who is to be brought up in a monastery in Lhasa, and given divine honours as a reborn Dalai Lama. He is trained in the ways which publicly and with dignity foster belief in his divinity; when he is fit to be seen, he is presented in public both in the court and the temple. It usually takes 16 to 18 years for the formation of this boy-god and a new god-star[^p417-62] had not yet been trained, ready to be produced.[^p417-63] As soon as the lama had completed his embassy to the Emperor, he returned home.

<!-- PDF page 418 | book page 409 -->

From that time on, the Emperor did nothing to detract from the public veneration of the lamas which had been his custom hitherto, but neither did he cease to make some shrewd inquiries into what the archpopes in Lhasa might be plotting with the young god-star. Among other things, he intercepted a letter written by the Depa to the Prince of the Sao Dazi[^p418-64] who was aware of the secret, in which the Depa was complaining about the vices of the young Dalai Lama, who was refusing to study the books of his elders, and showing an adolescent eagerness for the delights of the world and the company of women.[^p418-65]

It happened also that when in the kingdom of Tibet, the Dalai Khan (Commander-in-chief) died, the Depa questioned the assumption that he should be succeeded by his son; since the new Dalai Lama was inclined to favour the Depa’s faction, the young Dalai Khan, to whom the flower of the army had remained loyal, killed the Depa after a fight, and took the Dalai Lama prisoner. More than five years earlier the Dalai Khan wrote to the Chinese Emperor saying that this man was not the true Dalai Lama reborn from heaven, but some kind of substitute pseudo-god about whose birth a mistake had been made. He observed none of the decencies appropriate to a god: he drank, gambled, hunted on horseback, killed men and violated virgins, and gave an archpope leave to associate with whores, putting all this down openly in writing. Could His Majesty, in virtue of his supreme power, please give judgement in the affair? The Emperor was absolutely delighted at being acknowledged as Lord by the Dalai Khan; he took advice from the princes in Beijing, and wrote back to say that in such a difficult and distant business he was unable to give judgement; let them bring the man to Beijing and then judgement would be given in accordance with justice. Without delay, the Dalai Khan made two attempts to arrest the false god and bring him to China. Once, because armed men had assembled to protect him from ambush along the way, they had to retreat; a second time, they were prevented by a lama stronghold along the route. On the third occasion the Emperor had sent military assistance, and the Dalai Lama was brought back far enough to have entered through the Great Wall, but there he died, and <!-- PDF page 419 | book page 410 | manuscript page 1029 --> the suspicion was that he had swallowed poison. The Emperor two years and a bit later, as an interim measure, appointed one of the archpopes to look after religious affairs under guidance, until there were clearer indications of the birth of another Dalai Lama.

In this same year of 1709 which I am now describing, the lamas came in haste with the news that from cracks in rocks the voice of a child was heard: after a procession had gone to the place and after the archpopes had been taken there and access had been gained by means of an iron bar and a great deal of labour, a newborn boy was found there, who beyond all doubt was the true God reborn on earth; they wished the Emperor to proclaim him as such.[^p419-66] The Emperor replied that he congratulated the lamas; he rejoiced in the birth of the godmade-man, and when he had made sure of the facts he would present him to the people to be adored. So that the facts could be truly established, they should go away, diligently care for the boy, and after | he had reached the age of two they should bring him gently to the court in Beijing so that he could be invested with divinity. At the same time the Emperor also appointed one of the leading men to go to Lhasa, to find out more clearly what the situation was.

Perhaps I shall seem to have to be telling a fairy story or something even further from the truth when I say that the Reverend Capuchin Fathers who are now in Lhasa have said not a word about any of this. No doubt they will say more when they understand more. On the other hand, neither in Lhasa nor in Beijing are these things public knowledge. I have told the plain truth about them, so that it might be clear how inappropriate was their request to the Beijing Jesuits for a letter of recommendation as a result of which a church and a small monastery might be built there for the Reverend Capuchin Fathers. This task would not defeat God’s omnipotence, but it is certainly beyond our powers! With all our hearts we wish that what the fathers intend for the conversion of that kingdom should happily come to pass, and we commend it in our prayers to God. But, to speak in human terms, it cannot succeed so long as a false god reigns there; perhaps, as I said, as things stand the kingdom might fall, that room might be made for Christ the true Son of God.

### 24 A letter of Fr. Laureati to Fr. Vice- Provincial concerning the trouble to his mission caused by the decree of the Lord Patriarch

May God spare the Lord Patriarch, whose decisions have caused me so many troubles; were I to count them, they would be more than the days and hours of my life until now when I write these words. ### 25 The wretched state of the Christians in the province of Fujian

The very day on which I arrived at the capital, two Christians were ordered by the mandarins to be beaten, as if such a crime would be a suitable welcome back for the missionaries about whose exile the Mandarins had been so pleased, and whose houses they had taken over. It would have been all over with the Fujian mission had the imperial permit not been my bodyguard, which enabled me to resist their rage and force them to restore the honour of our Holy Law. It is not easy to spell out in just how utterly miserable a state I found this province. Everywhere the Christians were hounded by the mandarins, treated by their relatives as though they were a disgrace to their families and put in chains by the agents of the tribunals until they paid in silver to be set free. With fists, sticks, and knives wives were forced by their husbands and children by their parents to abandon their faith. At crossroads Christian teaching was mocked, and everywhere denounced | as false. If the sacred images were not being profaned, they were being defaced by contemptuous hands.

### 26 A most serious persecution

But all this counts for nothing when we come to consider our churches. Apart from one or two, they were targets for the abuse of a deranged mob; they were stoned, the roofs were shaken, the walls were even covered with obscene verses or smeared with mud. In front of the doors and in the very entrance itself excrement was thrown on the ground as though it were the most filthy sewer of the city. The Christians were not permitted to utter a word of complaint; if they ventured to reproach the people who were fouling the place, they were punished as though they had committed a crime. In Xiamen and Chuanzhou rocks rained down day and night; it was not safe for those who were guarding the church to sleep or eat inside. In the village of Guangtan the guards were maltreated with a stick, and utensils removed from the house. In the suburb of Fuqing xian the Christians were thrown out in the middle of their prayers by people with stones; the church in Fu’an was closed by the mandarins, and the Christians forbidden to enter it.

The church in Funing zhou suffered the same misfortune. In Ningde, statues were thrown down, and the church turned into the home of a mandarin; in Luoyuan the gates were closed by the Prefect, and the Christians in the middle of their prayers were severely beaten up by a raging mob; a statue of the Most Blessed Virgin Mary was kicked to pieces. In Changluo and Tingzhou fu the inscription in gilded letters Tianzhu tang[^p422-70] was pulled down by order of the officials, and a catechist was publicly subjected to 20 strokes of the bamboo, and everything inside the property was looted. To our churches, honour, utensils, golden inscriptions, and statues have been restored; the pagans have been expelled, insults and abuses have been everywhere forbidden by severe laws, and indeed more serious insults have incurred public criticism. ### 27 Fr. Laureati gets back Bishop Maigrot’s church

A large part of my difficulty lay in trying to recover Bishop Maigrot’s church. How many prayers, how much effort, how much money would that cost? But all this lies sometime in the future.[^p422-71] At the moment, there is ingratitude right at my doorstep. The Very Rev. Fr. Le Blanc from the neighbouring city of Xinghou fu was urging me constantly in letters to recover his church. But once <!-- PDF page 423 | book page 414 | manuscript page 1031 --> that had been achieved, although he had been told that it had been done both by me and by a servant whom I had sent as an eye-witness to inform him, even so, in letters which he wrote after the church had been recovered, he did not deign to write a single “Thank you,” avoiding the issue by writing about other things instead. Can Your Reverence not guess why he treats me like that? It’s to make sure that the Jesuits do not have any testimony to their co-operation from those Reverend Gentlemen! So again, when I had arranged it so that he recovered everything in the province of Yunnan at no cost to himself,[^p423-72] that same Very Reverend Fr. Le Blanc, writing to me, only expressed solicitude for my health; in another letter, which was never to leave the hands of Fr. Bénard,[^p423-73] he wished him to thank me by word of mouth, as having been solely responsible for all the favours which had been bestowed upon him by the zongdu and Governor of Yunnan.[^p423-74] I did not begin helping these gentlemen for their own sakes, nor will I desert them. But when the opportunity arises, I will make sure that they experience at first hand that Jesuits are not as the Seminary depicts us in its sketches. Speaking of Fr. Le Blanc, Your Reverence will certainly laugh when you hear the following little story. In April of this year, he was being carried in a sedan chair to visit some sick person or other and administer the last sacraments. On the way he had to pass in front of the zutang[^p423-75] of a very high-ranking family. When these people, who happened to be sitting near their door, saw a European being carried with great effort past their door in the chair, they told him to get down and cross a certain distance on foot, since that was the honour due to the founders of such a noble family. What do you think Fr. Le Blanc did next? This very man who with his friends ranted on about this kind of zutang being a shrine of idols, and that the tablets displayed there were superstitious images, asked pardon from those present for his mistake, willingly got down, asked the servants accompanying him to be patient, and passed the place— the “idolatrous shrine”—on foot and with his head bowed. I knew Fr. Le Blanc, and I dare to swear on my word that he would never have done such a thing | if he thought that the zutang really was an idolatrous shrine or that the tablets were really idolatrous; he would sooner have allowed himself to be cut to pieces <!-- PDF page 424 | book page 415 --> or to be dragged along that road at the end of a rope than whether outside or inside pay his respects to those buried there. It is quite clear, then, that they describe those ministers as sacrilegious who turn a blind eye to such honours, when their own practice conveys the message that they can pay them without any sacrilege.

### 28 The happy perseverance of the same Fr. Laureati in resisting the local people

It now remains for me to help heal the deep wound inflicted on the Dominican mission in Fu’an, which the officials would prefer to conceal rather than to heal. Almost the entire Dominican Fathers’ Fujian mission is situated within the boundaries of this city. Besides that, they also have five very small churches; I have inspected three of them, but not Quanzhou or Zhangzhou, since Fr. Magino Ventallol is still living there.[^p424-76] In the city itself, as you might expect given what I have described above, the pagans pulled down the wall which separated the Church of the Mother of God which was built for the women to use, to cut it off from the public street. They said that the foundations of the wall were built on public property, but in fact they wanted to render the church unusable since it would not be right either to celebrate Mass or for the women to congregate there without it. When I had been taken there and been fully informed about the justice of our cause, I advised the Prefect of the city to have the wall rebuilt. He was refusing to do so, since he sided with the pagans, so I told him that within two days I would invoke my rights and do the job myself. When I turned up, there was a pagan mob assembled wanting to stop it being done and they were giving the builders a rough reception. What was I to do? I hung up my imperial permit, that strongest of shields, with the fullest ceremony on the main doors of the church. When they saw it, they dropped the hostility from their attitude and the weapons from their hands. So that I could make the wall strong and permanent, I needed to defend it against insults with the words of the Supreme Tribunal: fang zhi zhao qiu zong liu.[^p424-77] Then the pagans whipped up other accusations, full of calumnies, which proceeded to both the civil and the military tribunals, reaching as high as the Governor himself. But these did not go down well with the officials, and indeed were rejected with altercations <!-- PDF page 425 | book page 416 | manuscript page 1032 --> and even blows, perhaps because the tribunals were at that time fully occupied with agricultural matters.[^p425-78] It remains to be seen whether once I settle in there, they will rekindle their hostility. At the moment they are powerless, and have no other means of revenge than to set their minds and pens every night to cause trouble by distributing all around the city leaflets against the Holy Law and the priests. They even delivered a particularly unpleasant example into the hands of the Viceroy.[^p425-79] Once I had returned to this city, I requested a meeting with the Viceroy to seek some remedy for all this quite outrageous behaviour, but I was dismissed by someone who was extremely hostile to us, who sent me home with insincere words, on the first occasion promising that he would come to the church as soon as he could so as to carry out his visitation, and again the second time round, saying that since the examination of the licentiates was about to take place it would not be right for him to talk to anyone in case he gave the impression that he was breaking a confidence. But it was especially important to raise this matter with him on such an occasion when all the mandarins of whatever rank and grade assembled in a common place. I approached the Viceroy there and set the whole matter before him, explaining how seriously those calumnies undermined not just the purity of our Holy Law but also the orders of the Emperor and the Supreme Tribunals. The Viceroy was extremely pleasant, and promised to sort everything out at once. But he did not keep his promises, and doesn’t give me to hope he will be much help. I am turning now to the advice of you in Beijing. I am not begging your help—once you know what I need, that will be enough to make you want to help. The anger of the gentiles in Fu’an grows stronger by the day, now that they can see that with the Viceroy’s support things might turn in their favour.

I must here give a general picture of how things stand in this mission. Nowhere in the whole Chinese Empire are the missionaries so badly spoken of as they are here. Even if in other places the gentiles insult us, I have neither seen nor heard of them attacking our moral behaviour. Here, there are obscene satires and insults hurled against them at every crossroads and in every square the moment any missionary shows his face. There are about forty consecrated virgins whom they insult in the most foul-mouthed manner. The pagans are suspicious of women going to confession, and voice every possible | kind of <!-- PDF page 426 | book page 417 --> insult. There is not respect for the churches, they never use any word for the priests except pa li,[^p426-80] and they write it with characters which have very bad implications.

### 29 The modesty and religious decorum which is essential for missionaries in China

I have often wondered to myself what is the reason for this unheard-of hostility. Far be it from me to claim that our men, who are everywhere held in good repute, are better than the Dominicans, but they are perhaps more careful to maintain Chinese standards of respectability. These good Religious spend every day going from one village to another, and they don’t want to ever miss a day visiting the Beatas.[^p426-81] They criticise other missionaries for hardly setting a foot out of doors, other than when the administration of the sacraments requires it, and even then, leaving home hidden behind the curtains of a chair. Last month, Rev. Fr. Fray Francisco Cavallero came back to the city of Fu’an by a secret route and under an assumed name.[^p426-82] I admire his zeal, but I would never have advised him to do what he has done, in case he were ever to be discovered, convicted of disobedience to the Emperor (by whom he was expelled by name), and cause trouble for himself, for us, and for the Christians.

In any case there is little hope that the Christian cause will make any progress in this mission. Many adult Christians lapse, and almost no pagans are converted. Those I baptised in Fu’an, 180 in total, were almost all children, and in the three other missions of the Dominican fathers, 1530 came to confession. In the metropolis here 156 were baptised, including one mandarin, a youji,[^p426-83] and one Bachelor. I heard somewhere just over 1000 confessions. I am not making any decision yet about Tingzhou, which is a very difficult journey from here; from Jiangxi the journey is easy and short, and it was never the responsibility of this church in previous years, being entrusted to the Jiangxi Fathers.

### 41 Father Joachim Bouvet, convalescing after an illness, comes back to Beijing from Tartary; he had asked permission to come back, but this had not been clearly given

Fr. Joachim Bouvet with two companions had begun a geographical expedition to follow the Great Wall to the shore of the Eastern Sea. He along with his horse had a dangerous fall, as I mentioned under August. The fall was followed by a chest infection and pain in his head; he had to be watched all night, since he could not coordinate his eyes. His companions and the mandarins who were in charge of the expedition discussed the situation and decided that the Father could leave the mapping project to others, and could either stay there or, having given notice to the Emperor, go back home to care for his health. They had sent word to the Emperor about what had happened, but the Emperor merely replied Sagha—I know. Maybe permission for the Father to return home was never requested, but in any case it was certainly not given. So, the Father came back to Beijing without properly having obtained the Emperor’s permission in advance. He was recovering from his injury, and arrived back late in the evening of September 16th. He went straight to the house of the mandarin Henkama, and the first thing he did was to inquire after the Emperor’s health. The mandarin was delighted to see how solicitous the Father was about the Emperor’s health, but said that it was not proper to have the ceremony inquiring after the Emperor’s health in his own private house. So, he requested the Father to perform it in the Palace with all of us present and taking part. So, as I said, we all went there to the Palace. Father Bouvet had not heard about the mistake in the announcement of the eclipse about which Fathers Grimaldi and Antoine Thomas were concerned, and he did not want the rest of us or the mandarin himself to know that he had come back without the Emperor’s permission. So, in good faith we all processed in, and because we were genuinely glad about the Father’s return safe and sound, we eagerly thanked the Emperor; the mandarin with equal enthusiasm introduced our memorial in florid terms. Perhaps he too hoped that His Majesty’s mood, | captured by good news, would more easily pass over the Tribunal’s mistake, forgiveness for which he was requesting at the same time. One has also to suppose that that was the reason why the mandarin included a mention of Fr. Bouvet in his congratulations, saying that his appearance was as good now upon his return as it had been at his departure, and this due to the liberality of the Emperor, who had kindly provided the officials in charge of the trip with an abundance of what <!-- PDF page 435 | book page 426 --> was necessary. The Emperor, however, by no means accepted this in the spirit in which we had written it. He made it clear that he had been offended by Fr. Bouvet’s having deserted the expedition without first getting permission, as will be narrated presently. It can be assumed, however, that Fr. Bouvet’s mistake was made in all innocence, or that he had thought that his situation had already been sorted out between Fr. Parrenin, his superior, and His Majesty.

September 19th. ### 43 A letter from the Prince, full of threats because of the departure of Fr. Bouvet, who makes his excuses to the Emperor

September 22nd. We were all bluntly summoned by note to the Palace. When we were all gathered, Henkama, looking upset and anxious, showed us a written reproof sent to him by the Emperor because of the recent memorial of thanks in connection with Fr. Bouvet. It had not been written by the Emperor in person, but by the Prince at his command; as a result the rebuke was much sharper, since the Prince had loaded it with his own hatred of Henkama, whom he had long been seeking to get rid of. He went around saying that in Palace business Henkama pretended to be deferential to further the interests of others rather than his own master; he deserved to be deposed from his office, cast into chains, and interrogated. How much further would he go with these lies? Fr. Bouvet was guilty because he had journeyed for several hundred leagues to get home when what he should have done was to recover where he was and then catch up with his colleagues who had gone ahead in what was a joint public expedition; it was <!-- PDF page 436 | book page 427 | manuscript page 1037 --> obvious enough that his injury simply provided him with the excuse he wanted; he had never been keen on the expedition, and ever since he had asked to be involved in mapping he had been looking for a way of getting out of it. Now his very face accuses him, since it has been said in writing to be in good condition, indeed better than it was before he left. The mandarin, and we, should have insisted upon the expedition which the Father had left being resumed, rather than allowed a thanksgiving ceremony and a pretence of innocence. This thunderclap threatened more lightning to follow. We were all completely taken aback. The mandarin was pressing us for a reply, but we could hardly think of anything helpful to say. On the advice of our friends in the Court, and taking our cue from the example of Henkama himself, we thought it best to admit our mistake humbly, so as to leave the way open for a pardon. In his personal letter the mandarin Henkama blamed himself very severely; in ours, the Fathers regretted the imprudence of which they had been guilty, but hinted at an excuse too, saying, quite truthfully, that we had no idea that the Father had returned without having obtained His Majesty’s consent in advance—but in any case, if it should happen again, we would urge the Father to rejoin the expedition. Fr. Bouvet himself took another line, writing a lengthy defence of his conduct, saying that he really had been sick and still was; | to back this up, he cited as witnesses several Fathers who were on the journey with him, and the prefects in charge of the expedition, whose agreed judgement was that he should not continue with the map-making. Henkama promptly said that he would not deliver such an excuse, saying that he could make excuses for anyone else, it was now his problem, and it was for him to admit the fault that was burdening him, as if by some Chinese magic he wished to wash away the sin of someone else by acting first; as for himself he would await in fear and trembling the threatened punishment. Neither were the other Fathers happy to go along with such an inappropriate defence; still, they did not try to stop Fr. Bouvet from finding some other means of conveying his defence to Fr. Parrenin, his superior, who when the opportunity arose might use it to help him. ### 46 Some observations on this document

Note: 1) That this document with its abbreviations, was given to the Franciscan Fathers in this form. The decree which is referred to was not made known to them as alleged, but the rejection of the appeal was indicated by this act alone, despite being based on and implemented by a decree.

2) That, despite the fact that it is said in this document that the appeal was presented “today”—that is to say, September 25th—as I said above, I am not clear about the date on which it was presented. The reason is that when <!-- PDF page 440 | book page 431 --> one considers such a long, tortuous, and painstaking document, there is good reason to wonder whether the presentation and drafting of the instrument could all have been done on the same day; so the question remains sub judice whether the document might not have been drawn up later than the day mentioned, or whether the presentation was the day before the day mentioned. One would not be far wrong to suppose that if it hadn’t been quite polished, a rough draft had been prepared, and signed to keep up appearances, on the 25th.

### 48 The mandarin Henkama’s illness and its cause

The mandarin Henkama recently fell sick—from sadness, as his servants said. The criticisms which the Emperor had ordered to be levelled against him had wounded him deeply and to the heart; he was doubly pained to hear that the Prince, not content with the severity of the criticism, was intent on taking the opportunity to do him harm. His son, very young although already a mandarin, suffered a horrendous beating in Tartary to pay for his father’s crime, as the Prince went about saying, since his father had been spared such treatment because of his old age. Henkama’s sickness grew worse, and carried him off not many days later, as will be related under October.

### 50 The royal command is communicated to the Beijing Jesuits that Fr. Bouvet has to return to Tartary. He delays

All of us in Beijing were summoned to the Palace, and were shown a royal command given in the light of our recent excuse, or apology. It was more or less in the following terms: “In that document there were many empty and useless words; nobody says whether Fr. Bouvet should be sent back to the previous expedition or not. What use is that?” The courtiers, Henkama in particular, in whose house we had to meet since he was unwell, were unanimous that those words were intended to convey to us that the Father should be sent back to the map project by us, or else that on his own initiative he should go back there as soon as possible, so that the Emperor would not have to remit the matter to the Tribunal to decide. And indeed, that is how things are usually done when someone from the household or the community has been at fault, and the Emperor wishes to avoid the rigour of the law; the Emperor hands him over to the household or the community so that within its own four walls a fair and good way of atoning for his fault can be agreed. From their long experience the Fathers were familiar with that kind of arrangement. They had grasped how annoyed the Emperor was from his own actions, from the humiliation of the mandarin, and from the punishment of the mandarin’s son; so, they began to urge Fr. Bouvet in no uncertain terms to arrange to rejoin the expedition. He, on the other hand, wanted us to say that he was not subject to any Jesuit, that none of us was his superior, and so he could not be ordered back by any of us. The mandarin was told of this, and the Father maintained that he was to clear his name himself, so that His Majesty would realise that there had been no deception, no fault involved in his leaving the expedition, though he admitted it was wrong to have come back without waiting for permission. The mandarin said it was simply not possible for him to convey any excuse for him, and that his business was simply to communicate the royal command; it would be very much to the Father’s advantage and that of his companions if he agreed to comply. If he refused, the punishment would fall on the Father and on the rest of us, and we could be quite sure that he had to write only one of two messages to the Emperor: either “The Father is on his way back to the mapping project,” or “He is not going to go back.” It was up to us to choose one or the other; he told us to stay at home while we thought about it, and give a definite answer on the following day.

### 52 Fr. Bouvet, having changed his mind several times, is ordered to return to Tartary

September 29th Fr. Bouvet was still changing his mind back and forwards throughout that night, writing several conflicting statements. There was no time for lengthy discussions, since the hour was approaching when the courier would have to be sent to the Palace. So Father Visitor asked Fr. Bouvet for his final and definitive reply. Kneeling before him with all the other Fathers he earnestly entreated him not to allow himself, the mission, and especially his French Jesuit companions to incur the wrath of the Emperor now hanging by a thread over all their heads; he must certainly be aware that we could not be blamed if he and his brethren were expelled by an indignant Emperor. Hearing this, Fr. Bouvet conceded a good deal, promising that he and the others would go to the mandarin’s house, on condition that we provided the mandarin with his written defence of his conduct, asking that it be passed on to His Majesty.
