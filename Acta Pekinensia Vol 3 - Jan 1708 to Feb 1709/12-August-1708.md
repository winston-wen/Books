Ever since the Emperor left for Tartary at the end of June, he has not encouraged the Jesuits working in his court with a single sign of his favour. He has been rather better disposed to Fr. Castner than to the others. This month, however, something grim, some dark business, was preoccupying the mind of the Prince. His expression was all kindness, but he was nursing rage in his soul and would give vent to dire threats, sometimes against those who were there, sometimes against those who were elsewhere, that the hour was coming when his malice would be satisfied. A letter to Fr. Tomás Pereira describes the Prince’s attitude to the Europeans:

### 1 A letter of Fr. Liebstain to Fr. Pereira

August 2nd, from Fr. Leopold Liebstain in Tartary, where he had accompanied the Emperor as his musician:

At the moment we are living here safe and sound, cheerful and happy when things are going well and when they are not. I hope that the same is true of Your Reverence and expect better news about Your Paternity’s health, rather than being afraid that something might be wrong. Here are the things I think worth writing about at the moment.

### 2 The Prince burns with murderous rage against the Europeans, and especially against Fr. Visdelou

The Eldest Prince recently began to talk at length about the Lord Patriarch. He revealed to us how he felt and how hard he took it that Bishop Maigrot had been allowed to go scot free. He kept saying that it was through his doing that the Patriarch had been so completely perverted. Although it was true that before Bishop Maigrot’s arrival the Patriarch would occasionally go ballistic about things, he could always be brought back into orbit. This was not so after Bishop Maigrot came. He was even more upset (witness the fact that he bitterly brought the matter up again and again) that Fr. Visdelou, whom he accused in so many words of being the principal cause of all the problems, had escaped unharmed. He added that, should that Father by some happy chance ever fall into his hands, he certainly would not get away without punishment and a well-deserved telling off. He went on to reprove him, in his absence, with disloyalty to his brethren and with other devious attempts to discredit the Europeans in the eyes of the Patriarch. Fr. Parrenin, who had repeatedly been present and who knows the languages well, would be able to give us a far better account. He fell silent, leaving us blushing with shame and embarrassment. Zhao Laoye,[^p301-1] otherwise a most steadfast friend of <!-- PDF page 302 | book page 293 --> us Europeans, agreed with the Prince about much of this, and having launched several attacks against Fr. Visdelou, took the view that he should indeed be punished. I do not know what to think about how the Prince would react if even the smallest disagreement about the present storm in Guangzhou fell to him to deal with. Thus writes Fr. Leopold.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

When Fr. Leopold talks about the “storm in Guangzhou,” he is speaking about the conferral by the Patriarch of a bishopric on Fr. Visdelou, and the commission given to him in Guangzhou by [the Patriarch] to make a French merchant, Mr. ……, a Knight of the Golden Spur. They had already remarked upon Fr. Visdelou’s behaviour at the end of July.[^p302-2] And there is no doubt that the Prince’s anger would have been provoked all the more had he heard about the arrangements between the Patriarch and the aforementioned Father, which did go rather beyond the bounds of friendship. Not that there was anything wrong in the honours conferred by him on Fr. Visdelou. But once the Prince’s bile had been aroused against the man whom he would have been glad to punish, he would be all the more liable to be angry if he had to watch him leave as it were in triumph, not merely unpunished but festooned with honours.

### 3 The origin of the Prince’s hatred for Fr. Visdelou

Furthermore, I would ask the reader not to suspect that it might have been the Beijing Jesuits who set the Prince against Fr. Visdelou. If they had made the Prince angry, they could easily have got him to destroy the Father. The main reason for his anger against Fr. Visdelou was provided by His Lordship the Patriarch in Beijing during the months of July and August 1706. In an attempt to excuse himself for having brought in the Bishop of Conon as a man who had an excellent knowledge of Chinese, whereas he knew none at all, the Patriarch said he had never actually met the Bishop of Conon; in praising his knowledge of Chinese he had relied upon the testimony of the Jesuit, Fr. Claude Visdelou, who said he was uniquely qualified to deal with the matter of the controversies before the Court, and knew Chinese much better than he did himself. Fr. Gerbillon had at the time privately alerted Fr. Visdelou about this very point, expressing his concern and advising him to look to his own safety as best he could, since were he to be <!-- PDF page 303 | book page 294 | manuscript page 966 --> summoned by the Emperor, there would be no easy remedy available to us. | However, God has so disposed that he has up to the present remained out of reach of the Prince’s claws, and we have avoided the calumny which would certainly have been levelled against us had any harm come to him. As Fr. Leopold hints, it is quite true that the Prince in Tartary has spoken about this business on several occasions. For Fr. Parrenin had already written the same to Fr. Kilian in July, and it happened that sometime between the 9th and the 18th of that month, when Fr. Jartoux was with the Prince, the Prince said to him: “Wouldn’t it be a good idea to summon Fr. Visdelou here, punish him with a beating, and consign him to the wilds of Tartary?” Fr. Jartoux pleaded against this, asking him to be merciful to us: he should not add Fr. Visdelou’s punishment to our own, since it is painful enough for us to know that His Majesty the Emperor has been offended by him and that we have no way of making satisfaction, and it seems likely that the Father will leave China in the near future, and would then be absent for longer than if he were exiled in Tartary itself.

### 4 The imperial decree in favour of the missionaries which we had requested begins to be promulgated

News of an imperial decree, given on April 27th, began to spread in Guangzhou on August 5th, according to a letter of Fr. Monteiro of August 8th. He writes, “Aos 5 deste Agosto se começou a publicar o Vên Li Pu sobre os Missionarios,/ e temos delle jà treslado. Tem ido ao Pu Chim Su, que està Chao Kim Fu.” In translation: “On August 5th reports began to arrive about a rescript from the Tribunal of Rites. It had been sent to the bu zhengshi,[^p303-3] the Keeper of the Royal Treasury who is at present in the city of Zhaoqing. We already have some of it translated.”[^p303-4]

It must be pointed out, however, that Fr. Monteiro here gives the name of only part of the whole. What was published: (1) contained our memorial and the statement of the Prince who referred it to the Emperor; (2) the Emperor’s consent; (3) a small addition made by the Poyambas; and finally [sic. 4] the Libu rescript.[^p303-5] Since the other parts made no difference to implementing the decree, and the imperial decree, rather than being completely new, was a renewal or simply a restatement made by the Tribunal of Rites, Fr. Monteiro was content to use the term wen, which refers to a Libu rescript.[^p303-6]

August 8th. Fr. Romain Hinderer wrote to say that on his way back from Beijing he had landed in Guangzhou on the 3rd of August and had found that excellent arrangements had been made for him to leave for Xinhui xian in order to receive back the church which the mandarins had turned into a school, and then to look after the first sepulchre of St. Francis Xavier, which had been entrusted to him by the Emperor. August 10th. Father Girolamo Franchi wrote to Fr. Vice-Provincial about the egregious mistake of a certain neophyte about the reason for the prohibition of the rites decreed by the Lord Patriarch. I will give a report of it all in the Father’s own words:

In any event, it will perhaps not be a waste of time to know that Friar Miguel Fernández told me a year ago that without difficulty he persuaded some of his Christians to take down the tablets of their ancestors. Indeed, while he was dealing with them on the topic of the patriarchal regulation, they had replied that the regulation was a very good thing, and that he should have prohibited those rites altogether so that Christians could be distinguished from the natives. Given this, it could happen that Fr. Fernández with good pastoral intentions might be saying or writing something along these lines; what if (and I have no definite information on the matter) he should have arranged for something to be written on this matter by some of his Christians? For this reason you need to be aware of what happened to me the day before yesterday, 8 August 1708.

### 5 The Chinese Rites as they were set up were not superstitious

Li Paoluo, a Brother of the Third Order who for many years now has been Fr. Fernández’s catechist, invited me to eat the lunch which I had brought with me in his house. In the course of the conversation, when the subject of the Chinese Rites came up, he went on to say that these rites were utterly superstitious and that the Supreme Pontiff could not possibly allow them. I was astonished that a neophyte should be so learned as to know the limits of pontifical authority and power. I asked why on earth the rites were superstitious and why the Holy See could not permit them? “Because,” he replied, “the infidels think that the soul <!-- PDF page 305 | book page 296 | manuscript page 967 --> is present in the tablets.” Seeing his mistake, I put a further question: “Were the rites when they were first set up superstitious?” “Certainly not,” he replied; “as they were set up they were purely civil, to express a spirit of gratitude.” “But then you should understand,” I added, “that the question at hand is not concerned with | what many infidels do when they practise these rites but about what the rites were instituted to do, for to this day the Society has never allowed even the smallest element of superstition in action or in belief in the practice of these rites.” Having learnt how he was mistaken he did not say anything further. This being so, what on earth Fr. Fernández might have said to his neophytes while discussing the regulation with them, and whether he persuaded them to take down the tablets and promulgated the regulation afterwards, I do not know. I think it unlikely that the neophyte by his own wits concluded that the Holy See could not have permitted the rites to neophytes for the sole reason that many infidels associate superstitious beliefs or practices with them. Thus, Fr. Franchi.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

### 6 Unlettered people have to be taught to unlearn their mistakes

It might occur to the reader to ask whether it might not be better to leave unlettered people who are misled by some pious error in their mistaken belief, and that they should take the tablets down and have nothing to do with the rites, rather than to explain to them more clearly the true state of affairs so that, once they have understood it, they will have less of a horror of the rites and will be less eager to avoid them. I have no desire to work up any sweat trying to solve that problem. I ask the reader to consider whether Christ in the flesh, preaching his Gospel, would have decided that it would be better to leave his disciples and others in the error that one must not eat with unwashed hands, and that a man is defiled by what passes into his stomach. The truth must be taught, and precepts must be separated from mere counsels, lest those with less judgement be ensnared. A peasant, pious and devout, a member of a confraternity of a religious order, might easily accept the teaching that one should not honour Confucius (which in any case is not something that concerns him), or that the tablets are to be taken down when he is not accused of doing so. If someone were to be accused of lack of care for a tablet who has aspirations to engage in higher studies or has already obtained a qualification in them, and you should manage to persuade them to avoid things which they know are not in their origin and in themselves bad, I will take you as my oracle.[^p305-8] But in situations involving the <!-- PDF page 306 | book page 297 --> unlettered, as Fr. Franchi correctly points out, evidence will be collected which is indeed based on error, but is none the less clamorous and very threatening to the Jesuits.

### 7 Concerning Fr. Guéty’s servant, and the calumny he invented against the Jesuits

On August 12th, in the College in Beijing, Fr. Visitor, Giovanni Paolo Gozani, before witnesses heard John Wang, the servant of Fr. Guéty, discuss the matters he had laid before the Lord Patriarch in the latter’s boat while he was travelling from Nanjing to Guangzhou. A year and more had gone by since that deposition was first made. The Beijing Jesuits knew about it having been informed by those to whom the Lord Patriarch had shown several things critical of the Jesuits, which by fair means and foul had been collected in the records of the legation.[^p306-9] They were also aware that the said servant had returned to the capital long ago; they had seen him regularly, sometimes visiting this church and others the Jesuit church. However, the calumny which they had suffered and would suffer again at his hand had not wounded them; they rested on their clear conscience and had no desire to bother making inquiries about the servant or about the content of his deposition, and how he had made it. They took the view that it would not look well for them or for the Patriarch to enter into a dispute with each other about the statement of a good-for-nothing servant. Fr. Visitor decided that the case should not be ignored to this extent. He had discovered that His Excellency even in Guangzhou and Macau had publicly displayed the same calumny for people to read, to the consternation of many who, because of the weight of the Patriarch’s authority, believed the charges against the Beijing Jesuits accused in the report. They thought it would be wrong not to believe what was being formally and so forcefully put forward by such a great man. The Father Visitor further considered that, since the Patriarch had thought it proper to summon such people—in other respects quite unsuitable—before him as witnesses against the Jesuits, no effort should be spared to meet pressure with pressure by legally using the same man as a witness against himself. If the Beijing Jesuits wanted not to give any credence to the charges the Patriarch was bringing against them, it was clearly the duty of his office to get to the bottom of the whole matter so that he would not appear to have simply taken the Jesuits’ word for it when they denied that the Patriarch’s Acta were correct. So, he summoned this John Wang, in the presence of Fr. José Soares the Vice-Provincial, Fr. Antoine Thomas the Vice-Rector, and Fr.

### 8 The judicial inquiry and interrogation of Fr. Guety’s servant

In the name of the Lord, Amen. I, Giovanni Paolo Gozani of the Society of Jesus, for the time being Visitor of the Japanese Province and of the Vice-Province of China, bear witness and make known the following:

When I was informed on good authority that the Most Illustrious and Very Reverend Lord Charles Thomas Maillard de Tournon, Patriarch of Antioch, Apostolic Visitor, etc., in the year of Our Lord 1707, the 46th of the Emperor Kangxi, in the third month, while he was travelling in his boat on the way from Nanjing to Guangzhou at the town of Wucheng near the city of Nanchang, in the presence of Rev. Fr. Buonaventura da Roma, ofm, his interpreter; Rev. Fr. Andrea Candela, his Chancellor; Mr. Marcello Angelita, his secretary; and Domenico Marchini, his chamberlain, summoned to appear before him a Chinese man by the name of John Wang, 32 years of age, from the city of Ganzhou in the province of Jiangxi, who originally had been taken into the domestic service of the Most Illustrious Reverend Lord Patriarch in Beijing, and then joined the household of Rev. Fr. François Guéty, a French cleric who at that time by order of the Emperor Kangxi had spent a considerable time in Tartary. On the basis of what this servant said the Patriarch instituted a judicial process against the Fathers of our Society, at present my subjects, Tomás Pereira, Dominique Parrenin, and Antonio de Barros, and considerably to the prejudice of these priests he had so informed more than a few missionaries that he, on the basis of legal and indubitable evidence formally given during his apostolic visitation, had determined: (1) that Fr. Dominique Parrenin at the beginning of September 1706 in Tartary had advised the Great Prince[^p307-10] that letters from the Rev. Charles Maigrot, Lord Bishop of Conon had been given to Fr. Guéty by Doctor Borghese; (2) that when the said Fr. Guéty was questioned about these letters, Fathers Tomás Pereira and Antonio de Barros had hidden in the next room, and that the Great Prince had several times gone in to ask their advice about which questions to ask and about the answers which had been given; (3) that when Fr. Guéty was subjected to a storm of hostility and insults, those priests, lurking within, guffawed and applauded.

As I say, when I found out about all this, after my appointment as Visitor, I left Kaifeng fu in the province of Henan and went to Beijing to find out for myself about this and other matters. Fr. Antonio de Barros had left Beijing much <!-- PDF page 308 | book page 299 | manuscript page 969 --> earlier, having been sent to Rome by the Emperor at the beginning of October 1706. Fathers Tomás Pereira and Dominique Parrenin had no difficulty in demonstrating that the charges maliciously brought against them were false. When I then inquired about the whereabouts of the man called John, all the priests replied that he had come back several months earlier to Beijing, where he had a wife and children, and that he sometimes attended our churches. None of the priests there wanted to say anything about the calumny, thinking it did not even merit a refutation. I, on the other hand, not because I doubted the innocence of the fathers but in an effort to disentangle the truth from the calumny, invited the man named John to come and talk to me. In the Jesuit College in Beijing near the gate called the Xun Zhi Men,[^p308-11] on August 12th 1708, in the presence of Fathers José Soares the Vice-Provincial, Antoine Thomas the Superior of our Eastern Church, and Kilian Stumpf, the Procurator of the Vice- Province, I questioned him and received the following replies:

### 9 Some of the subjects on which he was questioned by Fr. Visitor before selected priests

(1) I asked him his name and address; he replied that he was born in the province of Jiangxi in the city of Ganzhou, that he had in the past worked as a servant in several churches, that eleven years ago he had sold his body to a merchant called Liang Zaishen for ten ounces of silver, and that he was still at this time that man’s slave, eking out a living wherever he could from whatever payment his master chose to give him. Through the Rev. Fr. Appiani he had been taken into the household of the Patriarch; then he had been given to Rev. Fr. Guéty as an exchange; he was at present staying with his master.

(2) I asked how and for what reason in 1707, the 46th year of Kangxi, in the 3rd month, he came to be near the city of Nanchang and in the town called Wucheng had arrived at the boat of the Most Illustrious and Very Reverend Lord Patriarch.

### 10 The servant’s replies to each of the questions

He replied that at the end of July 1706 he had accompanied Rev. Fr. Ludovico Appiani, the Patriarch’s interpreter, and Mr. Marcello Angelita his secretary when they were summoned along with the Bishop of Conon and Fr. Guéty to a place in Tartary | called Karahodon. When the others went back to Beijing, Fr. Appiani arranged for him to stay on with Fr. Guéty, whom the Emperor had commanded to see to the clocks <!-- PDF page 309 | book page 300 --> until the end of October in that same year. After that, he stayed with him in Beijing until the very end of December. He had received five silver drachmas from him as salary for his time in Tartary, and ten more for his time in Beijing. Later, he had even followed Fr. Guéty when the Emperor had ordered his expulsion, but on the way he was dismissed by him without any wages in the city of Nanxiong in the province of Guangdong. When he had tearfully begun to leave, Fr. Guéty and the Illustrious Bishop of Conon each made him a gift of 3 drachmas, saying that he should go and demand his wages from the Lord Patriarch to whose household he belonged. Finally, he said, as he was leaving these two men, another three silver drachmas were given to him by Rev. Fr. Mezzafalce who was there and who asked him to inquire on his behalf after the health of the Lord Patriarch. So, forced by poverty and need, and after a short delay waiting in Nanchang, when he found out that the Patriarch’s boat was near the town called Wucheng, he was taken in a small boat so that he could ask for his wages with which to pay to get back to his wife and children in Beijing.

(3) I asked him whether the Illustrious Lord Patriarch had questioned him about what had happened in Tartary regarding Fr. Guéty, and he replied that he had.

(4) I asked, “What questions did the Patriarch put to you, and how did you answer?” He replied: “The Great Lord Patriarch asked how and for what reason Fr. Guéty was out of favour. I replied that after the imperial prohibition placed on him and on us his servants, forbidding him to communicate with Dr. Borghese, he had on the very next day received letters from Borghese; and, when at the Emperor’s order he was asked by Zhao Laoye about the ones he received, he first denied having received any, then he pretended that they were in his chest, but in the end admitted that he had taken one away hidden in his sleeve, and had torn another into pieces and thrown it into the latrine. From there Zhao Laoye had had it retrieved, washed, stuck together again, and shown to the Prince. This was what Fr. Guéty had done wrong in the eyes of the Emperor, and the reason why he had incurred the Emperor’s wrath, and been punished.” That, said John Wang, was what he had said.

(5) I asked whether the Patriarch had inquired how it had come to the knowledge of the Emperor and the Prince that letters had arrived from Dr. Borghese and been handed to Fr. Guéty? He replied that he had not been questioned about this at all. I insisted: “Perhaps you said something about this without being asked?”; and he replied “I never said anything about this at all.”

(6) I asked, “Did you see or hear Fr. Parrenin revealing that these letters from Dr. Borghese had been delivered to Fr. Guéty?” He replied, “I neither saw nor heard any such thing. Fr. Parrenin left at that moment to go to the Palace while we stayed in our own tent.”

Guéty when he spoke to you and your fellow servant?” He replied, “That is quite correct.”

### 13 The Bishop of Ascalon’s zeal for the Catholic religion, and his piety

How much our Society and in particular our missionaries in China owe to this Illustrious and Very Reverend Lord for such a defence people will judge when they have read it carefully from start to finish. The whole work deserves the highest praise, but especially it is the intention and earnestness of the writer which will redound to his eternal credit. He admits before God and the world that he has unsheathed his pen because some of these foolish people, while venting their feelings against the Society by rushing in to print, are, although perhaps unintentionally, leaving not merely the Jesuits but the Holy Catholic Church wide open to the scandal and mockery of the heretics. ### 14 The Report on the Sinic or China mission written by Fr. Giambattista da Iliceto. A short account of the state and the tribulation of the Sinic or Chinese mission in this year 1708

The still flourishing Catholic faith in Japan was already beginning to die when Almighty God decreed that the Chinese mission should be brought into being. For indeed it was in the year of Our Lord 1579 that four Franciscan fathers, namely, Fr. Giambattista Pesaro, Fr. Pedro de Alfaro, Fr. Sebastiano de San Francisco, and Fr. Agostino,[^p313-13] the first from the Italian observance, and <!-- PDF page 314 | book page 305 | manuscript page 972 --> the rest Discalced from Spain, making light of the death penalty imposed by the Chinese on any Europeans entering China, sailed from the Philippines to China and entered the great city of Guangzhou, | where they sang the hymn Te Deum Laudamus antiphonally as they, the first missionaries in imperial Sina or China,[^p314-14] planted the first seed; for clad as they were in the Franciscan habit, their heads tonsured in the style of Religious, barefoot and with cords round their waists, it was in the manner of the Apostles that they planted the mission in poverty and humility. At first, they were thought foolish by the indifferent crowd (though it was for Christ’s sake). However, it was not long until the people, having carefully noted their constancy in every kind of virtue, began to respect them. They assigned to them a lodging inside the walls of the city for their convenience, and gave them permission to live at peace in the very city of Guangdong or Guangzhou itself.

### 15 The first steps and progress of the China mission as described by the same Fr. Iliceto

On the feast of the Beheading of John the Baptist on June 24th of that same year 1579, Fr. Giovanni Battista celebrated the first mass in China as an offering of their first fruits, and just as these Franciscans were the first missionaries after the Apostles (or disciples of the Apostles) to preach the holy Gospel of Jesus Christ in China, so too one of their number, Fr. Sebastiano de San Francisco, fortified by the rites of the Holy Church, ascended from Guangzhou to the heavenly Jerusalem (as we may piously believe), and is the first of all the missionaries to be buried in China.

Six months after their entry into China, that is to say, at the beginning of 1580, some Christian Europeans (if such they can be called) tricked them into going from Guangzhou to Macau. Macau is a small city on a tiny island near Guangzhou, subject to the emperor of China but inhabited by Portuguese. And since they were no longer permitted to return to China, to keep themselves busy they built a Franciscan church in the city of Macau itself. Because they were called

<!-- PDF page 315 | book page 306 -->

“Castilians,” to distinguish them from the Portuguese, that church is to this day called the Church of the Castilians, thus designating it as a kind of safe outpost from which to re-establish the Franciscan mission in China. In the following year, 1581, Rev. Fr. Michele Ruggieri of the Society of Jesus went from that very city of Macau into that very same Guangzhou; he was indeed the first Jesuit, but he should be reckoned as coming second to the four Franciscans who had entered and then been expelled from China.

Because Rev. Fr. Ruggieri immediately realised that his Franciscan missionary predecessors had been considerably inconvenienced because of the public nature of the Franciscan habit, he laid aside his own Jesuit dress and put on clothes which were Chinese in every detail, with the idea that in this way he could capture the minds of the Chinese and so win over their souls. This change of clothes was a help to him, and also because he was not attacked by the people of Macau either, he established himself firmly in this mission. As time went on, however, many people, from Portugal, Italy, Spain, Germany, and France—Jesuits, Augustinians, Franciscans, Dominicans, and eventually secular clerics—too flocked to the same China to pursue their apostolate, adopting the same practice of wearing Chinese clothes to avoid persecution. So gradually, after a few decades they found favour and goodwill in the sight of the people and from the Emperor himself. And so gradually and one step at a time, some sort of understanding of our holy faith spread throughout almost the whole Chinese Empire, and it grew peacefully day by day, and so amazingly that in those days Christian temples were publicly built and the Catholic faith was preached everywhere. Many volumes explaining the faith were set in print and the Holy Gospel was spread throughout the Chinese Empire. The number of believers increased marvellously day by day, and were nursed in the faith each according to their capacity by some hundred missionaries.

Sad to say, however, because of the multiplicity of missionaries from several different countries and religious orders, and much more seriously because of the divergence of opinions among them there arose a chaos of confusions, to which the present distress of the Chinese mission can be traced back.

### 16 Fr. Iliceto’s view of Confucius and the honours customarily paid to him in China

(1) Certain missionaries, and not just a few, take and defend the view that Confucius is the master of all literate Chinese, or at least the scholars. However, the Dominican Fathers, and the Franciscans, Augustinians, and secular clergy, have provided powerful arguments which reveal him to be an idol. This Confucius flourished around 550 bc. He taught rhetoric and household management and dictated ten books which are held as sacred by the Chinese, in which he does not make a single mention of God, nor of paradise or hell, and says nothing about the soul, nor about eternity; the suspicion therefore is that he was an atheist. There is not a city in China in which there is not a shrine dedicated to their Confucius, although many missionaries defend the view that it is not a temple but a hall. What is clearly to be seen is that in a prominent place in this lofty house or temple a large altar has been built. Above this altar a statue or head of Confucius, gilded and well made, has been placed under a tent or canopy. On this altar candles are lit, and various spices and incense burned. Twice a year, at each equinox, solemn sacrifices or offerings are made in honour of Confucius, consisting of the flesh of goats, pigs, and deer. The official who presides at the offering strangles them, makes atonement, and then sacrifices them in front of the image of Confucius; they venerate the image on bended knees, indeed with their heads bent to the ground, and pray that he would deign to accept them in the odour of sweetness. Wine too is offered upon the altar in front of the image of Confucius to the image with the most profound reverence; they raise it on high and replace it with <!-- PDF page 317 | book page 308 | manuscript page 974 --> the greatest care, making sure that no drop is spilt. When the ceremony is finished the meat and wine are distributed to people deemed worthy. To the right and left of the image of Confucius stand twelve statues or images whom the Chinese reverence as his twelve principal disciples, just like pseudo-Apostles, in addition to which 72 other minor disciples are venerated in a less prominent place. In front of all these statues and images candles and lamps are lit, and spices and incense are being burned. Certain missionaries, and not just a few, allow their Christians to be present at these ceremonies, and are willing to join their people in venerating and making offerings to Confucius, not as to an idol but to a master from whom they have learned rhetoric. On the other hand, the Dominican Fathers and the Franciscans, the Augustinians, and the secular clergy prohibit the Christians from taking any part in offering libations to Confucius, nor do they genuflect nor bow their heads before the image of Confucius; indeed, they neither go to nor go into his temple when sacrifices are being offered, on the grounds that they say Confucius is an idol, and can prove it.

### 17 What they think about Heaven, Shangdi etc

(2) The Chinese believe that above Heaven there exists some substance, devoid of life, invisible and incorporeal, which they state is without intellect, memory, and will; they themselves call it Shangdi, and adore it as God. And just as this visible sun knows how to produce a fish and make a serpent harmless through the disposition of nature, so some Chinese say that their Shangdi, even though it has no memory, intellect, or will, still knows how to reward the good and punish evildoers as the situation demands. Certain missionaries, and not just a few, preach that this Shangdi is the Christian God, but a God who sees and is alive. But the Dominican missionaries, the Franciscans, the Augustinians, and the secular clergy prohibit their Christians to seek for the true God under the name of Shangdi, taking it as the name of an idol.

(3) The Chinese have the custom of reverencing their dead, whether they died as Christians or died in idolatry. Temples or shrines are dedicated to them, and they gather there, put together an altar, burn candles and incense, and | perform in honour of the dead all the rites we have already mentioned in connection with Confucius. Moreover, in each home the Chinese have decorated an altar or table, as their resources allow, on which they set up tablets in the place of honour. Inside these tablets there is a hollow place, and in this hollow, they believe that the souls of their dead are at rest. On these tablets the names of the dead are inscribed in beautiful lettering. They would have it that the dead are within, and twice every month offer them bread and wine with the deepest reverence.

### 1716 About the tablets of the dead

Some missionaries, and not just a few, allow the Christians to do all these things, whether the deceased died in the faith or not, teaching that they should understand these rites as having a merely civil meaning. In contrast, the Dominican Fathers, the Franciscans, the Augustinians, and the secular clergy prohibit these rites to the faithful Christians entrusted to their charge; they should avoid them as being unclean and idolatrous, since these practices have been proven to be superstitious.

(4) There is a sect in China which engages in idolatrous worship of the material heaven, to which at fixed times they offer sacrifices. Certain missionaries, and not just a few, have placed on the façade of their churches a tablet upon which in letters a cubit high is the Chinese inscription Jing Tian, that is, “Worship Heaven.”[^p318-17] As a result of this inscription, the sect which worships a material heaven and other infidels think that we Christians too belong to that same sect that worships a material heaven. However, the Dominican missionaries, the Franciscans, the Augustinians, and the secular clergy are appalled by this inscription, and instead have placed there the holy name of Jesus, or the sign of the holy cross.

### 18 The expression

The Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon, Vicar Apostolic in the Province of Fujian, which is like a kingdom in the Jing Tian Chinese Empire, ordered under pain of censures that each and every missionary living in his apostolic vicariate should remove completely the inscription Jing Tian if they had it, and if they did not, never to put it up. Some missionaries, and not just a few, strongly objected, and appealed to Rome to examine this question about the inscription as well as three other points; quarrels and disagreements multiplied everywhere, and very many books, handwritten and printed, appeared arguing on each side. Pretty well all the missionaries leapt into the fray, and became caught up in refuting the views of the other side. And so, charity between the missionaries gradually grew cold; if only sin did not abound! For about a hundred years the Christian faith was promiscuously yoked together with infidels and with the Confucian and other rites I have described. Yet there were not lacking those moved by zeal for the purity of the Catholic faith—the Dominican Fathers, Franciscans, Augustinians, <!-- PDF page 319 | book page 310 | manuscript page 975 --> and finally the secular clergy—to cry out to the Holy See for some timely remedy. Yet many other missionaries were sending back to Rome accounts which were very different from theirs, thus causing the controversies to drag on for 70 years. ### 19 Concerning His Lordship de Tournon, the Patriarch of Antioch

For the same reason, so that everything could be peacefully settled without the clamour of confusion, the Holy Father himself from among all the outstanding members of the Roman Curia singled out His Excellency Charles Maillard de Tournon, a domestic prelate of His Holiness; nominated him Patriarch of Antioch; consecrated him with his own papal hands; honoured him with a personal papal sermon at a public consistory, praising to the heavens his prudence in all things, his piety, and his learning; and sent him to China, that is to say to the Chinese Empire, as Apostolic Visitor with the powers of a papal legate a latere. For if these two are willing, they can do a lot to protect us and to further our mission, and in time it may be possible to have a hospice for Observant missionaries from China in Manila just as our people have in Mexico. In short, all our hopes in this most distant land are based first of all on the protection of God, and then on Your Reverence’s favour and that of the Mexican Province. If they are favourable to us, we would not be afraid if Hell itself were against us, but without their support it is impossible to survive in these very distant lands in which we poor little Observants are placed, as it were on the edge of the world. We have never received from our superiors in Europe the smallest scrap of support in all the eight years we have been in China.[^p327-24] We have always had to cling to Divine Providence which has never failed us, and now we cling to Your Reverence and to the holy Mexican Province, into which we place our hopes of being incorporated.

### 30 The seminary founded in Manila

Last year, 1707, I sent Your Reverence the public charter for the foundation of the Manila Seminary in which it was stipulated, as had been the case for the previous seven or eight years, that in future this seminary will contribute to the Observant missionaries in China the yearly sum of 1600 pesos on request to the mensa of the Misericordia of Manila.[^p327-25] In this way we’ve shut the mouths of our Discalced, and put paid by that clause to their hopes for what would happen if the mission of the Observants <!-- PDF page 328 | book page 319 | manuscript page 980 --> in China ceased to function, since in that case the said alms would revert to the Holy Land in Jerusalem, as stated in the aforesaid charter. It was necessary to write the charter in this way to block off any avenue for complaints and to shut up the mouths of our enemies nearer home. I am now about to arrive in Macau, a small island attached to China inhabited by Portuguese, from where Fr. Gabriele, having been expelled, is ready to embark for the Philippines. I hope in a few days to enter China to go and cheer up my very dear friend and companion Carlo da Castorano, and to advise and help those poor Christians who are like poor sheep in the middle of so many wolves without the advice of their shepherd. May God be kind enough to bring me there safely, and I shall baptise the first child I find with the name of John of the Cross,[^p328-26] and the second Joseph de la Llana,[^p328-27] offering both of them to God on behalf of Your Reverences. With heartfelt desire I humbly kiss the ground and ask your paternal benediction.

From this city of Macau, 20 March 1708.

### 33 Reasons why he thinks he must reply

However, considering that these writings of Fr. Iliceto go unchallenged; rather, one certainly ought to reply to them and demonstrate to the world their iniquity, for the benefit not only of the Christian Republic but of those writers themselves, that they may recognize their fault and correct it.

(2) The Bishop of Malaga’s motive for writing the Catholica Quermonia was because Pierre Jurieu, the distinguished Calvinist preacher, when he was fleeing from the Christian king who at the time was purging France of heresies, took himself off to Holland and there wrote a book called l’Esprit de Mons. Arnaud, in which, relying on an equally infamous authority entitled La Morale Pratique des Jésuites, Part i, he attributes to the aforesaid bishop a book or a pamphlet, or a scandal sheet, which was soon condemned and confiscated by the Sacred Tribunal of the Inquisition, entitled Teatro Jesuitico.[^p330-32] The Bishop justifiably felt hurt at having had fathered on him such a monstrous and poisonous offspring, and he complained bitterly to the Holy See, proving that the attribution was a manifest forgery and doing so with such conclusive evidence that it seems as if he might have been satisfied with that. But because Jurieu’s attack was not <!-- PDF page 331 | book page 322 | manuscript page 981 --> directed so much against the good name of the Bishop of Malaga as against the infallibility of the Roman Catholic faith, the good bishop was not satisfied until he had shown just how wide of the mark Jurieu’s shot had been. I have set down his reasons here to make it clear how incautious Fr. Iliceto has been in what he says, and how much he resembles Jurieu.

“The more beautiful,” he says, “and more holy conversions or missions which Roman Catholics boast of are | those which Christians undertake in distant parts of the world with such exhausting toil for the sake of preaching, with huge expenditure of money, etc., as can be seen in the sacred expeditions which are undertaken by the Jesuits in the great kingdom of China, and other regions of the world. But it is these very things which are altogether hateful and detestable even to Catholics who judge them as sacrilegious, politically motivated, and full of cupidity, and publish their complaints. But the argument of the critics does not work; indeed, it proves the opposite.”

### 34 He uses the example of the Bishop of Malaga replying to Jurieu

The argument was that from the zeal, labours, expense, and dangers with which Catholics in these and other parts of the world endeavour to spread the Roman faith one can infer its infallibility and credibility. Jurieu intends to prove the minor premiss of the syllogism upon which all its force depends by appealing to the authority of the Bishop of Malaga, making him the author and guarantor of all the calumnies and insanities which heretics and disloyal Catholics have invented, along with the people they have deluded into thinking ill of the Religious of the Society. Jurieu could now make his case even more convincing on the authority of Fr. Iliceto, pointing out that he is a serious Religious of a very holy order which out of zeal for missionary work has gone all over Germany, Muscovy, Persia, India, and China and has finally arrived in America. For suppose some Catholic controversialist wished to respond to Jurieu by saying that the evils which he alleges are peculiar to the Jesuits while other orders are quite different and work in a very different way in China and in their other missions: the good Fr. Iliceto has provided Jurieu with plenty of material to reply that it is not just the Jesuits—the Franciscans of the Philippines Province of St. Gregory (to which those who go to China belong) are no better than the Jesuits, since Fr. Iliceto presupposes that they have vices no smaller than those which Jurieu attributes to the Jesuits. Again, the Bishop of Malaga refutes the minor of the above-mentioned syllogism by the counter argument: he was not the author of the Teatro Jesuitico; the Jesuits in those places were not at all as they were painted by the heretics and their other detractors; and, finally, that the actions of which they are accused are not merely innocent, but very tactful and appropriate, as time has shown.

Again, if the Bishop of Malaga, thousands of leagues distant from China, still felt it to be his duty to make a reply for the sake of the honour and credit of the Jesuits who were working in those missions, being as he was well informed about them, what obligation must fall upon me? (3) Well then, he begins his Latin report by trying to claim that Fr. ### 35 The first chapter of the report is refuted, where Iliceto claims that the Observant Franciscans were the first of all to come to China

To show that the Observants of whatever nationality would be very useful in this kingdom, it was enough to speak of its size, the huge population, and to point out that it was well-known what great fruit the Church had gained from the missions of the Observants in the New World and elsewhere; there was no need to invent anything else. Furthermore, that from the very outset this Father’s report is not lacking in falsehoods seems to indicate that they are being presented here as <!-- PDF page 333 | book page 324 --> the first links of a chain. The Franciscan Discalced Religious, and with them some of the Observants, did not come to the Philippines until the middle of 1577, but in 1575, by order of the Governor, the Augustinians Fr. Martin da Rada and Fr. Jeronimo Marin had already come to China to establish the route from the Philippines with the intention of remaining in China to preach the gospel. These priests were received as ambassadors; taken to Fuzhou fu, the capital city of the province of Fujian; and when they put their proposals received courteous replies to the effect that the Viceroy could not settle such matters without an express order from the Emperor, whom he would consult in due course. And so he asked them to return to the Philippines. During their stay they celebrated many Masses and recited many Te Deums (I doubt very much that they sang them); so, they stayed in the provincial capital for more than a month, and were made very welcome. Let Fr. Iliceto consult the general history of Antonio de Herrera[^p333-33] and that of Fr. Grixaba on Mexico,[^p333-34] and others from the Philippines, and he will see that the Italian Fr. Pesaro was not the first. He is making it up, assuming as he does that Pesaro did not arrive in China until 1579; he was not “heedless of the law of capital punishment” for if there were such a law in China, we do not know that it was ever enforced against the Europeans, still less against those from the Philippines; he did not found a single mission, and neither did the Augustinians found many, even though they made more efforts to do so. Then in the following year 1576 they went back in a ship owned by Chinese who ill-treated them and left them marooned on a beach on the island of Luzon. The Chinese language is such that even today, when we have so many dictionaries and other means of learning it, there is hardly anyone who, however clever they may be, in two years could be of any use; so, what are we to say about Fr. Pesaro and his companions when they lacked the aids which are available now? It is easier to forgive the Discalced fathers and the Augustinians, who seem to be even more prejudiced in relating these and similar stories than one who goes on to others much more harmful. ### 37 The third chapter, on Confucius

(6) He says this: “That Confucius is the master of all literate Chinese, or at least of scholars, is stated and defended by some missionaries, and they are not few. Since there are no religious communities in China besides the Society, the Dominicans, the Franciscans, and so on, and all except the Society prove and demonstrate that Confucius is an idol, it is obvious that the Society must be the one which denies this. Let us go to the truth, which is that it is not only the Society but every educated person who denies that Confucius is an idol, and the Dominicans and Franciscans etc. neither prove that he is nor intend to do so, for previously more or less everyone agreed that he is not an idol. There is not even a single individual who in his treatises on these controversies has seriously held the contrary. To begin with the Dominicans, see Fr. ### 40 The views of the Franciscans and the Augustinians

(7) Meanwhile, let us go on to consider the Franciscans, among whose writings no such proposition is to be found, although some of them have written a bit about the cult of Confucius. Later, the said bishop, as he was under an obligation to, wrote a lengthy treatise defending the cult, in which he simply takes for granted that Confucius and the ancestors are not regarded by the Chinese as idols and that he had no need to go to the trouble of proving what everyone already accepts.

### 41 The view of the secular clergy

(8) We come now to the secular clergy. They are either Italians sent directly by the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith or Frenchmen from the Paris Seminary. The first started to arrive in China in 1700. Most of them did not make any effort to learn the language properly, and the one or two who did try still hadn’t acquired a twentieth of the knowledge of Chinese literature which is needed to be able speak sensibly on these topics. So, I do not know whether any of them could seriously hold that Confucius was an idol. The second group, far from saying any such thing, think such a view absurd, even though they maintain that the cult of Confucius is illicit. See the Historia Cultus Sinensium, printed in Cologne in 1700; on p. 282 appears the memorandum which the above-mentioned Charmot as Procurator for the Most Illustrious Lord Charles Maigrot, Bishop of Conon and Vicar Apostolic of Fujian, presented to the Sacred Congregation of the Holy Office on 28 October 1698. In it, in response to the complaint of the Procurator of the Society that Charmot had represented Confucius as an idol and “presented an image of him as an Idol to the Holy See,” Charmot replied: “The Jesuits accuse me and the Most Rev. Lord Maigrot of many false and ridiculous things, to insult us and hand us over as already condemned by the judgement of their own consciences. We nowhere said that Confucius was worshipped by Chinese scholars as a god, or their ancestors as heavenly beings. Whatever the Chinese understand by the word ‘spirit,’ the reverence which they pay to them is not free from superstition and idolatry even if they don’t exactly think Confucius is a God or their ancestors heavenly beings.” That is the basis on which Fr. Iliceto purports to prove and demonstrate conclusively on behalf of the Dominicans, Franciscans, Augustinians, and secular clergy that Confucius is an idol and that only the Jesuits defend him as being no more than a master.

(9) After describing very inaccurately the ceremonies with which the Chinese honour their Confucius, the good Father says that the Jesuits “defend the view that all these things can be directed to Confucius not as an idol but as a master from whom they learnt rhetoric.” Fr. Iliceto makes it very obvious here | how eager he is to defame the Jesuits, and simply for this reason his witness ought not to be heeded. Earlier he intimated that they maintained that Confucius was not an idol, contrary to the consensus of all the other missionaries; now he claims that they teach that, even assuming he were an idol, he could still be reverenced under the title of master—a doctrine which is not only alien to Christian people but amazingly incoherent, since Father Master Paz, whom he quotes, says with reason that any reverence paid to anything outside this world which is held to be a god or an idol must be assumed to be offered on the basis of its divinity. But it is obvious how seriously mistaken this assumption is since <!-- PDF page 338 | book page 329 --> neither the Fathers of the Society nor those who oppose the cult of Confucius either say or assume that he is an idol.

### 42 The fourth chapter of the report, regarding the meaning of Shangdi. Refutation of Iliceto

(10) In § 2 the good Father sets out to explain the Chinese idea of Shangdi, and is mistaken on every point. He wishes to persuade us that the Fathers of the Society teach that a lifeless entity, without understanding, will, or memory is the Christian God, and that those Dominicans, Franciscans, Augustinians, and clerics prevent their people from worshipping the true god under the name of Shangdi, which is the name of an idol. I am not attacking in detail these fairy tales which the Father has invented; I only wish to note how totally mistaken he is. When the Law of the Gospel at first was spread in China, our God was called the Lord of Heaven, Tianzhu. This title was applied to God rather carelessly; there were considerable drawbacks, more than there were with Shangdi, which means “Supreme Emperor.” Even when they realised this, they still kept to it because the word was already in widespread use. That being said, it should be remembered that Fr. Matteo Ricci in one of his books, which cannot be too highly praised, in arguing against the atheists claims that the ancient Chinese referred to God under both titles, Tian and Shangdi, and he establishes this convincingly from books which the Chinese hold as authoritative.[^p338-38] Before the 1640s some priests of the Society, basing their view on the books of atheist exegetes, had their doubts and questioned whether Ricci was right.[^p338-39] In the end, however, the view taken in his <!-- PDF page 339 | book page 330 --> book was shared by the majority both of Jesuits and Chinese scholars, and by all the Christians who have published on the Law of God—eight or nine in print, not counting manuscripts—who follow Father Ricci. This was the situation until 1670, when the Most Illustrious Señor Navarrete, with his overt opposition to the Society, on which he greatly prides himself, raised the question all over again. Fathers Le Favre and Brancato did more than enough to answer him and their treatises are still in print.[^p339-40] Then in the 1690s the Lord Bishop of Conon, Vicar Apostolic of Fujian, issued an order to that province forbidding the words and characters Tian and Shangdi to be used in referring to God, without giving any special reason for his decree other than to say in vague terms that they were suspect. Meanwhile what has happened is that the Dominicans, Franciscans, clerics, and Augustinians have used these words and given to their Christians the books which use them. Two Franciscan authors, Fr. Antonio de S. Maria and Fr. Pedro de la Piñuela, have sent to the printers books in which they agree with Fr. Ricci in respect of saying that the ancient Chinese knew God under the titles of Tian and Shangdi.[^p339-41] Fr. Matteo Ricci’s books along with those of Fr. Pantoja,[^p339-42] using these words and characters, have been printed at the expense of the Augustinians and Franciscans. The tablets are still in use in their churches and they have distributed and still distribute many of these books. It can be seen from this that everything which Fr. Iliceto says in this section is a complete fabrication in order to vilify the Jesuits.

### 43 A refutation of Chapter 5 and Iliceto’s account of the tablets of the dead

(11). In § 3, dealing with the tablets of the dead, he says: “They put up tablets arranged to enclose a hollow place in which they believe the souls of their dead rest.” When this sentence was read to four or five of the old missionaries, they could not contain their laughter | at the strange picture which Fr. I come now to the one true statement which the author makes on this topic: it is certain that before 1693 some Dominicans and a few Franciscans made considerable efforts to persuade their Christians not to use those tablets, but not many paid any heed. After all, there are not many Chinese who could be persuaded that it is wrong to remember one’s father and to reverence him as though he were still alive. ### 44 Almost all the missionary Religious allow their neophytes to use the tablets

In 169343 Monsignor Maigrot in his province of Fujian issued an edict for the province forbidding the tablets to be used unless one of the characters which are usually inscribed on them had been removed, on the grounds that it appeared to him to be superstitious.[^p340-44] He required a protest to be placed next to the tablet, rejecting the mistaken opinion of some of the ignorant common people. He tried to have this order carried out in Fujian, as did many missionaries in other places. In some places they succeeded, because the Chinese Christians did not believe in such ideas, but elsewhere they failed to get the character removed or the protest added since they thought it unnecessary. So, the Franciscans in the provinces of Shandong, Guangdong, Jiangxi, and elsewhere, together with the Augustinians, carried on as before, allowing the tablets and the reverence paid to them. Fr. Iliceto knows this very well, so I do not know how, short of deliberately lying, he can state that the Dominicans, Franciscans, etc. do not allow the cult of the tablets, and that only the Fathers of the

<!-- PDF page 341 | book page 332 | manuscript page 987 -->

Society approve of it. He will reply that his statements about the Dominicans, Franciscans, etc., are indefinite. Since they are not about what is necessarily the case, they are not universal; so, they can be true even if only a few of each group have wished to forbid the practice. But this is an obvious subterfuge and goes quite against the impression he wishes to convey, which is that the Chinese rites are so evil that all the groups condemn them apart from the Jesuits, who alone defend them. That is the view he wants to promote in America, through the Most Reverend Commissary General.

### 45 Other chapters in the report are refuted

(12). In § 4, in addition to some truths he publishes many falsehoods. First, he says that certain missionaries, and many of them—he means the Jesuits—put tablets with the characters Jing Tian on the facades of their churches. I don’t know on which façade he saw one of these inscriptions, large or small. He says that the infidels believe that we belong to that sect—if there is any such sect at all in China—which adores the material sky. So put, that is false, as I have proved in my treatise dealing with this and other controversies. He says that the Dominicans, Franciscans, Augustinians, and secular priests have a horror of such superstition. It is true that the French secular clerics say they have: whether they do or don’t I shall say below. The fact is that the present Emperor donated these characters to the Jesuits in 1676 or the following year[^p341-45] to be put on their churches as a mark of honour. It is a very common custom in China to give such inscriptions to honour families or individuals deserving of praise. When, during the persecution which lasted from 1665 until 1670, the churches were not functioning the missionaries were restricted to the one in Guangzhou, which then and for some years afterwards was the sole church in that province. When they saw that the churches were standing empty, the mandarins profaned them by using them to accommodate anyone they wished. Even when the missionaries went back to their churches five years later, the mandarins were unwilling to give up the custom they had established. | Much trouble had to be endured by the Jesuits, who solved the problem by putting up in the hall of their houses a tablet just like the one which the Emperor gave to the church in the capital and inscribed with the same characters. They did this to everyone’s satisfaction everywhere they needed to, except for the province of

Guangdong where it was not necessary. All the churches there apart from the one mentioned had been open since 1674, and since the mandarins there had not taken possession of the churches and were not in the habit of turning them into hospices, they did not feel themselves inconvenienced.

### 46 How Chinese scholars interpret the character

Moreover, outside the province of Guangdong many missionaries put that tablet on their houses, so great is Tian the respect with which the Chinese regard these inscriptions given by the Emperor. This did not cause the missionaries the least scruple, nor their Christians, since they well knew that Chinese scholars understand that the character Tian not only is used to refer to God, the Lord of the World, but has many other accepted meanings as well—tian does not just mean the material sky, just as the word coelum in Latin has many meanings. The Dominicans put it on their house in the city of Zhangzhou fu and in other churches in the eastern part of the province of Fujian. The Franciscans put it on their church in the city of Nan’an fu in the province of Jiangxi and on another church in the province of Fujian, as well as others in the province of Shandong. The Augustinians did not put up any, since none of their churches was outside the province of Guangdong where, as I have said, there was no need for them. There was no need for them either in the churches they opened later in the provinces of Jiangxi and Guangxi. A quite large tablet with square characters, such as Iliceto describes, when painted and gilded weighs at least seven or eight pounds, which is why they did not put one up in Shaojing fu when they opened their church there. None of that caused me or my companions the least scruple. The French clerics did not put any up either: I am not sure what the real reason was, though it is clear that their churches did not need them since they had all been opened after 1685. However, on the lanterns which they used when traversing the street they had painted the letters Jing Tian jian,[^p342-46] which is equivalent to saying, as those priests themselves believed, “We belong to the tribunal which reverences Heaven”; and that is just what the tablet says, “Heaven may be reverenced,”[^p342-47] or, more accurately, “may Tian be reverenced.”[^p342-48] That is what that character means, for, although it is ambiguous, it is disambiguated by the context in which it is written, as I have shown in the controversies which I submitted to the Holy See.

### 48 The difficulty of prohibiting the Chinese Rites

This decree and the censures with which the Lord Patriarch prohibited the Rites or commanded the missionaries to prohibit them must have been so “secret and without fuss” that no missionary ever set eyes upon or even heard of any such thing. But he never said so in so many words, because in truth His Lordship was in no position to give definite news about the resolution. The case was not decided until 1704 (if, indeed, it has been decided at all even yet), and the Patriarch initiated his measures in the middle of 1705. When it was clear <!-- PDF page 344 | book page 335 --> from the letters from Rome that at the beginning of 1704 the dispute was still continuing, I replied to the Lord Patriarch that for His Lordship on his own authority and relying just on his own opinion to undertake to forbid the Christians to practise those rites was for many reasons a useless endeavour; the first reason being that the Chinese were not being given sufficient reasons to explain sufficiently why their rites were superstitious, when they had previously given excellent reasons in favour of them. The Chinese were strongly wedded to their view, which was shared by the most learned of the missionaries who had been or who are now in China. If he carried out his proposal, the missionaries would be subject to the most serious scandals, as happened in Fuzhou. Nobody, not even His Lordship, who after all had only just arrived in China and was in no position to give advice, should just take it into his head to risk such damage and loss of souls. This is what I said to the Patriarch in several meetings with him, and others gave him the same answer. But His Lordship produced no such document from the Holy See, nor was he willing to make a law against the Rites; all he kept saying was “You have to get rid of those rites even without being told to; in that way you will safeguard your reputation and nobody could say that you had condemned the Holy See.” In the end the Patriarch issued no command to the missionaries, with or without censures. Fr. Iliceto has made that up to oppose the Jesuits.

### 50 The Bishop of Ascalon requests that the question should not be decided until the parties have been heard

Then, as I said before, the Jesuits pleaded with me to refute in Rome the false charges being made in the reports, that I together with the Augustinians had prohibited the cult of the ancestors and of Confucius. ### 51 Why a declaration from the Emperor was sought by the Jesuits

(15) Seeing that they were so maligned, the truth so threatened, and the salvation of this kingdom so much in jeopardy, and considering that the truth in these disputes was so clear that a village sacristan could sort it out, the Jesuits concluded that the key to the whole difficulty was that nobody could settle it better than the Emperor of China himself, since he is the best judge of his own laws and customs, and a most able and learned man. So, in 1700, five years before the Patriarch arrived in China, the Jesuits living in Beijing presented a memorial to the Emperor, the substance of which was that the Chinese rites had come to the notice of people in Europe who wanted to learn about their purpose, etc. They had to answer questions, and thought that this and that was true, but were afraid that as foreigners they might be mistaken. So, they begged His Majesty to correct their mistakes. The Emperor replied that things were as the Jesuits <!-- PDF page 346 | book page 337 | manuscript page 990 --> understood them to be. Not satisfied with this, the Jesuits consulted more than ten of the most noble and learned persons in the kingdom, who confirmed their interpretation in writing. All this was put together and printed as a book in Beijing before the end of 1700, and reprinted the following year in Guangzhou.[^p346-51] Consider here what Fr. Iliceto says, that the Jesuits fled from the Pope to the Emperor as to a higher tribunal, which is precisely the opposite of the truth. They verified the facts with the Emperor, since nobody better than he could settle the matter in the face of the cavillings, subterfuges, and equivocations used by their opponents; and, when this had been verified, then they had recourse to the Pope as the highest tribunal and ultimate refuge. After that the Lord Patriarch arrived, in April 1705, and as a result of a great deal of energetic lobbying on the part of the Jesuits, the Emperor ordered him to be escorted with honour to Beijing where he received him with considerable ceremony. Later on, the Emperor and the Patriarch fell out despite all my futile efforts; things went downhill every day until in the end the Emperor ordered him to leave the Court, and later ordered him to be taken to the Portuguese city of Macau and to be held there until the return of Fathers Antonio de Barros and Antoine Beauvollier, who had been sent by the Emperor to the His Holiness to conduct the business concerning China. This is what Fr. Iliceto describes as the appeal from the Pope to the Idolator Emperor.

(16) The good Fr. Iliceto says that Our Lord Pope Clement xi, now reigning (and may he do so for many years to come), “gave, to be remembered in perpetuity, his eternal decree whereby the Confucian and the other | rites mentioned above were abolished.” This decree was requested by some of the families of the missionaries in China, as Fr. Iliceto points out—and well would they pay for it. What actually happened was that the Emperor, having been personally informed that some missionaries were contradicting his declarations about the meanings of the characters Tian and Shangdi and about the Chinese rites and on other matters which I shall not mention in order not to make trouble for any particular individual, decreed that those missionaries who did not have a special certificate allowing them to remain in China—and that was almost everyone—should be expelled from the kingdom. The Beijing Jesuits, prostrate <!-- PDF page 347 | book page 338 --> and with many tears, begged the Emperor to suspend this decree, and after several attempts succeeded in arranging that, before it was published, time would be allowed for the missionaries to apply for and obtain a residence permit. The Emperor said: “Write to them all, that those who wish to remain in China should come into my presence as soon as possible to obtain the certificate. I wish to examine them personally and find out what they think. Those who do not wish to come should leave China at once, before my servants expel them, which would be harmful for them and bring discredit to your Law.”

### 52 The Bishop of Beijing and other missionaries henceforth accepted the Emperor’s permit under certain conditions

### 58 Fr. Giambattista Iliceto accuses the Jesuits of being responsible for the Patriarch’s ill-treatment

I have no wish to be the historian of this tragedy, but I simply have to relate something of it to give the lie to the many false assertions which Fr. Iliceto strings together here.

First, when the Patriarch first arrived, the Jesuits asked people at Court to obtain permission for him to go up to Beijing. Twice the Emperor refused the <!-- PDF page 354 | book page 345 --> request, saying that he was a foreigner, recently arrived, and with no knowledge of the customs of China, and he could cause a great deal of trouble. The Jesuits persisted nonetheless, and in the end the Patriarch was escorted and received with honour in Beijing, but was so unwell that for a month he was unable to get out of bed.

### 59 The Emperor’s declaration about the Chinese Rites

At this time, when he was at the baths, it was arranged that he would be visited by two of the Emperor’s chamberlains, and one day, not without instructions from the Emperor, they asked him what people in Europe thought about the declaration which His Majesty had made about the rites in 1700. The Patriarch replied that from his point of view there were some difficulties in it which we still do not understand. Very good, they said, now you have an opportunity to put forward those difficulties, and His Majesty will settle them all. Not just on this occasion, but on others he was given to understand that the Emperor would be happy to clear up these points, not because he was setting himself up to pass judgement on whether the Rites are permissible or not, or were compatible with the Law of God—all that, said the Emperor, was the business of the Pope who is responsible for it—but because the Emperor wanted simply to explain the meaning of the characters and what the customs of the kingdom really were and what they understood them to mean, since that was for themselves and not foreigners to settle. Despite this, for several months the Patriarch did not reply to the Emperor at all on this point, but through his interpreters Fr. Frosulone and a Piedmontese priest from the Congregation of the Mission who spoke and still speaks very little Chinese,[^p354-68] he began to persuade the Christians in Beijing to desist from the Chinese rites. I accept that the Patriarch’s intention was not to issue commands, but simply to exhort and advise; but his interpreters were saying that they should desist from the rites etc. because they were superstitious and condemned by His Holiness, and that was what the Patriarch, whom they had a duty to obey as they would obey God, wanted, etc. The Christians were extremely upset by this, and had not the priests of the Society moved quickly, they would have brought a complaint to the Ministry of Rites, which would have led to considerable unpleasantness. The Emperor and his sons the princes had spies everywhere and missed nothing; they gave the Jesuits warnings to pass on to the Patriarch, that the Emperor did not appreciate the Patriarch discussing with common people one of the most sensitive matters of state (since it was the basis of his policy) and yet not talking about <!-- PDF page 355 | book page 346 | manuscript page 994 --> it to His Majesty, who was ready to give the reason for the laws and customs of the kingdom. The Lord Patriarch had been in Beijing for more than six months and had made | extremely important intrusions into matters relating to politics, not religion, and which belonged to the Emperor as a prince of the highest rank. The Emperor said goodbye to him most courteously, before he left to go to Tartary for the hot season, as was his custom. But on the day of the departure, the Patriarch offered or rather presented to the Emperor an important gift in the person of the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Conon, who he introduced as very well acquainted with Chinese literature and who would explain the controversies about the Chinese rites, together with a French cleric named Guéty, who was a skilled clockmaker but who knew nothing about what is essential for a missionary and whose ignorance and lack of talent caused extremely serious problems.

### 60 How Bishop Maigrot was received by the Emperor, etc

These two priests, summoned by the Lord Patriarch, arrived on the very day of the Emperor’s departure. As I was saying, they were presented to him and he received them. They underwent a long and critical interrogation in which the Emperor ordered the Bishop of Conon to put down in writing a summary of what he had against the Chinese Rites. The man gave him simply a confused set of texts from the canonical books which appeared to prove his case, to all of which not only the Jesuits but others had already replied. The Emperor laughed when he saw them, but to find out more about them he told him to follow him to Tartary along with others from the Patriarch’s household, the clockmaker, and the two or three Jesuits. There he received the Bishop with honour and began to discuss the Chinese books. He discovered that, as we all are, he was in a state of hopeless ignorance. For even those Europeans who know most about Chinese learning at best manage to reach the standard needed for someone to get a baccalaureate in China. It is a fact that there are a very few who learn much but are like blind people who dream that they can see, and, being deceived themselves, deceive Europe. ### 65 What kind of a man is the Chinese emperor?

(22) Finally, the proof of this Father’s calumny can be seen if one considers the character of the Emperor. Fr. Louis Le Comte had been at the Palace and Court of Beijing for some years but returned to France in 1692, where he was so well respected that he was chosen to be the confessor of the Duchess of Bourgogne, the sister of Her Majesty the

Queen. In the book which was printed in Paris in 1696 under the title Nouveaux Memories sur l’état présent de la China, there appears a dedicatory letter to the Most Christian King. In translation it reads: “The present emperor succeeded his father while still a minor, and soon stabilised his throne which had been through a period of uncertainty. He expelled the pirates from their moorings and scattered them, and imposed his authority on the princes of Guangdong and Fujian; he subjugated the eastern provinces which had seceded; he made tributaries of the Chinese kingdom many Tartar princes; and he forced back within their own frontiers the Muscovites who had waged war and traded as far as the Eastern Sea, and now treats them as his vassals and keeps them in subjection. In this way Fr. Le Comte painted a true likeness of the Chinese emperor, and yet Fr. It is almost impossible to enumerate the fantasies and dreams which the Father includes in his letter with its bad Spanish! He says that the Emperor seems to persecute the missionaries, but this is not so; on the contrary, he likes them, lavishes his favours upon them, and only pretends to persecute them to please the Jesuits who incite him against all the missionaries who do not follow their opinions.

### 66 The persecution of the missionaries is no fiction, as Iliceto would have it

Could Fr. Iliceto not tell us whether it was to him or to someone else that the Emperor unburdened the secrets of his conscience with such candour, confiding that the persecution which he pursues with such rigour is only a pretence, so that we might have this fact to console us? But all of this is pure fairytale. It is true that up until 1707 the Emperor always behaved very affably and kindly towards all the missionaries, irrespective of their nationalities or <!-- PDF page 359 | book page 350 | manuscript page 997 --> fraternities. ### 71 The mutual love between the Franciscans in China

(24) The good Father returns to his attempt to prove his claim that they expected some assistance from the Discalced Fathers, which they did not receive. What truth there might be in this will become clear when I say that everything passed through my hands and that I can swear that I have seen, or can be morally certain, that things took place as follows. In 1699 I was in Manila waiting to return to this kingdom when four Reformed Franciscan fathers arrived in the city along with the two Observants, on their way to China. Once they had arrived in Guangzhou, they were again welcomed as guests of the Discalced, with the same generosity as if they had themselves been Discalced. More than twenty of us missionaries from different nations who all found ourselves in Guangzhou can bear witness to this. Within a month, Fr. Stefano had been sent to Zhejiang with a veteran Religious who was to teach him the language, and Fr. Frusolone remained in the church of Shunde near Guangzhou with Fr. In that year, too, the same Bishop della Chiesa who is Bishop of Beijing, having lost his secretary and companion, invited Fr. That is what happened to those first two Observants.

### 75 An examination of his behaviour in Mexico

Fr. Provincial informed us from Manila that Fr. Iliceto told the Viceroy of Mexico that the Discalced Fathers in China did not have the twenty missionaries which His Majesty was supporting, to give him the impression that the Fr. Provincial’s certification was fraudulent, while in fact during the years in question, 1703 and 1704, there were not twenty—there were twenty-one. Again, to make it clear how he felt towards his Discalced brethren, it is worth noting what he says in his letter; were it not that he says it himself, I would say nothing about it. Well, he says that in 1707 he sent a document to the Most Reverend Commissary General of Mexico, which states that after seven or eight years there will be paid by the Misericordia the sum of 1600 pesos per year for the Observants’ mission, on condition that if that mission is discontinued, the funds will pass to the Holy Land in Jerusalem; that, he says, will shut the mouths of the Discalced because they will no longer receive the funds eventually. It seems that the situation is that the large donation which Fr. Iliceto had collected in Mexico he had invested very profitably in Manila, to the extent that in seven or eight years there will be a capital of 32.000 pesos, yielding an interest of 1600. Of course, it is possible that the Observants’ mission will cease to exist, in which case it would have been very reasonable that the alms should be applied to the mission of the Discalced in accordance with the intention of the benefactors, which clearly was that it should be used for the Chinese mission. Fr. Iliceto, because he anticipated these contingencies, made his arrangements out of malice towards his brethren. God give me strength! Should it happen that the Observants’ mission should cease to exist, what harm would it do them if the alms were to be applied to the Discalced, who supported and encouraged the foundation? I do not criticize him | for investing the money nor for getting such a large income from it, though that is quite contrary to the rule of St. Francis, as the Fr. Commissary General will already have noticed, and will have corrected by refusing to honour such conditions. The Lord Patriarch will do the same when he finds out about it. What I do criticize is that the Discalced Fathers who have done so much to assist his mission are the object of such rancour: his outlandish devotion to Jerusalem, which the benefactors are not likely to approve of, might put an acceptable gloss on it to the outward eye, but his letter reveals the real truth about his inner feelings. I believe that when this comes to the notice of the Supreme Council of the Indies they will certainly not approve the kind of devout behaviour which is so contrary to the law and to His Majesty’s insistence on being careful about giving to beggars, when the money is being given to the Indies and even more to outsiders like Fr. Iliceto. But Fr. Iliceto, that masterly speculator and predictor of future contingencies, says that Fr. Gabriele da San Giovanni is passing through Manila and claims that he will have a great deal to put up with from the Discalced there. He will <!-- PDF page 368 | book page 359 --> not have permission to be there, but nevertheless he will be welcomed with the charity and care with which he was welcomed in the Discalced Fathers’ houses in Guangzhou and Nan’an. ### 76 Iliceto has been rebuked by his superiors and the Lord Patriarch and deservedly so

If it is true that Fr. Iliceto has been, as he says, upset by the Provincial, whose kindness and humility and religious observance I know well, since we were procurators together in Madrid, Rome, and in Mexico, it will be because Fr. Iliceto deserves it for the confusions he has caused. By his own account, he went to Manila en route to Mexico, collecting alms with such greed and shady practice that when the Lord Patriarch found out he was extremely scandalized, complaining about him to me, and not just to me but to the members of his order in Guangzhou in 1705. When he came back, he was bound to carry on with his misbehaviour, so they should have reprimanded him. But they did not tell him off as they should have done, and if the provincial and the guardian are to be blamed for anything, it is for this failure to act. It is a good thing that every religious order, including the Franciscans, have statutes whereby one province does not encroach on the territory of another, nor set up a convent in the territory of another. This didn’t stop the provincials and the Guardian from giving him permission to collect such large alms in Manila or from making arrangements for him to seek even larger sums in Mexico, when they could and should have stopped him; it would have been quite simple for them to do so by sending a note about him to the Governor. But in this whole affair they behaved in a manner quite contrary to the procedures on which His Majesty is so insistent and the result of their taking the easy way out is that the Discalced in China have no love for the Observants for sowing such harmful weeds in their field.[^p368-74]

### 77 He makes many false complaints about Fr. Frosulone

(27) Space does not permit me to refute all the lies which this Father piles up in speaking about Fr. Frosulone just because he is an Observant. In the letter, he says he told the Emperor of China to his face that he ought not to adore Confucius. I have already stated that this type of proceeding had been delegated by the Emperor to a prince, one of his sons, together with a chamberlain, and it was before them that Fr. Frosulone, as has been admitted once and for all, professed his willingness <!-- PDF page 369 | book page 360 | manuscript page 1003 --> to adore Confucius, as he says; when later the edict of the Lord Patriarch appeared, in the month of June before the same judges he said that he could no longer keep his promise, since His Holiness had prohibited the Chinese rites. At no point did he say any such thing face to face with the Emperor, as with great solemnity Fr. Iliceto’s letter twice alleges. I have no doubt that had the occasion called for it the Father would have said the same thing to the Emperor’s face, but in the end he did not do so, nor did anything similar happen to Fr. Iliceto. The entire account of what happened is as I have said, and has been put in writing by Fr. Frosulone himself and signed by six or more Religious. Iliceto states that the Lord Patriarch wrote to the Supreme Pontiff to obtain preferential treatment in this mission for the Discalced over all the Observants. But it is certain that His Lordship has no justification or discretion which would lead us to believe any such thing. The Observants were the most recent arrivals in this mission, they have done the least | work of all, not having had time to do more. We know, of course, that the owner treated the workers whom he took into his vineyard at sunset in the same way as those who were there since early morning, and even he did not avoid the complaint “He has made them equal to us”;[^p369-75] yet Fr. Iliceto asks the Lord Patriarch to behave as though the Observant Fathers have not only been put on the same footing as the rest, but are to be given preferential treatment. It is easy to see that this anomaly is intolerable.

This Father betrays his hostile intentions not only in what he says, but in what he does not say. He says that in the persecution in Cochinchina there died for the Catholic faith 20 Christians and 2 clerics, whereas in the death notices and in the catalogue of the martyrs we find six priests, including Fathers Pedro de Belmonte and Joseph Candoni, both of them Italians, both of whom died in the prisons from the hardships of the persecution.[^p369-76] But, how could Fr. Iliceto have a good word for the Jesuits whom he blames for all our misfortunes?

### 78 Other false statements made by Fr. Iliceto about Tonkin and Fr. Ripabottoni

### 79 About the Lord Patriarch’s entry into China

(28) Monsignor the Patriarch of Antioch, Visitor Apostolic of these missions with the powers of a legate a latere, was not received in China as a papal nuncio because, for political reasons, he did not present to the Emperor the brief of accreditation which he had brought for this purpose. He simply announced that the Pope had sent him to visit the missions and that as he bade him farewell, he instructed him to seek an audience with the Emperor and thank him on his behalf for the kind way in which he was treating the missionaries. But the Patriarch made a mistake which he could not correct. Had he been received as a nuncio or as an ambassador the Emperor is not so uncivilized as to maltreat him, contrary to the law of nations. This gentleman arrived by ship from Manila in the vicinity of the city and port of Macau. He then clandestinely embarked on a boat and landed on one of the city beaches, where the Lord Bishop João de Cazal and the Captain General, who is the Commander of the Garrison, met him and welcomed him, and, with the agreement of the Lord Patriarch, did not perform an artillery salute or any other demonstrations which might have made it difficult for him to travel on from here to the metropolis of Guangzhou, given the sensitivities, or indeed the suspicions, of the Chinese. He arrived in Guangzhou in the April of 1705, and reached the Court in Beijing at the beginning of December. During the nine months he stayed there, there was one occasion on which the Emperor asked His Holiness to send him some doctors and musicians etc. The Patriarch presented him with a memorandum stating that the Portuguese in Macau were not allowing missionaries to pass through Macau, and unless this were sorted out, how were musicians <!-- PDF page 371 | book page 362 | manuscript page 1004 --> and so on supposed to get through? | This memorandum was not well received, since it contained other matters as well which annoyed the Emperor and his sons; the Portuguese too were extremely hurt, believing that it was only by the grace of God that more damage had not been done.

### 80 How and when the Lord Patriarch was deported to Macau, and his reception there

Despite this, when the Lord Patriarch was ordered to be deported to Macau and was kept in detention there from June 1707, the Portuguese politely provided him with a house which was standing empty. Then, when he arrived and they saw the size of his entourage, they arranged for the occupants to vacate one of the best and largest houses, which the Lord Patriarch took over and, so it is reported, paid his landlord 300 pesos in rent, since that was what the house was worth. As soon as the Lord Patriarch had arrived in 1705, he began to exercise some kind of jurisdiction in Macau, because the Bishop received a Jubilee proclamation,[^p371-77] ordering him to publish it. I do not know whether there was any other such act which the Bishop went along with for the sake of peace, thinking that it would not make any great difference. But he did make a protest to the Lord Bishop of Malacca, who at that time came to Macau to be consecrated, asking him not to undermine the privileges and legal customs of the law and patronage of Portugal. So, things remained until the Lord Patriarch returned to that city, whereupon the Captain General requested that he should not exercise any jurisdiction until he had presented his brief of accreditation, approved by the Council of Portugal. The Lord Patriarch denied that he had any duty to present briefs of accreditation, whether approved or not, and it was quite sufficient for him to present a brief to give to the Bishop when he happened to meet him, in which His Holiness says that he is an apostolic visitor with the faculties of a legate a latere.

### 81 How the Lord Patriarch behaved

They then asked the Lord Patriarch at least to suspend any exercise of jurisdiction until letters had arrived from the Viceroy in Goa, which were expected within the month, but he remained obdurate, and at the same time began to warn individual ecclesiastics and diocesan priests that they had better take his side and obey him rather than the Lord Bishop or the Captain General. The latter had provided him with a military guard—at first as a mark of honour, but later it was retained to make sure that there were spies and witnesses to what the Lord Patriarch’s cronies were doing. He, fully aware that the guards were more of a prison than an honour, <!-- PDF page 372 | book page 363 | manuscript page 1005 --> requested their removal, whereupon there ensued several exchanges of requests and replies. Finally, a French cleric was put in prison,[^p372-78] and at this point, the Lord Patriarch excommunicated the Bishop, the Captain General, his council, the captain of the guard, and the Provincial of the Society, and published a document denouncing them. Matters reached a complete deadlock. The Portuguese now realised that they were making no impression and that the Lord Patriarch would never give in, since he was intent upon maintaining at any cost the right of the Holy See to issue commands throughout the world without needing anyone’s permission. So, the Captain General ordered his soldiers to prevent the Lord Patriarch and his household from leaving their house, and in the same way prevent any European from entering the Lord Patriarch’s house, allowing only the Chinese servants to come and go in order to provide essential supplies for those under house-arrest. This order was carried out for a year with very few exceptions permitted. At first, some of the servants were searched to see whether they were bringing or taking letters from some of the Lord Patriarch’s supporters to others. I do not think there was a single Portuguese who openly admitted to being one of them, other than the Superior of the convent of Saint Augustine, who was sent back to Goa for his pains. This is what happened, as Fr. Iliceto says, and I have no doubts that this kind of detention and the restrictions imposed by the guard are contrary to canon law regarding sealed bulls, and that those who had caused, provoked, or advised it are subject to canonical excommunication. All things considered, however, it is not right to blame them more severely than their fault merits. War gives our king the right to engage in hostilities on his own initiative against the Portuguese, even if it involves taking lives, but it does not give individuals the right to damage someone’s reputation by imputing to them more crimes than they have actually committed, all the more because they inhabit this city of Macau so deserving of good repute in the eyes of these missions.

### 82 The attitude of the Portuguese of Macau towards the missionaries

It is true that the King of Portugal | has for years been insisting in Rome that the Sacred Congregation [of Propaganda Fide] should not send missionaries to these parts without his approval, since to do so violates his <!-- PDF page 373 | book page 364 --> privileges. In line with this, there is a standing order in Macau to arrest any missionary sent by the Sacred Congregation and send them back to Goa; some Captains General have wanted to interpret this order as applying also to missionaries from the King of Castile coming through Manila, even though not directly sent by the Sacred Congregation. For this reason missionaries from the King of Castile have had some trouble in Macau, but nothing so serious as to have prevented any missionary from entering China. Leaving aside these minor harassments, which have affected just two or three people who were so mandated, missionaries on other visits to Macau have always experienced many good works, devotion and eagerness to spread the faith, as one would expect from Christian men and from such a Catholic country as Portugal. I myself have been subjected to harassment worth noting on three or so occasions in the almost thirty years since I came to this mission, but I have also experienced the great kindness, devotion, and generosity of the people of Macau in support of our missions. So, the angry outbursts of Fr. Iliceto could not possibly arouse my sympathy. How sad is this shameful dishonour for the Catholic faith! So those are my general comments on the Macau Portuguese.

### 83 The Lord Patriarch was not thrown into prison in Macau

### 84 Neither was he forbidden to communicate by letter

It is indeed true that at first they searched the servants to see who it was in Macau who was in touch with the Patriarch, since they were afraid of some kind of plot. But from start to finish the Lord Patriarch has been in communication with Guangzhou here and with China on paper, contrary to what Fr. Iliceto says. I myself have received many letters and some official documents from him; none of his or my letters or those of his other correspondents is known to have gone astray. Fr. Iliceto goes on to say that the gloss which the Portuguese put on to justify their oppression of the Lord Patriarch, the “coloured pretext” as he calls it, is that he has not had his credentials recognized by Portugal, and Fr. Iliceto <!-- PDF page 374 | book page 365 | manuscript page 1006 --> clearly wishes to convey the message that the real explanation is to be found in a Jesuit cold North Wind, since it is his view that from this North Wind all evil is let loose. It is anybody’s guess what this might involve; but nobody will be able to guess unless his heart is as full of rottenness as Fr. Iliceto’s.

### 85 The Dominicans and Jesuits are maliciously blamed for the Patriarch’s troubles

### 98 Why the Franciscans are appealing to the Supreme Pontiff

Simply in order to fulfil my obligation and to obey the command of my superior, and out of concern for the affairs of our mission, which for the time being have been entrusted to me; and, seeing that at the present moment our Religious are all concerned about the imperial order which has come from Beijing in which the Emperor requires that any European missionary who wishes to remain in his kingdom of China should at once leave for his Court in order to receive his certificate or permit, and that anyone unwilling to do so should leave China immediately as an exile, never to return; and, in order better to decide what should be done in circumstances as difficult and complex as those which confront us now, when the decision will affect whether our Religious will remain in China engaged in their apostolic mission, each one making his contribution so that what has been achieved in so many years of work to the benefit of souls should not be lost, or will leave the mission with no hope of ever coming back, thus losing the fruit of our labours.

In obedience to the arrangements which the Father Provincial of our Province of St. Gregory of the Philippines[^p386-89] has made well in advance through the letters which he wrote to the commissary of this mission and his counsellors, requiring them in the difficult and adverse situation in which we find ourselves for the time being, in the crisis in which the whole mission is placed, the counsellors, their commissary, and whatever other Religious it might be thought best to include, should meet together and decide, by their votes and opinions, whatever seems to them most to contribute to the service of God. Obeying, as I said, this higher order of our Father Provincial, we held the meeting of myself and the Religious who were present in Guangzhou and after a full discussion of the matter we find ourselves in God and in our consciences unconvinced that we should abandon the mission and leave China simply because we failed to go to Beijing to obtain the Emperor’s permit. But it is impossible to combine obtaining the permit with the unreserved[^p386-90] and total <!-- PDF page 387 | book page 378 --> observance of the Lord Patriarch’s decree, the observance of which has been so rigorously put to us. We can discover no other means of remaining in China and looking after our Christian communities except the one which many other missionaries have already adopted: an appeal to His Holiness against Your Excellency’s decree. This is the only permissible way for us to avoid being drowned in a sea of disasters. In so far as we are all unanimous and agreed on what we believe needs to be done, I and the others who have signed this paper setting out our appeal prostrate ourselves at the feet of Your Excellency to set before you the reasons which have persuaded us to follow the path of an appeal. Our procedure will be to follow the correct legal form as far as is possible in the present circumstances; our sole intention is the one we have already stated, to prevent the mission from being lost, as indeed it will be if there are no workers and ministers to tend it. Listed in order are the principal reasons which have motivated our appeal, as follows:

### 99 The Franciscans go on to explain the reasons for their appeal

1. We must take into account that if we fail to obtain this permit from the Emperor we shall immediately be deported from China. Missionaries who are of longstanding and essential will be gone, and with them we shall lose the Christian communities and churches. The Emperor has intimated that the motive for the deportation is the existence of such rebels in his kingdom, who understand little of these matters and of how useful they are for the peace and good government of his vassals. Given this he will not allow the same kind of people to come back, nor allow others to come who hold the same view.

2. To obtain the permit it is necessary to give the Emperor a written affidavit stating that one has followed, and will in future follow the practices of Matteo Ricci, which are those already followed by the Society of Jesus and by other ministers who have followed their example either in part or completely in running their missions, in accordance with the decree of Alexander vii of the year 1656. Your Excellency’s decree entirely forbids all this. Even though there is no lack of ministers who could interpret it in a benign sense according to the principles of law and the teaching of Catholic authors, everyone is afraid to do so on their own account and because of the burden of responsibility for the distress caused to the missionaries by the following point.

<!-- PDF page 388 | book page 379 | manuscript page 1013 -->

3. We have asked the ministers, particularly those who have greater direct responsibility, to express their difficulties | and doubts to Your Excellency with the intention that your reply would clarify your decree, so that we could present ourselves to the Emperor and obtain his permit. Not only has Your Excellency failed to resolve our doubts by giving clear replies to our questions, but what brief reply you did make has served only to put more difficulties in the way of our obtaining the permit, since you continue to order us to bear the burden that those who appear before the Emperor must say that they intend to observe your decree.

4. Although we believe that the decision has already been taken in Rome about the controversies in China that Your Excellency’s cites in the decree, as yet we do not know what specific points were determined. We have not seen any official document from His Holiness stating these decisions and requiring the missionaries to obey them. As a result, their consciences are yet further troubled without any hope being offered of hearing anything further. This is the point which we find absolutely essential if we are to be able to give straight replies to the Emperor when he puts his questions to us, without making the slightest mistakes on our part on matters of faith and the Christian religion, as befits legitimate sons of Holy Church.

5. The points Your Excellence commands us to observe in your decree, even if you corroborate them with some new decree from His Holiness (and leaving aside anything involving the truths of the faith or other matters which can be reduced to such truths by a genuine definition of the Pope) apply only to some new observance in matters of usage and custom and new practices in the missions. This produces such serious problems as we have already experienced, and this enormous mission of China is on the verge of being lost with no hope of recovery. If one then considers the instruction which Rome gave to the vicars apostolic,[^p388-91] in the section which begins “If in carrying out the orders of the Sacred Congregation …”[^p388-92] etc., and the other which begins “Every effort must be made ….,”[^p388-93] etc., not only will vicars apostolic be bound in the present <!-- PDF page 389 | book page 380 | manuscript page 1014 --> crisis in the mission to delay the execution of such commands before they have first informed His Holiness of the serious problems which strict observance produces—and this part is also relevant to the missionaries who have to carry out such orders, since they too must avoid bringing about those harmful consequences—but if they are unable to find any legitimate way of being released from obedience to the person who insists that they carry out his orders, recourse to the Supreme Pontiff, giving an explanation and making a plea, is permissible, and is a duty as a matter of natural law. It is this point which obliges us to lodge our appeal without Your Excellency’s knowledge or supervision, for the time being, until such time as His Holiness, once he has been well-informed, should decide what we must do in future.

6. Given that the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon and the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Macau and so many Fathers of the Society of Jesus have already appealed to His Holiness with the intention that this mission be saved and not totally destroyed for lack of ministers; given also that observance of the decree of Your Excellency is demanded completely and without variation from the interpretation which Your Excellency demands and also ordains that no missionary may present himself to receive the permit without provoking the Emperor’s anger with serious consequences both for them and the whole mission; on these assumptions, we reach the reasonable conclusion that there is no licit way to observe Your Excellency’s decree other than the most difficult and impossible course of declaring to the Emperor that we surrender our lives by opposing the well founded customs of his kingdom which he believes are based on sound natural reason. On close examination, we see no other course than to put aside what Your Excellency enjoins and orders in his decree, and we have no alternative but to appeal to His Holiness and to join our appeal to those already made. We see no other licit mode of action than what we have adopted.

7. Since the Emperor of China is the legislator of his whole empire and has the right to clarify any doubts, we might have about those matters already raised concerning the rites and customs of his kingdom, namely the intention of his subjects in the veneration of Confucius and the ancestors, we refer you to his response and declaration already sent to Rome and placed in the hands of His Holiness. Now he has provided further information together with that of the Fathers of the Society to persuade our Supreme Pontiff of the most severe inconveniences and disturbances which would follow in his empire if one should attempt to establish a new practice contrary to that which established by the first ministers who founded the mission. And the concern of the Emperor that we missionaries should do everything on our part to remain in | China while awaiting the new resolution of His Holiness appears a most solid reason to do so—outweighing any number of appeals. Since we have no other <!-- PDF page 390 | book page 381 --> remedy more licit or more efficacious that to appeal against the decree of Your Excellency, we do so with great urgency and hope of being heard.

8. The last reason and the very serious grounds for the missionaries of St. Francis being obliged in conscience to use every licit means to remain in the mission and see that it not be lost by any negligence or carelessness on our part emerges very clearly when we examine the reasons why the mission was established in the first place and continues in the present. Its beginning was the journey to Rome to the feet of His Holiness of Fray Buenaventura Ibañez (of holy memory).[^p390-94] After first informing them of his business regarding the China mission, he sought the help and support of the Supreme Pontiff and the Sacred Congregation of Propaganda for a mission of Spanish Religious to work in China. He submitted to them letters and dispatches supporting him from Our Most Holy Catholic King of Spain. His application was received well. He was granted permission by the Father Provincial to take with him as many of the Reformed Discalced Religious as wished to go and was nominated as their commissary to lead them to China. Eight Religious, making with the commissary nine in all, were presented to Our Catholic King. He piously congratulated Fr. Buenaventura for his zeal and expressed his thanks for the help of the Sacred Congregation. His Majesty sent orders for their passage to China at his expense and, knowing that Franciscan poverty could not maintain them in China and that the mission would collapse unless he augmented their resources by the amount required for the sustenance of the Religious, for building new churches, etc., ordered that every year sufficient money to maintain them should be sent by the royal treasury of Mexico. When several years of our Religious in China had passed after the Catholic King had received new information of our perseverance in the mission and the great fruits it had achieved in converting souls and founding new churches with His Majesty’s alms, which demonstrated his religious zeal for the spiritual conquest of souls, he agreed to liberally increase our mission to the number of twenty missionaries, all maintained from his royal treasury at the same rate as for the first.

### 100 The current state of the Franciscan mission in China

From these favourable beginnings has developed the increase in the China mission of St. Francis that holds today: covering five provinces of China it has founded, if <!-- PDF page 391 | book page 382 | manuscript page 1015 --> one counts the main and smaller churches, in cities, towns, and villages more than fifty churches. The main ones in which the ministers live are well planned and well constructed, as everyone knows. The number of Christians that have been baptized and are served is not small. In the census taken at various times of the newly baptized, there has been a yearly increase of more than a thousand baptisms. Given that our mission is in such a state and with hope of even more progress in the future, how could the missionaries of St. Francis want to leave China, thus losing everything they has worked and sweated for over so many years? What accounting could they give to God and the Sacred Congregation, to the Catholic King, to the Order and its Manila Province which has extended itself by founding churches as far as the Chinese Empire? If it is licit, as we suppose, to appeal against Your Excellency’s decree in the state in which we find ourselves, so bothered by scruples of conscience and by the difficulties in obtaining in any other way permission of the Emperor to remain in China and so apply a remedy to so many souls who would be swallowed by the Hound of Hell without pastors to care for them, we cannot cease exerting ourselves to the utmost to stay in China by appealing against the decree of Your Excellency to His Holiness, well informed of everything including the miserable state in which this mission finds itself, in order to apply a due remedy to save it.

### 102 Notable points in the above appeal

The reader might think it strange that I should have been at such pains in the record for June to demonstrate that the conclusion some people wished to draw from the relation of the Franciscans in Shandong was false. The suggestion was that they had testified that there was no need for someone who stated that they had previously followed the practice of Matteo Ricci, which Alexander vii had permitted, and followed them at present, to swear that they would continue to do so, it being understood this meant they would do so unless the Holy See should decide differently; after all, a permit was given even to those who openly protested against taking part in the Chinese rites or allowing them. The falsity of this entire position is clear from the whole process of the appeal, and especially from the first two reasons which the Franciscan Fathers gave, which are as follows:

Firstly, we must consider that unless we obtained an imperial permit, we would at once be deported from China, and there would then be a shortage of the essential missionaries of long standing. All the spiritual growth there had been in the converts, and all the material in churches which had been constructed, would alike be ruined. And we must bear in mind that (as the Emperor put it) the reason for bringing the punishment of exile down upon us was the behaviour of some turbulent Europeans who, in his empire, with insufficient knowledge or experience of Chinese affairs, attacked rites which were based on the moral and political doctrine which ensured peace and the proper subordination of subjects; that is why he is unwilling to allow people like this to return, and has no intention of admitting to China people who hold such views. And <!-- PDF page 393 | book page 384 | manuscript page 1016 --> we must consider, secondly, that in order to get an imperial permit, it is necessary to hand the Emperor a handwritten affidavit that the person seeking the permit has followed and will continue to follow the practices of Matteo Ricci, which are the same ones which the Jesuits have kept, as have other ministers who, when undertaking missions, also follow them either in whole or in part in accordance with the decree issued by Alexander vii in 1656.

However, Your Excellency’s decree forbids all this. Although there may be some ministers who could interpret it in a benign sense, in accordance with the principles of canon law and the teaching of Catholic authorities, all are afraid to interpret it on their own responsibility because Your Excellency threatens them with excommunication and prohibits any interpretations of his decree. The result is not a little burdensome and upsetting to ministers and others.

This makes it clear that this conclusion was not simply an invention of the six Franciscans in Guangzhou, or the two from Shandong (Frs. Miguel Fernandez and Martin Alemán) who have been in Guangzhou for many months, or the two Franciscans in Jiangxi (Juan Fernández Serrano and Diego de Santa Rosa) who themselves appealed, as will be recorded at the end of November, nor by the people in Shandong themselves.

Why then raise the matter? My answer is that at the time when the records for July were compiled, the facts about this appeal were not known in Beijing. The appeal | was made on August 30th and was sent to Macau, where it was handed to the Lord Patriarch in September. On September 25th the Patriarch refused the appeal, and it went back from Macau to Guangzhou, and finally made the journey to the court in Beijing as November was already coming to an end.

As for the rest, I admit that, when we did see the appeal, it was clear that there was no need for the elaborate arguments such as I produced at the end of June. Yet I would ask the reader not to wish to delete them altogether, for the times are such that nothing is so settled that some people might not hope that they could find some similar device to unsettle it; not only has the very appeal of the Franciscan Fathers been described as frivolous etc. by the Lord Patriarch, but also—who would believe it?—the Franciscan commissary himself wanted to deny it had ever existed, and invoked an anathema against the appellants, as will be shown by authentic evidence under the month of October.
