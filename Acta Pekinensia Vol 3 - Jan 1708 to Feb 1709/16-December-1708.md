### 1 A sorrowing Emperor, anxious ministers, a court in turmoil

December 1st. In the game park just outside the city the important people erected a camp full of pavilions widely spread round the tiny Imperial Palace. They were all in the grip of fear and of a profound sadness, but nonetheless, they all tried to arrange everything which it was their job to provide—everything by way of fowling or hunting which might serve to cheer His Majesty’s spirits and help them to avoid any thunderbolts from their overcast Jove.[^p520-1] At the urging of a group of his ministers. His Majesty went with them as far as the portico of the house, where bands of huntsmen bristling with every kind of weapon were waiting for him. But the speed of the excited hounds held no delight for him, and the busy aggression of the hawks failed to hold his attention. Now his lethargic body is refusing to ride; now he is rejecting a carriage as unsuitable for hunting; now it is his portable throne which will not do because it affords him no shelter from the wind. Everyone was sent back to their tents; dejected, the Emperor would hide himself away, and go hunting in secret. Not long afterwards he captured an extremely dangerous wild animal, calculated to devour not just one son, but all eighteen of them.

### 2 The lamas’ fetishes and the crimes of the Prince are detected with remarkable astuteness

December 2nd. The Emperor had not been seen for two days, while a more urgent though highly secret inquiry was instituted among the household servants of the Prince.[^p520-2] Eventually, either through fear or because torture was used to compel him, one of the Prince’s eunuchs, by the name of Yang, began to tell a story which left everyone dumbfounded. His confession was at once relayed to the Emperor: the Prince had employed some of the lamas’ wiles and buried some fetishes in the ground, so as to incite the Crown Prince to do dreadful things, and hence arouse the Emperor’s hatred against him; the Prince had also made provision to have all his brothers put to death. The Emperor ordered the Tartar sacrificers, commonly known as lamas, whose names had <!-- PDF page 521 | book page 512 --> been revealed by the eunuch, to be brought face to face with the Prince without warning, so as to discover whether he or they would agree to confess their crime. Everything was done with remarkable moderation. Investigators were appointed by the Emperor.[^p521-3] First they worked hard to discover where and at what time they could make sure of arresting the four lamas; then, under the honourable pretext of an imperial command, they led them in the silence of the night to the Prince’s residence. When they came into his presence, the investigators at once addressed the Prince, saying that the malice of the lamas had been revealed and that they had just now been condemned by his Father. His father would however make this concession to him, who had always been his most beloved son, and allow him to explain how and to what purpose he had been ensnared by the evil wiles of such men. In case it seemed that it was a needless terror which was causing them to tremble, the investigators enumerated the crimes which had already come to light elsewhere. You would have said the lamas were statues, so rigid with panic were they at hearing this. The Prince, glimpsing some hope of pardon, at once openly admitted that in truth there had been misdeeds and he had gone along with the lamas’ plans. When the Prince confessed to what had been done, the lamas too, unwilling to be excluded from any pardon, confessed that to please the Prince they had buried the fetishes, primarily aimed against the Emperor, in front of the famous shrine which is towards the north of the Palace in the place called the jiaochang4—the parade ground for the Arms Dedication ceremony. The investigators wanted both to get at the truth and to put an end to the fetishes, and immediately decided to achieve both ends at once. The lamas had no horses, since they had come from a nearby temple on foot, so the Prince provided horses from his own stable. The City Governor was told to open the gate and provide a military escort, carrying lights since it was the dead of night. A fetish doll of the Emperor was unearthed, and there was another inside the city in front of the gates of the shrine which bore the inscription Bao an si— Temple for the Safe Preservation of the Emperor5—and which the Prince had built three years before. This doll was clothed in saffron robes, with an iron <!-- PDF page 522 | book page 513 | manuscript page 1082 --> chain fastened with a mysterious bolt round its neck, and on its chest, it had plates embossed with the Crown Prince’s name, and the dates of his birth and his nomination as successor etc. Each doll was brought to the Emperor in the game park, but so secretly that the entire camp | knew nothing of it. When the Emperor saw the first one and realized that it had been aimed against him to arouse in him the restless Demon of Hatred against the Crown Prince and so drive him out of his mind, and that the second was intended to induce the Demon of Lust to possess the Crown Prince and drive him to disgrace, thus rendering him in the eyes of his father and of the entire Chinese world unfit to rule the Empire, he was terrified; but soon he breathed a bit more easily at the realisation that the lamas’ scheme had been discovered and rendered harmless. But he was still saddened at what had happened to himself and his sons. That same day he sent a eunuch to the Palace to the Crown Prince’s prison, to tell him to be of better heart, and that if anything were to happen, he and his father would share the same fate. He also encouraged him to enjoy some food as he otherwise used to do, and issued an order that delicacies should be brought him, in just the amounts and of just the kinds to which he had been accustomed since he was a boy. The Emperor had no intention of keeping this information secret, and when word got around the hunting camp, most people drew the conclusion either that the Crown Prince would soon be killed or that he would not be allowed to live should the Emperor happen to die. But they were mistaken, because they still did not know what message about his son the unearthed dolls had conveyed to the Father’s heart.

### 3 The Emperor recognizes the innocence of the disinherited Crown Prince

December 4th.[^p522-6] The Emperor returned to the city. Some of our people were already allowed to go in through the Dragon Gate—for instance, those who looked after the pharmacy, the clocks and the musical instruments. The eunuch Zhang Qilin did not want us to stay there until the usual time; knowing what was coming, he sent us and all the others away from the place, and towards evening the deposed Crown Prince was summoned to await the nod of His Majesty in the atrium of the Yangxin dian building, where all our work is done. When the summons came, he went up to the imperial residence.[^p522-7] When they first caught sight of one another neither the father <!-- PDF page 523 | book page 514 --> nor the son could speak; the drama was played out without words, watered with tears, drowned in sobs.

### 4 The futile exorcism of the bonzes

Then there was a performance with the bonzes, so that the devils which were besieging or surrounding us might be driven away. To achieve this the infernal sacrificers suggested that two men as substitutes should be clothed in the robes of the Emperor and the Prince, into whom the bonzes would transfer the devils; and then force the devils to transfer from them into some asses; they would then drive away the asses with whips, with the intention of carrying the sometime Princes of Heaven into the dry deserts.[^p523-8] This example of an inept exorcism might make you laugh—but that’s what happened, as I will relate on the following day.

Also, on the 4th. The Most Rev. Fr. Juan Fernández Serrano of the Order of St. Francis, Pro-Vicar Apostolic of the Province of Jiangxi, was asked in Beijing whether what people had written about the events in his residence in Nan’an in 1707 was true: Had the Lord Patriarch told him on one and the same day that his decree was a matter of doctrine[^p523-9] and so no appeal against it could be made, and also that he had written it in such a manner that the Pope could do whatever he chose? That is correct, asserted the Most Reverend Father, and wrote the following testimony:

### 5 The Lord Patriarch’s opinion and statements about his own decree

On 17 March 1707 in the church of St. Francis in the city of Nan’an fu, between the second and third hour of the afternoon, the Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord Patriarch of Antioch Charles Thomas uttered the following words in the presence of Fr. Juan Fernández of the Order of Franciscan Minors, Missionary and Notary Apostolic: I have taken every care in composing this decree, and I have left room for His Holiness to revise it should he so wish;[^p523-10] this despite the fact that <!-- PDF page 524 | book page 515 | manuscript page 1083 --> on the morning of that same day in the presence of the same Reverend Franciscan Father he had said that the decree was a matter of doctrine, from which there could be no appeal.

These words are written in a different hand, and the Rev. Fr. Juan Fernández signed it in his own hand in the following manner:

In substance it is so: Fray Juan Fernández Serrano.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.[^p524-11]

### 6 The report of the Dominican Fathers is similar to that of Iliceto

Also on December 4th. One of our friends wrote from Guangzhou giving the first news of the report of the Dominican Fathers, which in its assumptions, style, and purpose is scarcely if at all different from the extravagant letter of the Rev. Fr. Iliceto, given above under the month of August. Our friend writes about both of them on the day given here:

I have sent what information I can to Rome, Lisbon, and Goa. | The best bit is the reply to Fr. Iliceto’s report carefully and vigorously made by the Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord Bishop of Ascalon, Vicar Apostolic in Jiangxi. I am waiting for us in Macau to be sent the report of the Dominican Fathers, which those who were exiled from China and living in Macau had sent to Manila to have printed there. The Manila magistrate refused to give the press the permission they had applied for, on the grounds that the report was gratuitously insulting to the Portuguese. I believe they are preparing a refutation in Macau. But for a stronger defence to repel these attacks one has to wait for the Beijing Fathers and above all for Fathers Joachim Bouvet and Pierre Jartoux when they describe what happened in the inquiry in the city of Hangzhou in 1707 about accepting the permit.

### 7 A mistaken opinion about the power of the Jesuits in China

That report purports to persuade the world that the Jesuits have the power to make sure that the people they do not want are expelled from China and to keep in the mission those whom they choose to have as companions, and that the origin of this persecution lies entirely with the Jesuits. Furthermore, in that same report the Dominican Fathers state, whether rightly or wrongly I do not know, that the Lord Patriarch has within himself a little papal oracle, and that in some private letter or other he has received a papal decision which, along with a decree on the Chinese Rites, was sent to him by His Holiness by three different routes but that not a single one came into his hands. While I was waiting during these days <!-- PDF page 525 | book page 516 --> in Guangzhou for the report I have mentioned to be sent to me, to give myself something to do I transcribed with my own hand a letter of the Lord Patriarch’s written and signed on November 21st last year, and then sent to the Most Rev. Fr. Provincial of the Dominicans in Manila. This was sent from Manila to others in Macau and from there was communicated to me as well. From it you will not find it difficult to discover the assumptions from which the Patriarch gets his ideas; he believes, or wants others to believe, that those missionaries who have been expelled as well as those wishing to come can be admitted to China without any difficulty.

So says our friend. Here is a copy of the Patriarch’s letter to which he refers:

### 8 A copy of the Lord Patriarch’s letter about the Chinese persecution and about the report of the Dominican Fathers

Very Reverend Father, Your Paternity will have heard of the tragedies which continue to befall us in these missions. Not that I want to get started on the misfortunes which have affected me personally; I am referring to the testimonies of so many of your subjects, eyewitnesses and zealous, who know very well the origin of this persecution and how it is organised. It is not caused by gentiles, but by those whose obstinate behaviour is unbridled. I would not say such a thing did I not have abundant and conclusive proof. So, this letter of mine is not just to console Your Very Reverend Paternity and all this holy province about the apostolic conduct of those missionaries, with their obedience and their devoted concern for the interests of religion and of the Most Holy Apostolic See, an obedience which is the trademark of the zeal which is handed down in their evangelical institute. I am writing also to encourage you not to lose sight of the need to protect this mission, which is all the more glorious the more it has been brought low. ### 9 Some points worth making about this letter of the Lord Patriarch and the Dominicans’ report

By this letter, the Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord Patriarch makes several things clear, or does his best to do so, some of which I shall briefly note:

1) He approves of the Dominicans’ report, and indirectly endorses it as accurate, presenting its authors as eyewitnesses who out of zeal for God report what they have seen. For the reader to judge of the accuracy of that work and the justice of his approbation, it is necessary that he consider the report itself, together with the refutation of items in it. I cannot give a complete summary of it here, because of its prolixity; it contains 102 folio pages. The two opposing treatises written in Macau by the Reverend Vicar General contain far more, and again, there are two further treatises written in Guangzhou. I do not know how many pages those four would make altogether. There is also a much more comprehensive refutation at present going to press in Beijing, which will appear under the title On the Origin, Progress and Settlement of the Chinese Controversies.[^p526-14]

2) The Lord Patriarch admits that lamentable tragedies have occurred in the Chinese mission and are occurring still, and he wishes the Most Reverend Father Provincial to trust the report of his own brethren about them and about their sad results; but nevertheless, he then plays down these effects, as if at a <!-- PDF page 527 | book page 518 --> stroke and without any difficulty all these ills could be healed, and the forty missionaries who were expelled through Guangzhou could soon be readmitted through Fujian whenever he wanted. Can the Lord Patriarch here be trying to play down the massive ruin of the mission in order to curry favour with the Jesuits, so that while claiming that they have been calling the tune in the mission he can still make them less disliked? Wouldn’t that have been easy for me to have said? Still, I am convinced that he wishes to appear to excuse the author of such a disaster, while claiming to know who it is, but to avoid naming names.

3) The Lord Patriarch heaps praise on the Most Reverend Dominican Fathers and pays them every honour, and does so on the pretext of being fair. Well, the Apostolic Vicariate [of Zhejiang] had been entrusted by the Sacred Congregation to the Dominican mission in the person of the Most Reverend Father Alcalà;[^p527-15] but in 1705, the year in which the Lord Patriarch first entered China, Fr. Alcalà died, and after a few months it was he who transferred the vicariate to the secular clergy, naming as Fr. Alcalà’s successor in the province of Zhejiang the Rev. Fr. Mezzafalce. Then, because they gave obedience to the patriarchal regulation, nine Dominican fathers were expelled, and their mission, at the very time when he was writing his letter, depended on just one, Rev. Fr. Magino [Ventallol], an old sick man who was liable to be expelled any day. Things had got into that state because, as the Dominicans’ report says, the Dominican Fathers had allowed themselves to be persuaded that the Jesuits would find some way of enabling them to continue to insult the Emperor and yet stay in China. What kind of repayment with interest is that for the loss that the Reverend Fathers, who listened to him, had incurred?

### 10 The ceremony performed by the bonzes to expel the evil spirit from the Emperor

December 5th. In the Inner Palace prayers and abjurations were recited by the bonzes by which they claimed that the evil spirit hovering over or possessing the persons of the Emperor, the Prince, and the Eighth Brother could be locked into some evildoers. Their performance was begun at the third hour of the morning, the same hour at which the deposed Prince had been locked up in prison. Nobody in the Palace talked about this matter, indeed only <!-- PDF page 528 | book page 519 | manuscript page 1085 --> a very few people knew about it at all since it took place in the first of the women’s enclosures. I spent the whole of that same day with the eunuch clockmakers, together with two of our Brothers, Brocard and Stadlin, in a place that was separated by just a single partition from that hall in the women’s quarters. So, I heard without any interruption the orchestra and cymbals along with the harps, tubas, and trumpets. After having admired it (for the sound was more or less like that of funeral music) I asked several times what it was. The eunuchs, to keep up the pretence, replied that it was a ceremony for someone who had died, but that it must come from outside the Palace: the sound was being borne in on the wind. | I pointed out that the wind was from the west, and that I had come in through the west gate almost two thousand yards distant and had heard nothing; I could not understand how in the heart of the Palace the harps and cymbals could be heard quite distinctly when in an empty square they could not be heard at such a distance. The eunuchs laughed saying that if I was not willing to believe that it was music for someone who had died, perhaps I might believe it when I happen to see that person risen from the dead.

In the evening, between the third and fourth hour, quite outside the normal mealtimes, I saw tables loaded with sweets and fruits being brought in with great haste through the Yuehua men gate (which is on one side, right next to the Emperor’s quarters).[^p528-16] The waiters were not the usual Tartar ones but eunuch chamberlains, who were not losing any time about it. Seeing this, and guessing that it betokened some kind of good news, I made so bold as to ask one of them what this rather cheerful rush to prepare the tables meant? He whispered in my ear that they were being prepared for the deposed Prince, whose father had summoned him again to his presence. That same day, three men were led out from the prison, and, already carrying the devil, (according to the bonzes) they were put upon asses and led away to the Haizi—the suburban game park17— and from there taken out beyond the boundaries of the province of Beizhili, and forbidden to return. The lamas were ordered to say prayers for forty days, at which ceremony Ding Laoye[^p528-18] was ordered to attend. He has been my colleague on a particular job in the Palace for more than ten years without a break.

### 11 The two Franciscans after accepting their permits return to their churches

Also, December 5th. The Reverend Fathers Juan Fernández Serrano and Diego de Santa Rosa left, on their way back to their churches. December 6th and 7th. Nothing worth mentioning happened. The Emperor prolonged his stay in the city, and we went on with our normal tasks in the Palace in the usual places: inside the Longzi men or Dragon Gate were Father Pereira and Father Leopold,[^p529-19] as usual; again, Brothers Baudino and Rhodes were in the pharmacy, while Brothers Brocard and Stadlin were with Fr. Kilian looking after the clocks. Entry for the rest of our people was not as yet entirely unrestricted unless there was some particular reason. The same was true, we noticed, of the courtiers who previously used to work there in virtue of their office; so much so that even the officials Zhao, Wang, Zhang, Li, and the others were not allowed inside unless summoned by a eunuch.

### 12 Father Luis Gonzaga is professed of the four vows

December 8th. Fr. Luis Gonzaga, having first made the Spiritual Exercises as is our custom, pronounced the four vows of his profession, which were received by Fr. Giampaolo Gozani, the Visitor, in the library of our College, which at the moment we are using as a chapel while the new church building is completed.

### 13 The Prince is more strictly confined; various opinions about him

Also, on the 8th. The Emperor ordered his firstborn son to be more strictly confined in his house, specifying the area outside which he was not allowed to go. Guards were brought in, not the usual domestic ones, but ones drawn from the public authority under the control of high officials. The Emperor himself designated the prefects of the guard by name. The city was filled with various stories: some people were saying that the Prince was bound with chains, others exaggerated his freedom, and not a few were angry that he was not being treated more severely. For, they argued, either it is true that he employed fetishes to ensnare his father, his mother, the Crown Prince, and all his brothers, or it is false. If it is false, why is he under arrest? But if it is true, why is he not being taken out and punished so that the punishment of the son would be an example to the whole world, for the disobedience of such a betrayer has been held since time immemorial to be worse than treason and is a scandal to every human being? Such is the way that a vague rumour is spread around: people speaking in favour or against, depending on the faction they belong to, sermonizing to <!-- PDF page 530 | book page 521 | manuscript page 1086 --> people who pay no attention about the relaxed nature of the Prince’s captivity, or, starting from the same point, saying that they were afraid that he would be released because of the Emperor’s fatherly forgiveness, whereupon he would surely take revenge on the people whom he knew had been delighted to see him in chains. In fact, however, the Prince was still allowed considerable freedom. Not only were there no chains, but to help him to while away the time, all kinds of craftsmen were allowed to visit him who had once belonged to his household or had been paid to come. | Indeed he would send these same people round the city to do small errands for him. Some of them turned up in our three churches, hoping to find out I know not what; they asked me particularly about clocks and some other curios, as though the Prince had previously entrusted them to my care. We could easily discourage an annoying person who still tries to reap where he has not sown, and take up what he had not put down, all the more so when he tries to appraise the look on our face when talking about the Prince’s fall.

### 14 The Prince is stripped of his honours, wealth, and servants, etc

The Emperor was more severe in the matter of rank and goods. He stripped him of his princely rank, and ordered that the goods which he had bestowed on him through paternal generosity, far beyond what was due to a prince, should be returned to the treasury of the Imperial House. There remained only the ordinary allowance which was given to the Prince’s son, a boy of about twelve with the title of beile.[^p530-20] The Emperor divided this into ten portions and gave it to three people for whom the Prince was responsible: four shares to his son and four to the 14th Son, while the remaining two were assigned to some relative. The principal change involved the servants. His Majesty removed them all with the exception of the eunuchs, and divided them among the royal sons and daughters, but kept aside a small number to be punished.

### 15 Fr. Hortiz with two Augustinians is taken to Macau under escort

December 9th. An uneventful day in Beijing. However, in Guangzhou the Most Reverend Father Hortiz was deported to Macau together with two other Reverend Augustinian Fathers,[^p530-21] his subjects, and the magistrates sent an escort with them. The reason for the escort was said to be that previously, when they had been ordered by the Supreme Military Prefect in Shaoqing to leave there and go <!-- PDF page 531 | book page 522 --> directly to Macau, they had spent two months in Guangzhou, and would have stayed longer if they were able to.

### 16 A letter from Fr. Buonaventura da Roma

December 10th. The Rev. Fr. Buonaventura da Roma wrote the following letter from Ji’nan fu[^p531-22] to our Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial, full of patience and edification, and sent a paper written by Rev. Fr. Pedro de la Piñuela, a Franciscan who had died peacefully in the Lord about 7 years previously.[^p531-23]

The letter of Fr. Buonaventura da Roma:[^p531-24]

Today, December 6th, I received Your Reverence’s most welcome letter by the hand of my brethren (namely Fathers Tarin, Manuel Navarro, Miguel Roca, and Nicolas de San José, and Brother Antonio) who are on their way to the Court to receive the imperial permit. I confess I was much consoled by it. It seems that the Lord arranged for me to receive it before I got that one from the Lord Patriarch. For lack of time, I put off sending you a copy, leaving that until I get to Guangzhou. Anyway, that gentleman regards me and calumniates me as a rebel against His Holiness and in addition requires me under pain of suspension from priestly offices to travel via Guangzhou to accept his penalty. The suspension is reserved to His Holiness. After my brethren had left, I received some new orders much clearer. If it turns out that I do have to leave and go to Guangzhou for them to burn me or roast me—whichever they choose—what am I to do? Patience! And to meet all these expenses they provide me with nothing, nor does my own religious order wish to help me even if they could. Your Reverence can imagine how things are with me—fears within, battles without. But that won’t stop me from speaking out, and I shall always stand for the truth, for if things were to turn out the way they unjustly try to make them, I would already be a saint. May God forgive my sins, for which I suffer these hardships. Your Reverence will do me the favour of greeting the other Reverend Fathers.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

With the Emperor’s permission these were made available to all the churches of the kingdom; ministers inscribed them on tablets of gold decorated with the imperial symbols, and they were displayed in suitable public places where they could be seen by magistrates and people. Kilian in Beijing, in some personal distress that nothing had so far been done, at least so far as he was aware, to restore the Most Reverend Lord Patriarch to liberty. He did not elaborate on the matter, and nobody else in any letter has said anything about it to our people in Beijing.

December 15th. Fr. Laureati wrote to the Superior about his efforts to restore the mission in Fujian, and about the extremely weak little group of Christians in the districts of that province, which the secular clergy had been fruitlessly trying to tend for many years. Here is what he says:

### 18 The Fujian Mission: how Bishop Maigrot had looked after it

Before I came to this province, I often heard the Most Illustrious Bishop Maigrot say that it was usual for him not to recognize the neophytes of our Society as Christians, and that he would refuse to give the sacrament of confirmation to any of them. This made me feel more than a little saddened, and I used to think that in comparison with ours the Most Illustrious Lord’s Christians were doubtless all so many Anthonys, so many Hilarions.[^p534-33] My God! How many things are coloured by lies which in your light are revealed! I came to this province and at once saw neophytes flooding in from the most remote places—our own Jesuit neophytes as well as those of the Most Reverend Franciscan and Dominican Fathers—to receive the sacraments after two years of being deprived of them. They eagerly took me to their churches and everywhere I found the same piety and knowledge of the mysteries of faith to reward my labours. Although the number of those baptised was not up to that found in the other missions, the number in the last nine months was no smaller, reaching 400. But when no Christians belonging to the Most Illustrious Lord appeared, I began to wonder: whether they were perhaps so spiritual that they were invisible? Eventually an old man arrived; instead of asking for confession he told me all his domestic woes, and instead of absolution he asked me for a donation of money. When | I refused, he said, “At least give me a piece of that baked bread on the altar” (in Chinese, shaobing).[^p534-34] I was appalled by such ignorance, and I asked myself where on earth were the Christians whom the Illustrious Lord and his many associates had been educating for these twenty years? Then I discovered that the principal, almost the only, mission of His Lordship was in Changle,[^p534-35] to which he betook himself each month, or sent some other priest. Since I could <!-- PDF page 535 | book page 526 --> hardly accept that nobody should have appeared from such a nearby mission (it is about 8 leagues from the city [of Fuzhou]) I sent three messengers there, one after another, to point out the example of the many other churches at least two hundred (Italian) miles distant from here, and encourage people to examine their consciences.

### 19 The Fujian neophytes

Having eventually been told that they did want to see me, I set out for Changle at the invitation of a single neophyte, and was welcomed by three people. On the following day, eleven people altogether came to confession, of whom five were refreshed with the Holy Bread [of Communion]. The other six were full of the rice which they had earlier eaten at home, but said that they would receive the sacrament at a more suitable time.[^p535-36] From that Your Reverence will gather that there was nothing out of the ordinary to be found in the Reverend Lordship’s Christians except extraordinariness. What universal ignorance, what inconstancy. Good God! Two catechists, the best of the bunch, had one of them two sons and the other three daughters. When asked about his sons the first said that their poverty had forced them to go woodcutting; the other said that his daughters lived with infidels, and that the Most Illustrious Lord had given them permission and dispensation, without any previous warning having been given. Now they had turned to sacrilegious rites.[^p535-37] He admitted this with not a tear in his eyes. A year ago, when that dreadful storm fell upon us from all sides, only the very occasional individual among the Jesuit Christians—those whom His Most Illustrious Lordship had dismissed as worthless—denied their faith. But in this mission of His Most Illustrious Lordship, with the exception of three households, the rest flung all their sacred images and every sign of their Christian religion into the fire, or found some less impious hiding place for them …. One possible explanation for this inconsistency might have been that our (Christians) had tablets of the dead, but none of his did. But without exception all of his also had them. What is more than astonishing is the fact that the Most Illustrious Lord and Their Reverences his companions in many places made a chapel for the women in the home of a certain graduate within whose walls one can visit quite a fine ancestor shrine or, as they themselves say, a “detestable shrine” containing several tablets made not in the manner of the other Christians but in that of the gentiles. Moreover, in the finest hall of the whole house, in which the mandarins <!-- PDF page 536 | book page 527 | manuscript page 1089 --> often meet, one can see one of the Eight Spirits (Chinese saints) which they call the Baxian,[^p536-38] not accidentally placed there to hide their faith, but deliberately displayed on the very wall of the chapel itself. The graduate assured me that this had been seen several times by the Most Illustrious Lord himself …. If Your Reverence wishes to know how many people in this place (Changle) have been bathed in the water of salvation I can tell you: just one, a two-year-old girl, daughter of that same graduate. From all this you can rightly deduce the spirit in which that mission was founded, and how seldom God was present in their labours etc.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.

### 23 Fr. Antonino Ripabottoni. His view of the Chinese Rites

Muñoz the Dominican and Fr. ### 24 Why Fr. Kilian may not write to Fr. Iliceto

I am not writing this to damage the reputation of the said Reverend Father, but for his own good, so that through Your Very Reverend Paternity he might be taught to have regard for himself and his people; for he did more harm to himself and his Order by writings of that kind than he profited from the alms which he with great cleverness managed to obtain. I would write to him on this matter myself, just as Your Very Reverend Paternity advises, did I hope it would come about that in charity he <!-- PDF page 539 | book page 530 --> would accept me giving him advice, and profit from it by emending his life. Your Very Reverend Paternity must believe me when I say that I am not concerned about our good name so much as about what will be helpful for you; for that account of his did you more harm than it did us. For eighty years we have had to put up with relations and books and volumes most scurrilously written; they have annoyed us but not really harmed us, and we have preserved our innocence and come out victorious. I will do all in my power to be of service to Fr. Iliceto and I shall write to our Fathers in Macau to look after the man as best they can. They must pay no heed to the derision that will come to them from others for honouring the man with their friendship and kindnesses, despite the fact that he writes so much that is offensive to them and their friends.

4) I regard as the main sign of your sincere friendship that Your Very Reverend Paternity mentions to me the grounds on which Fr. Frusinone bases his mistaken view of us. I would discuss many aspects of this with him if he were nearer at hand; now that he has left for Europe I can only briefly and hastily write to Your Very Reverend Paternity about matters which require to be dealt with at greater length, since I cannot argue against a third party when they are not here. Fr. Frusinone’s reasoning is more or less this: the Bishop of Conon came to Beijing, he came because he was summoned by the Lord Patriarch, and the Lord Patriarch summoned him because the courtiers kept asking the Patriarch what view Europe took of the Chinese Rites. The courtiers put that question because the Jesuits six years earlier had informed the Court and the Emperor about the disagreements between missionaries. That is as far as Fr. Frusinone goes.

### 25 The reason which led the Jesuits to ask the Emperor about the meaning of the Chinese Rites

But I want to take the line of argument he has begun further. I say that the reason the Jesuits informed the Court and the Emperor that there was doubt in Europe about the meaning of the Chinese Rites and asked him to explain the matter to them was because in 1700 the Bishop of Conon both in China and in Europe, in writing and in conversation, challenged the Jesuits, if they believed in their argument about the controversies, to ask the Emperor for an official declaration of the meaning of the Chinese words and of the rites. The Bishop of Conon called for this courageous step to be taken because, as he wrote in his own report in 1695, and as he put it very forcibly through his procurator in 1697 to the Most Sacred Tribunal of the World, that the interpretation of the rites given by the Emperor, the Court, and the empire was quite different from the one given by the Jesuits to Alexander vii in 1656. He made such a fuss about this proposal because he had found <!-- PDF page 540 | book page 531 | manuscript page 1091 --> it in the manuscripts of Fray Varo.[^p540-42] Fr. Varo wrote in this way because he had received this interpretation handed down from Navarrete, Fr. Navarrete had inherited it from Fr. Antonio de Santa Maria and Juan Bautista Morales, and they took this view because they themselves, as they say, heard the testimony of ten Christians who had no qualifications in theology.[^p540-43]

### 26 The Jesuits are not responsible for the Chinese persecution

So here is my question: from this argument of Fr. Frusinone is it reasonable to infer that it is the Jesuits who are responsible for the present misfortunes of the Chinese Church? Rather may I not infer that Rev. Fr. Antonio de Santa Maria and Fr. Juan Bautista Morales are the origin of our misfortunes, past, present, and future? Perhaps Fr. Frusinone, if he were here, would say that those two were indeed the first to have so loudly engaged in controversies, but they were still just between Europeans and did not involve the Chinese, whereas it was through the Jesuits that these matters became widely known to the Chinese and to the Emperor. Let us assume, without conceding, that this was so. But how will that demonstrate that it was the information given by the Jesuits six years ago which gave rise to the present troubles of the mission? It is as if he is saying “So long as the Jesuits were being delated as idolaters only among Europeans, there would have been nothing to be feared in China| either for our Holy Religion because of the rites which Bishop Maigrot condemned, or for the Jesuits’ opponents who were procuring the condemnation of the rites in Rome.”

<!-- PDF page 541 | book page 532 -->

### 27 Who should be held accountable for the damage done to the Chinese Mission

From this I infer: Therefore, it was they [Bishop Maigrot and the adversaries of the Jesuits] who had reason to fear the disputes becoming public in China, and in that case the damage is primarily attributable to the people who first condemned the Rites on their own authority and arranged for them to be condemned by the Holy See, and who wanted to have that condemnation proclaimed throughout China as though it had come from an official source, and indeed did so in terms which mentioned only what was offensive in the condemnation of the Rites.

Even if Fr. Frusinone were to counter by saying that the struggle over the disagreements about the Chinese rites was indecisive, and that the decision against the Rites might have been more severe given the expertise of the informant, the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Conon and his colleagues, now, however, after the Jesuits have used the sword of the Chinese emperor’s authoritative explanation of the Rites to cut the Gordian knot regarding the facts of the case, the rites clearly can neither be condemned nor prohibited without giving offence to the Emperor unless it could first be shown that the Emperor’s interpretation was different from that of the Empire, and that is something which the Bishop of Conon could not have shown. My reply is that for that very reason the Lord Bishop of Conon should have stayed at home rather than displaying his scanty knowledge in the presence of the head of the scholars.[^p541-44] Nor was it appropriate that he was invited by the Lord Patriarch, nor should the Patriarch have been praised by Fr. Frusinone for having issued the invitation. It was rash in the first place to have prohibited the rites in Fujian on his own authority, but I cannot begin to say how foolish it was once the prohibition had been issued to attempt to defend it before the Emperor himself. The Lord Bishop of Conon knew that the interpretation of the Rites had come from His Majesty. The Lord Patriarch knew that it had been joyfully welcomed by the Supreme Pontiff. Those two prelates hope to sideline it with contempt; they hope that it would be possible to compel the Emperor to withdraw his declaration, or to change the opinion of the Empire, or to repeal the laws. This is indeed to hope against hope, and beyond all reason! I wish that Fr. Frusinone had thought about the line-up of the battle which was joined. On the one side is the Emperor of China, who since boyhood had become learned in all the literature of his country, and on the other, the Lord Patriarch, who in matters Chinese is no better informed than a day-old infant, together with the Lord Bishop of Conon who can hardly stammer a single word of Chinese. What kind of victory <!-- PDF page 542 | book page 533 | manuscript page 1092 --> was to be expected? What kind of disaster to be feared? It was not the Jesuits who drew up the battle line and ordered it to engage the enemy hand to hand. What Fr. Frusinone calls the cause of the disaster he should have described as the way by which disaster could have been avoided.

### 28 The interpretation of the Rites and the declaration was given at Bishop Maigrot’s insistence

The interpretation of the Rites which was asked for in 1700 was not merely a shield to protect those who defended the decree of Alexander vii; it was also a well-honed and shining spear which could do damage to anyone who was imprudent enough to run onto it. This metaphor which has escaped my over-eager pen is mistaken, and is more severe than is fair; so, I beg forgiveness, but I would still point out to Your Reverend Paternity that the Jesuits requested the interpretation of the Chinese rites and terms from the Emperor only when the Lord Bishop of Conon thought it should be done. He did not simply suggest it, rather he insisted on it in 1700, when neither we nor the Lord Bishop of Conon had any way of guessing or prophesying that an apostolic nuncio would come who would be bold enough to treat the Emperor’s interpretation in such a way as to incur ill-will for himself and harm to the mission. For in itself the interpretation was harmless, and would have laid open the truth about what had been done in China, would have confirmed Alexander vii’s decree, would have made the conversion of the Chinese easier, would have eased the consciences of the missionaries and brought peace to the Chinese Church.

That this agrees with the copy of the original, I so testify.

### 31 Reasons why the summons should be deemed illegitimate

Firstly, because it is concerned with a forane vicariate, responsibility for which lies with the Most Illustrious Archbishop of Goa, Agostinho da Anunciaçõ, and with the Most Illustrious Bishop of Macau, João de Cazal.[^p544-48] Since it is public knowledge that there is a dispute between Your Most Illustrious Lordship and these <!-- PDF page 545 | book page 536 | manuscript page 1093 --> two prelates, it is quite certain that Your Most Illustrious Lordship’s | jurisdiction is not unchallenged but is in dispute and in doubt so far as concerns this matter.

Secondly, because the above Father has not been informed of any definite place for the hearing. Were Your Illustrious Lordship to say that the place is made sufficiently definite by the fact that you are at present staying in the city of Macau, this does not seem sufficient: the law does not prescribe that the place be determined in this way, as though it could be fixed by where the judge is at present residing.

Thirdly, because there is no assignment of a safe and convenient place, as required by law, to which the person summoned can be compelled to attend. The city of Macau where Your Illustrious Lordship is staying is not for the time being a safe and convenient place: the requirement of an imperial permit, and the circumstances which currently obtain in the city of Macau, make it morally impossible for the aforesaid Father to come to the city: he would be arrested by the mandarins and punished for breaking his undertaking (since he had promised the Emperor not to leave China). Even assuming that he could reach Macau in freedom, before he could in person show his obedience to Your Most Illustrious Lordship, he would either be sent back into China by the prefect of the city,[^p545-49] who would not wish to incur the guilt of admitting someone who had broken an undertaking made to the Emperor; or else the Prefect would imprison him, ordering him to take the first opportunity to sail for Goa, and give an account of himself to the Most Illustrious Metropolitan and the Viceroy of India, following the precedent established in 1707 and applied to other Religious, even those belonging to the prelatures of his own order.[^p545-50]

Fourthly, no definite date is named on which the Father is to appear, despite the requirement of the law that the person summoned should know whether the date is legally fixed or not.

Fifthly, the peremptory time of less than 60 days assigned by Your Most Illustrious Lordship is insufficient. How could the Father get from Nanjing to Macau within 60 days, when Your Most Illustrious Lordship took some 70 days to travel from Nanjing even as far as Guangzhou, and that when travelling under an order from the Emperor.[^p545-51] Moreover, although in accordance with the old law 20 days suffice for each formal warning, in accordance with the new law <!-- PDF page 546 | book page 537 | manuscript page 1094 --> the requirement is for 30 days; taking into account the circumstances—the distance involved, the difficulty of the journey, the winter season, the fragile health of the individual involved, and his advanced age, the aforementioned 30 day period for each formal warning should be lengthened, not shortened. For these and other reasons which in law favour the Father, the above summons is apparently illegitimate and hence invalid. Therefore, with all due respect and deference to Your Illustrious Lordship, I humbly ask and require that you should be so kind as to declare this summons void; in the event that this request is denied, I appeal to our Most Holy Lord Pope Clement xi against the aforementioned summons and against the censures in the aforementioned order of Your Most Illustrious Lordship as constituting an irreparable burden, and I request from Your Most Illustrious Lordship once, again, and again, and as often as necessary to give the required prompt attention[^p546-52] and copies of the records made in connection with this matter, and I swear to all that is required etc., and seek justice, etc. João Baptista of the Society of Jesus, Procurator of the Vice-Province of China.

That this agrees with the authentic transcript, I so witness.

### 33 The Fathers are asked about the Emperor’s illness and how it might be remedied

Also, on December 23rd. Around midday we were all summoned to the Palace, specifically to meet in the inner pharmacy. The first to turn up were Father Parrenin, Father Kilian, and Brother Rodes,[^p547-55] because their residence was nearer. There along with the mandarins and their assistants from the Wuying dian was the eunuch Zhang Qilin, recently promoted to high office by a singular favour from the Emperor, to be known as the “General President of Matters Relating to His Majesty’s Body”, an unprecedented title and function in this dynasty. He, having invited the Fathers to sit down, said that the Emperor professed to be feeling well, but added that he is suffering from palpitations of the heart, which interrupts his sleep and brings other discomforts as well. He said he knew that we were devoted to truth, which was why he was asking us what we thought the cause of his illness might be, and whether we knew of any European remedy for it. Having been given permission to speak freely, the Fathers replied: without doubt the illness stemmed from the sorrow which had clouded the serenity of his Majesty’s heart for almost four months; as a result, his blood has contracted melancholy humours, infected with which his heartbeat does not circulate his blood properly, which has a bad effect on small parts of his heart. The remedy known to them was a tincture of alkermes,[^p547-56] though there are other medicines too in our books. Then the others arrived: Fathers

<!-- PDF page 548 | book page 539 -->

Grimaldi, Pereira, Soares, and Castner, and Brother Baudino. The same question was put to them by the eunuch, and the same reply was given. Although, speaking with one another in a European language, we considered other possibilities which occurred to us, the consensus of the more expert opinion was that we should not offer anything other than that medicine; we were not doctors after all, and this was the only thing we could recommend while trying to be of service and without doing any harm. The eunuch insisted that, if there were anything else in our books that might be suitable, we should mention it. It could be offered to His Majesty so that he could choose what he wanted. At that point Brother Baudino remembered that in the royal pharmacy were kept some formulae for medicines, which he mentioned to the eunuch. The eunuch ordered that the list be brought out; one of those present suggested that Signor Borghese, the doctor of the Most Illustrious Lord Patriarch, might be called. The eunuch agreed, and he was summoned by a secretary. Meanwhile, since a tincture of alkermes could not immediately be found in the pharmacy, Brother Rodes took some from our residence; the pharmaceutical handbook which we consulted stated that an artificial mixture of some kind, prepared from the heart of a wether, was just as effective as a tincture of alkermes for palpitations. It was recommended by Lazare Rivière, whose work had been translated into Chinese by the Jesuits.[^p548-57] The eunuch asked for more details: had we ever seen this used to good effect? What was the dose, how was it to be administered, at what time should it be taken, and how long should one expect a cure to take? We replied that we had often seen it being used effectively. Fathers Kilian and Soares added that in previous years they had used it themselves; half a dram or a whole dram or more could be taken; it worked more speedily if taken in wine; sometimes no effect was experienced after the first dose, especially if the illness had been getting worse for some time. But without doubt His Majesty could expect to feel some improvement after the second or third dose, and after the sixth, seventh, and eighth he would be free of the palpitations. The eunuch had all this written down. As Doctor Borghese, who had been summoned, was entering the room, the eunuch said: “Our master never asked us to consult this man; so, let us hear from him separately.” The secretary who had fetched him first led him into a separate wing where formerly the Fathers used to translate <!-- PDF page 549 | book page 540 | manuscript page 1095 --> European books into Chinese and where Gherardini[^p549-58] | used to teach the eunuchs and other pupils to paint. Then, while the previous recommendations were being written down, the doctor was led into the laboratory. The entire pharmacy consists of a single building about fifty feet long divided by walls between the beams into three rooms, one of which is to store medicines and their ingredients, one is used for the preparation and distillation of medicines, and the middle one, which is only about 10 feet wide, is intended for prescribing and consultation, and also serves as a staff dining-room. Here we were, then, eight Jesuits and the appointed eunuch, two mandarins, two secretaries, and another two eunuchs experienced in pharmaceuticals. I am relating all this in detail because of the calumny with which the doctor later assailed us, which he hurled so strongly at us because he was offended that he was not consulted in the same place as us, but in the laboratory next door, as I have said.

### 34 Signor Borghese is interrogated about the illness

With Father Parrenin as interpreter, the eunuchs questioned the doctor as he had us, except that he did not want the doctor to be told that the sick man under consideration was the Emperor. The doctor replied with what he judged to be helpful advice for that condition. The eunuch did not criticise what the doctor said, but he asked what, in addition to a regime of diet and exercise, the doctor would recommend by way of medicaments. The doctor replied that there was hardly any need for medicines, but that if the trouble persisted a vein could be cut to draw off some blood. When that had been said, the eunuch was invited to supper while we waited in the same place, some of us chatting to the doctor and others making sure that the secretaries were correctly translating into Manchu what had previously been said in Chinese or was contained in the pharmaceutical handbook. The eunuch did not wish to write down what the doctor had said, saying that it was sufficient to have heard what he thought, and His Majesty could not be offered things which he had not asked for.

### 35 The Fathers encourage the Emperor to get over his grief, which is making him ill

Fathers Pereira, Soares, and Kilian agreed with one another that on this occasion they should make a bolder move. So, somewhat apart from the others at the door of the hall, they spoke more informally to the eunuch as <!-- PDF page 550 | book page 541 --> he came back from supper, and by their expressions, their tone of voice, and their words conveyed how concerned they were for His Majesty’s health, that they wished the best possible treatment for him, and that nothing held out the promise of a quick and effective cure unless he could be rid of what caused the melancholy spirit or humour. They admitted they were not doctors but, like any sensible man, they could see that grief had eaten its way into the Emperor’s very heart. As any father would, he could not help himself being saddened by the mistakes of his sons, but he had this unique gift beyond all others, that his prudence could control his sadness, and the wisdom that comes with age could turn the youthful excesses of his sons to the benefit of the Royal House and so set an example to the whole of posterity. Let him remember his previous strength, which had snuffed out vicious rebellions as it were in a single moment, conquered numerous enemies, transformed wars into peace treaties, and brought tranquillity to everyone living under the Chinese heaven. Let him therefore restore peace also to himself, and understand that in the eyes of the wise no damage is done to his great reputation by the base malice of the few individuals by whom the royal sons had been led astray. There are always evil people in the world, and their evil machinations cannot impede the greatness of the wise; rather they render it all the clearer and more august. Say to him that in our small way and out of the reverential love which we owe him, we beg him not to allow his heart, which at other times governs with such gentleness, to stay hardened any longer. Let him settle the business, to the great benefit of his reputation. It was with willing ears that the eunuch listened to this; he asked us to say some of it over again, as though he were disagreeing, saying that it was a very difficult task, more so than we could understand. Of course, the Emperor, with the loftiness of his mind, well understood how to avoid the sharp reefs which for long lie hidden under the surface of the waters, but his body lacked strength, sleep had deserted him, he had lost interest in food, and peace of mind was nowhere to be found, neither in the privacy of his house nor out of doors in his gardens. There was nothing in his troubled heart to help his immense spirit with its interior struggle. When the Fathers and the eunuch had for some time exchanged arguments about how to remove the cause of grief, the eunuch calmly promised to convey the message. He quickly collected all the notes, together with the tincture of alkermes and a few ounces of wine to be taken with it, so as to carry it all from the royal pharmacy to the Villa. He left a little after the fourth hour of the evening, taking with him the mandarins and the secretaries and asking Fr. Parrenin to go at once to the Villa as interpreter, together with Brothers Baudino and Rodes, who are both pharmacists.

Also, on the 23rd. When the Emperor had sent the message that we should assemble for the meeting I mentioned, he also sent someone to summon the

<!-- PDF page 551 | book page 542 | manuscript page 1096 -->

Second Son from the prison to the Villa. Fathers Parrenin and Kilian discovered that by chance as they were crossing the marble bridge which provides a way across the royal fishpond in front of the | Palace. They saw the Prefect of the Stables, a man by the name of Wu She,[^p551-59] alone and without a servant, going slowly past on horseback but in a manner which suggested he was on business. It all seemed rather odd to the Fathers, and they inquired what this might mean. They were in no doubt that somebody else must be nearby to whom this man was to act as a companion or a servant. He replied in a low voice, “The Prince is coming out”. There was no question of saying anything more, or asking any more questions, and they hurriedly left the bridge. But because the situation was so unusual, they were unsure whether they should wait on their knees for the person to pass. Then they noticed that people were being told to stand back a little, though they were not required to make any sign of honour in the usual way. So, keeping out of the way round a corner of the square, they saw the Prince travelling in a cart which was covered by a black cloth inlaid with gold, and with red wheels, of the kind used by women below the rank of queen. A few horsemen preceded and followed the cart, but not in the order of a formal guard, because the Emperor wished the departure to be unrecognized.

### 36 The death of Father Tomás Pereira

Also, on December 23rd. Late in the evening while Fr. Tomás Pereira was reclining with the others at supper, he complained quite a lot about a headache, and eventually felt that his right arm was becoming cold, and showed it to the others. Soon he could no longer stand up, and his forehead sank down on to the table. The Fathers persuaded him to go to bed, to which he was helped by two servants. While he was on the way he asked for his confessor Fr. Soares to be called, together with Fr. Grimaldi. They came running, and found that he was too ill to speak, but having been asked for a sign, he received Holy Absolution. Physical remedies were administered, but to no effect. So Extreme Unction was also given, and about a quarter past midnight the Father peacefully passed away. This death was very sudden, but nevertheless was in one way blessed, in that Fr. Pereira had that same day completed the observances which were required to gain the Jubilee granted by His Holiness our Lord Pope Clement xi for European peace.[^p551-60]

### 37 The medicine given to the Emperor by the Fathers proves useful

December 25th. Many Christians were present, as they had been on the previous Sunday as well, to receive the sacraments of confession and the eucharist. After dinner, Fathers Parrenin and Kilian and Brothers Baudino and Rodes were invited to the palace pharmacy. (The Fathers in the College were not invited because of the funeral of Fr. Pereira.) We were to know, also, that previously, on the 23rd, His Majesty had ordered the Eunuch Chamberlain Wei to try the medicine as an experiment, and that it had been such an excellent experience that he would use it in future. We were to offer it, if we had it to hand. At once Brother Rodes went to get some from our house, and brought it. It was kindly received and brought to the Emperor by the three men just mentioned.

### 38 The Emperor orders the Fathers to name one of their number to replace Fr. Pereira

Also on December 26th. The eunuch Zhang Qilin and the prefect Zhao came to the residence within the Saffron Wall about two in the afternoon, summoning all the Europeans from the three churches. Everyone came <!-- PDF page 553 | book page 544 | manuscript page 1097 --> except Father Gonzaga and Brother Vieira, for whom there were no horses available at such short notice. When we had all gathered the delegates put it to us that | His Majesty had said that we should take account of the fact that Father Pereira was dead; he needed to find a replacement for him, who would be his mouth and ears for conveying his commands and presenting our business and petitions, whether for us all or for an individual. So, we should choose someone from among us whom we judge to be as the dead man was, a reliable man, upright of heart, one who detests falsehood, and who in carrying out business relating to the Lord Patriarch will not depart from the path to which the Emperor has hitherto kept. In making our choice, the Emperor leaves us free to propose names between which he will decide whom be believes to be suitable. His Majesty would except Father Grimaldi and Antoine Thomas, whose great age and frailty would not allow them to go out to the Villa if anything urgent came up, etc. He also excluded Father Joachim Bouvet, but in his case did not give any reason. We replied first of all by expressing our most humble gratitude that His Majesty, in the midst of so many and such important affairs of state and his own household business, should find time to think of us and our needs. Secondly, we have all had an intimate knowledge of His Majesty’s insightfulness, and he should choose whom he wishes; we wished for nothing better than to accede to his good pleasure. The delegates would not accept this reply, and Zhao, pretending that the suggestion had just occurred to him, said that perhaps we might propose two or three names and allow the Emperor to select one. Fr. Antoine Thomas gave us this same advice, speaking in a European language, when the votes were being counted in our first ballot. Everyone thought this was a good idea. Fr. Soares, was nominated first, Fr. Kilian second, and in third place some nominated Fr. Parrenin and others Fr. Castner. However, the delegates presented the names of Fathers Soares, Kilian, and Parrenin to the Emperor.[^p553-61] As the Eunuch was leaving, we asked him whether the Emperor had taken any of the tincture of alkermes. He replied that he had, and that it had done him good.

### 39 Finding one of the Emperor’s sons as a replacement for the disinherited Crown Prince

Also, on December 26th. It is said that the Emperor consulted the grandees and magnates of the Empire about which of his sons they thought should be appointed as Crown Prince and successor. They are reported to have said that they thought the Eighth Son should be chosen, <!-- PDF page 554 | book page 545 --> though they knew that His Majesty’s own inclination was to restore the Second Son. The magnates gave that answer not because they had any doubt at all that His Majesty would restore the Second Son, but to let him know how much damage could be done by too hasty a decision in such important matters, suggesting that in future he might first summon his ministers and listen to their advice and be more inclined to take a decision after discussion with them.

### 41 A letter from Fr. Appiani about the death of Fr. Pereira

Also, on December 27th or the 26th, a note was sent to me which Fr. Appiani had written. Fr. Appiani had not written down the date, and I left it out by mistake. Here is an exact copy of the note:

Very Reverend Father,

You surely know that it was only yesterday evening that I received the truly unexpected and sad news of the death of the beloved Fr. Pereira. No piece of paper like this can convey how I felt when I had word of it, and I can limit my grief only by recalling the teaching of the Apostle, “We would not have you be ignorant concerning those who are falling asleep; you should not be sad as those are who have no hope.”[^p556-66] I hope and trust in the Lord Jesus that he has passed over into the sleep of eternal rest, while waiting to receive the consolation and reward for the labours he has undergone in the hard and truly difficult task of spreading the good news of the Gospel. Nevertheless, since I myself am still unworthy to offer the unbloody sacrifice, I have recited the Office of the Dead for him, and intend to go on doing so for as long as God gives me, while being ready to celebrate Masses in the Lord’s own time if he so wills.[^p556-67] Father Pereira’s passing has taught me where I too must go, for God grants to those who fear him a sign to prepare themselves for the passing of that irrevocable judgement. God grant that, having been found watching, I may reply “I come,” for it is for that that I was born. I do not know the day or the circumstances of his death; please, would Your Reverence tell me? In my last letter I did not criticise a single European, nor did I wish to. Nor did my words, so far as I am aware, give any grounds for suspicion however slight. That person should have <!-- PDF page 557 | book page 548 | manuscript page 1099 --> been blamed, lest anyone approaching him should wander from the path of true life; from the abundance of his heart, he admitted that if he held the staff of this tripod, within ten years he would not leave the name of the Christian religion. But he attacks that enemy who knows how to profit from temptation, to humiliate and overcome it. May it please God, our Omnipotent Majesty, to inspire Christian princes with better counsels, that there may be peace, that so the Christians may increase in numbers, more fervently and more abundantly even as they now seem to be reduced to the depth of calamity. The state of this mission is truly lamentable because of the fewness of workers. May God, through an abundance of His Spirit and of His holy gifts, make up for what we lack. Nothing remains for me, worthless and born out of due time, but to offer my cold prayers. I do not write letters, nor have I any inclinations to do so. I entrust myself to Divine Providence who has an abundant care for the [poor], and takes care of me through faithful ministers his servants, not through ravens.[^p557-68]

### 42 Three of our men are selected to receive the Emperor’s commands

Also, on December 27th. The Prefect Zhao was sent from the Villa by the Emperor, and ordered us all to gather in the residence inside the Saffron Wall. At the third hour, he arrived, alone. We were ordered to kneel, and he revealed that it was the Emperor’s word that since some people had voted for Father Soares and others for Father Kilian, and Father Parrenin himself was used to accompanying the Emperor on his journeys, these three Fathers together should constitute the group through whom his commands and our business should be conveyed. We all thanked him, and assured him once more of the admiration in which we held His Majesty’s breadth of heart, which, though full of many and at this time unusually painful cares, is still so kind as to find room for the tiny details of our affairs.

### 43 The Emperor’s Secondborn Son is restored to liberty

Afterwards, in conversation, Zhao said that today the magnates had met in the Villa to propose a successor to His Majesty, and had once again proposed the Eighth

<!-- PDF page 558 | book page 549 -->

Son. To this the Emperor replied that the Eighth Son certainly was talented, but was less strong-minded than the battle-lines of business required. The Second Son had indeed done wrong, but beneath his wrongdoing was a cause which was not of his own making; the magnates should therefore weigh which of them should be preferred. When they had reflected, they for the third time replied that they were all ready to give complete submission to whichever of the sons His Majesty should appoint as heir; for the rest, they had no further advice to give him. The secret votes of both the Tartar and the Chinese Magnates were collected by the First Minister. When unlocked, all the Chinese votes bore the character Ba, “Eight”, and the Tartar ones the word Tescha Kuntcha, which also means “Eight.” They begged the Emperor not to delay in settling the business by his own choice and authority. The Emperor listened to them, and said that among his sons, none of them equalled the Secondborn in the talents required to rule, but since the reasons for and against were in conflict, and the Emperor himself still enjoyed full health, a definite choice could be postponed; what would be done for the moment was that he should take charge of the Second Son and instruct him properly, and then reconsider what it would be advisable to do. At the same time, The Emperor ordered that the Palace of the Second Son (or the Deposed Son) which was still closed, should be opened, and restored to its original state in terms of furniture, the number of eunuchs, and the remaining staff of male and female servants. At the same time, he restored to freedom his Thirteenth Son, and his two little grandsons, the children of the deposed Prince.

### 44 His speech to the leaders of the Empire

At about the fifth hour of the evening we heard that the Second Son at his father’s wish had met with the assembled magnates, and had spoken roughly in the following words:

Pillars of the Empire! You know the mistakes I have made, and how I was dragged into those mistakes. So I would not allow myself to think that you would seek to elevate me to the pinnacle of government. But think only of this: what would be for the public good, and what would be most helpful to preserve the Royal House which Heaven has moved from the deserts of Tartary to the midst of the kingdom, and which blesses its subjects with tranquillity and happiness. I give you but a single piece of advice: let not private passion seduce you. I know that you would never allow your good judgement, your integrity, to be warped by bribes, or hatred, or love. There is but one fear which might trouble my heart: whether, were it ever to happen that I began to govern, I would revenge myself on those among you who, when my mistakes were made public, agreed that I should be deposed from the inheritance of Empire. Do not, I therefore exhort you, trouble your breast with this concern. If you <!-- PDF page 559 | book page 550 | manuscript page 1100 --> were to think it right to exclude me from the throne, there will be no opportunity for me to take revenge. If you judge that it would be for the preservation of the Empire to restore me to my former position, why should I even consider revenge? Here I am, in your midst, and before Heaven I swear that I will for ever observe an inviolable amnesty in either case.

However, people, and more especially those in the know, believed that the magnates had agreed among themselves that they should all | take the same line even though not all of them had known about or consented to the deposition of the Prince, because there was a more secure hope that all would escape unharmed if they abstained from any further vote and were simply to give allegiance to whichever prince the Emperor saw fit to choose.
