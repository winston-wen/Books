On November 1st only Fr. Pereira, Fr. Kilian, and Brother Baudino went to the Palace. Absolutely nothing happened. When the last two had left, a eunuch called Zhang Qilin came out through the Dragon Gate looking for Fr. Pereira in the Qingyin gong and, without indicating that he was doing any errand for the Emperor, simply asked him why he had come. The Father replied that he had come as he usually did, with no particular purpose in mind. The eunuch inquired whether he had heard about what was going on in the Court (about the Crown Prince). “I have heard many things here and there,” the Father replied. “We don’t pay any attention to rumours, still less believe them when such things have nothing to do with us. We have come to Court every single day to make respectful inquiries about His Majesty’s wellbeing, for we were concerned that he must have suffered because of his most unfortunate troubles.” The Father’s words were conveyed to the Emperor who ordered an answer to be conveyed: mini beie sain—“My body (meaning my person) is in good health.”

### 1 Various writings appear concerning the Prince

November 2nd and 3rd. There was a deluge of announcements, some of them even in print, because the Emperor wanted to show the world how justified was the anger which made him disinherit his son and exclude him from the succession. Many of his wiser advisors regretted that the restraints on his feelings had been so far relaxed that the details of the prince’s adolescent failings and the Palace’s disapproval should be brought into the light of day, as if he were any mere commoner; it would have been more dignified to limit the revelations to one capital offence, if such there were, which would be proceeded against, and cast a cloak of silence over the rest as not deserving of death and deposition from the succession. Even if it would all serve to arouse public opinion against the prince, it would equally provide grounds for criticising the Emperor and bringing shame upon the imperial family for his failing to bring the Crown Prince up properly, not to mention the fact that his brothers by comparison were hardly any better. So, some people hoped that in the Chinese manner some advisors would emerge who, with an admirable freedom, would restrain the Emperor from over-reacting in that way. But that was precisely what the Emperor was afraid might happen; so, he made it impossible for his advisors to carry out their duty and give him wise counsel by ordering that no Chinese mandarin should be allowed to enter the Palace during these days and having several wagons loaded with chains and fetters brought into the Palace to strike terror into the

### 3 The Prince’s ambition and nefarious plots

November 5th. All the buildings and the wall intended to form the Crown Prince’s prison were completed, and …

… on the 6th, between the third and fourth watch of the night, he was led there. The title huang taizi (August Prince)[^p491-3] was first removed from him and in its place he was given the common title er age, that is, “second-born son.”[^p491-4] Once the Crown Prince had been condemned to prison for life, the Firstborn Prince, with his own interests in mind, began to dislodge the remaining princes and especially the eighth from the Emperor’s favour. To achieve this he used various devices, succeeding at least to the extent that those at Court who thought themselves in the know lost no time in secretly congratulating the ambitious young man on how it had all worked out. He couldn’t reject the congratulations; indeed he was so encouraged by them that he stupidly dropped his guard against those whom he knew to have been loyal to or supporters of the Crown Prince; meanwhile he vented his madness on the younger ones until a more convenient way of taking revenge on the older ones should present itself.

We Europeans were afraid both for our own safety and for our Holy Religion, since he had so often said that he was and would remain its enemy. Since there were no other ways either to pacify the lion-cub who was already devouring people or to evade his claws, in our prayers we handed our troubles over to Heaven. And in the Providence of God it came about that, because he was even savaging the Crown Prince’s eunuchs, the Emperor’s eunuchs cunningly informed His Majesty not about the eunuchs, some of whom had already been beaten to death by the Firstborn Prince, but about the chief craftsmen who had done some highly skilled work under the Prince’s supervision, saying that it was a tragedy that the people who had decorated the Emperor’s museum and the Queens’ chambers with works of art which would astound <!-- PDF page 492 | book page 483 | manuscript page 1067 --> posterity should now suffer a miserable death. The Emperor made inquiries: “Which of the craftsmen were suffering at the Firstborn Prince’s hands?” The eunuchs gave the names of the ones who from time to time had presented a specimen of their art for the Emperor to look at: this one they said has been beaten to death, that one strangled, another had taken his own life with a knife, others have fled, and others have pretended to run away and gone into hiding. When they saw that the Prince’s behaviour displeased the Emperor, they at once added that the craftsmen were people of inferior rank whom they would never have mentioned had they not seen and heard mandarins too, even some of high-rank, who were afraid they were going to be subjected to the same treatment, and were preparing to pre-empt the Prince’s cudgels and swords.

### 4 He is rebuked by his father

The Emperor had no doubt that the chamberlains were probably right, knowing the character of his son whose brutality he had himself often made use of to punish others. He considered how inopportune it would be at this moment for senior people to be turned against his house either by the threat or by the actual exercise of savagery. On the morning of the day after the Prince had met with the officials in the Yangxin dian, he summoned the Prince and ordered him to resign from all his official positions, upbraiding him for his untimely severity, which he would do better to replace with peaceful benevolence. |

In front of the doors of the Dragon Gate there was a crowd of officials along with almost a hundred people, among whom were some of our people, who had been summoned to meet the Prince and give him a conquering hero’s welcome immediately upon his return. Hitherto whenever he had returned from Tartary he had never gone to the office in that courtyard. But here he was—the Prince, coming out, his face very flushed and he himself not a little discomfited! He first took the President[^p492-5] aside at quite a distance from the crowd and whispered in his ear that he had been deposed from his magistracy and was going home. Quicker than the rock poured out water for Moses, the President burst into floods of tears.[^p492-6] The Prince did not wait for words of sympathy either from the President or the rest of the crowd, but rushed off; but we, as though the clouds had parted and Phoebus come into sight, began to think the omens offered hope for calmer times for ourselves and our religion.

### 5 The deceptions of the Prince to destroy his brother

A further and very sad act in the drama soon began, which posed a real threat to the royal family. As I said, more than any other of his brothers the Prince feared the eighth-born son,[^p493-7] by common consent a young man who was Heaven’s gift to government. To destroy him, the Prince made the fourth son[^p493-8] envious, so that he could trick the young man into confiding any eagerness for ruling which he might feel in his heart. To carry out the trick, the fourth son brought in a soothsayer who foretold that the eighth son would become Emperor. The eighth son didn’t reject the prophecy, and the fourth son, along with the other brothers (the Prince had deliberately absented himself) accused him of unlawful ambition.

November 12th. It is extraordinary what a blow this news was to the Emperor. He immediately ordered the eighth son to be led by a chain round his neck to the royal stables where the Crown Prince had been interrogated at the beginning of the affair, and to be kept under guard by fifty handpicked soldiers. The soothsayer was arrested immediately, and an interrogation made it clear that he had been invited not by the eighth son but by the fourth. That made the eighth son’s fault appear less grave, and the fourth son was beaten by his father. Since the chameleon-like [Firstborn] Prince had not shown himself among the accusers nor made any obvious move against the eighth son, the Emperor did not at once realize that he was hand in glove with the fourth son and the other brothers to bring about the downfall of the eighth son, so he directed the punishment at the soothsayer.

On November 14th the unfortunate soothsayer, so good at knowing the fortunes of others and so bad at recognising his own impending fate, was taken down to the torture square, where he underwent a butchery, being cut into the tiniest pieces for almost six hours; the spectacle was witnessed by a huge crowd, including one prince and one noble whom His Majesty had ordered to be present from start to finish on the grounds that they had often availed themselves of the soothsayer’s services and so deserved some share in his punishment

On the 14th of the month too, the eighth son was released from his chains and sent back to his home, but stripped of the title of Imperial Marquis,[^p493-9] an office to which his outstanding conduct over several years had brought great distinction.

### 6 The nephew of the Emperor Wanli is executed along with his sons and nephews

On November 16th the tragedy of the grandson of the Emperor Wanli, a member of the previous Tai Ming dynasty, entered its final act.[^p494-10] Throughout the rule of the Tartars, this man had lived a quiet private life in the regions of the south, where he had three sons and two grandsons. He had neither the fortune nor the influence which would have brought him to anyone’s notice, or made him in any way threatening. The danger came from the uninvited initiative of some bonzes and bandits. At the beginning of this year, a rumour went round all China that the wheel of empire was on the turn, and that the former rulers would re-emerge from the depths into which in their turn the Tartars would be plunged. However, Heaven smiled upon the Tartar Emperor; many of the rebels were captured, including their leaders and bonzes, who gave people to understand that the intending rebels had decided to produce this ancient Chinese octogenarian as the rightful claimant of his ancestral empire, in order to attract the populace to themselves; they further claimed that the old man was somehow aware of the plan. That is why after the others | he too was eventually brought to Beijing this month. He was questioned about the truth of all this and made some vague replies, so that the Emperor himself had made up his mind to allow him to live out the rest of his life in the royal mausoleum of his ancestors, and sent him there. But further difficulties arose afterwards, and a charge was lodged against him by the magistrates. While it appears to have been true that he had in no way contributed to the rebellion, nonetheless he had never denied that he had been aware of it, and he had done nothing to report it to the authorities. So, he was condemned to death, and was publicly cut into four pieces, while his sons and grandsons were beheaded.

Meanwhile the Emperor’s fourth and eighth sons, just as a fisherman is alerted from having had a bite, realised that their previous friendship for one another had been turned into enmity by the devices of the [Firstborn] Prince, and were now eager to reveal his treachery to their father as well. Nor could it have remained hidden for much longer, since there were many sparks to indicate that something unpleasant was cooking in his seething heart. I am not sure which was the clue which induced the Emperor on

November 19th to order the Prince to be brought from the Villa to his own residence inside the city by two eunuchs; one of these was the Principal Chamberlain, <!-- PDF page 495 | book page 486 --> Li Yu, and the other Liang,[^p495-11] the first among the four universal presidents of the eunuchs. They told him not to go out of doors until the pleasure of their father the Emperor was made known to him.

### 7 The Emperor’s pain and anxiety etc. about his sons

And so, with his first and second sons both being subjected to heavy penalties for their offences, and the whole crowd of the rest of them hardly blameless, the Emperor began to be tormented with incredible depression, wondering to whom he should hand on the kingdom, to whom he could entrust his safety, and whose judgment he could rely on. His fear that the [Firstborn] Prince had done to the Crown Prince exactly what he had done to the eighth son, bringing him down by underhand trickery, was certainly not groundless. Cares engulfed him, anxiety overcame him, he could not sleep, and His Majesty’s body was growing flabby. Now a sick man, the Emperor took himself off to a completely secret hideaway in the orchards round his villa, stationed his bodyguard inside the very gardens, and forbade access to all dignitaries and even to his own sons, until some light could dispel the darkness surrounding so many of his affairs. In pursuit of this light, the Emperor ordered all the [Firstborn] Prince’s servants, the officials as well as the underlings, to be herded into the Prince’s villa, shut up inside, and questioned for more than a few days. The Prince with the women who had been left at home, along with some eunuchs, was placed under the scrutiny of the gate guards.

### 8 A letter from the Bishop of Beijing to Fr. Vice-Provincial in which he exonerates and praises the Fathers

Some days ago, I passed through the city of Linqing, and in conversation I mentioned the Emperor’s latest decree about the piao (permit) for the Europeans. On that occasion the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing and the Rev. Fr. Carlo (Castorano) mentioned the letter which they had sent to Fr. Vice-Provincial and the reply which they had had from him. They went on to say: 1) that they had never believed that it was by or through the Beijing Jesuits that additions had been made to the decree which might displease the Europeans. 2) What they had written to the Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial had been written in a friendly spirit and with sympathy for the Beijing Fathers, because anything in that decree which does not please the clerics would certainly be attributed in Europe to the Jesuits; they themselves however had never in any way wished to present the Beijing Fathers as somehow guilty. 3) They had taken it very much amiss that, when they had written with such goodwill and affection, they had received a rather brusque response inviting them to Court to examine something which they had never criticised or said anything against, especially since to start on such an inquiry | was not merely dangerous but utterly impossible. 4) The Most Illustrious Bishop added that now, that is to say, after he had written the aforesaid letters, and had had a reply, he had at least some grounds for writing to Europe on behalf of the Beijing Fathers to avert the calumnies to which that decree will give rise. When I was speaking to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing, I said that I understood that the Most Reverend Franciscan Fathers in Guangzhou had sought and obtained permission from the mandarins to go to Beijing to apply for the permit, but also that I did not know whether His Excellency the Lord Patriarch had replied to the questions about whether, in applying for the permit, someone might depart in even the slightest manner from his regulation. I gathered from his reply (and not without grounds) the some have it as a principle not to leave the mission of their own accord, but to apply for the permit in such a way that they will be expelled from the empire by the Emperor. This principle is more or less tantamount to wishing to push the mission finally over the edge.

Liba fu,[^p496-14] 19 November 1708.

### 9 Testimony of the Bishop of Beijing about Fr. Jean-François Gerbillon

In a letter of August 7th. the Lord Patriarch had rendered his decision to Fr. de Goville, Procurator on behalf of the Society against the appointment of Fr. Visdelou as a bishop. In the letter not only did he find Fr. Laureati guilty of ambitioning for the post of apostolic vicar, on which more below, but also accused Fr. Gerbillon of having desired and then having tried to be appointed Coadjutor Bishop of Beijing, in the following words: “We must not keep silent to you about what we have so far wished to remain secret, namely, …. that Fr. Gerbillon, the founder of your mission and its superior while he was alive, so far from wishing to have nothing to do with the dignity of the episcopate, more than once requested to be made Coadjutor of the Most Reverend Lord Bishop of Beijing, making it clear that the endowment of the church was etc.”[^p497-15]

When this formal charge became known in Beijing, Fr. Visitor ordered Fr. Parrenin at once to safeguard the honour of his well-deserving former superior. So the Father wrote to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing, who, in a reply dated November 19th, says:[^p497-16] “Of my most dear Fr. Jean-François Gerbillon I can <!-- PDF page 498 | book page 489 | manuscript page 1070 --> say in all truthfulness that neither he nor the Lord Patriarch ever spoke to me about him being my coadjutor. I cannot believe that he would have spoken to the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch, since I have always known him as an excellent Religious. I hope in the Lord that he will be in Heaven and will pray to God Our Lord for us. What makes me laugh about this incident, is that Your Paternity is writing about a coadjutor. Not even the Lord Patriarch could have done that, nor would I have permitted such an insult to His Most Serene Majesty the King of Portugal.”[^p498-17]

### 10 A letter of Fr. Castorano to Fr. Kilian

November 22nd. The Rev. Fr. Castorano wrote the following answer from Linqing to Fr. Kilian when he received the letter from Fr. Giovanni Baptista da Iliceto, which I had shared with him as a friend the previous month of October.

Most Reverend and dearest Father in Christ,

On 15th of this month, I got Your Very Reverend Paternity’s letter written from Beijing on October 29th together with the brief note and what is claimed to be Fr. Iliceto’s letter. I was delighted to read that Your Most Reverend Paternity’s health is fine, and may God keep it that way for a long time. Thank you, too, for the news you shared, and for remembering and keeping up your friendship for me, undeserving though I am.

When I was in the city of Dongqiang fu[^p498-19] early this month, in the mission there, | I heard news from Beijing, in rather vague terms. It was reported that the Crown Prince had been deposed and is confined in prison for trying to poison his father, but God did not permit such an atrocity to take place, and so disposed things that the plan rebounded on the head of its author. We are living <!-- PDF page 499 | book page 490 --> in peace hereabouts, but have to put up with serious hunger because of the shortages due to the meagre harvest. This was caused by the persistent drought which has lasted almost this entire year.

### 11 Fr. Castorano excuses Fr. Iliceto

In connection with the brief news about the sad state of the Chinese mission and the letter sent by Fr. Iliceto to the Most Reverend Fr. Commissary in Mexico, I have no formal comment to make, since I cannot get a quick answer from him to my questions, so as to discover what is the state of affairs. But for the sake of saying something, here are two comments: first, in terms of what I know, and then in terms of what I might foresee. The first is that I very much doubt the brief notice was written by Fr. Iliceto himself, since I do not recognize his personal style and turn of phrase. It follows that both the meaning and intention was conveyed by others. I have had a number of letters from him, the last one dated 15 March 1708; in them he says that he has to put up with many attacks from his brethren and from the citizens—and especially from the Capitan General—in Macau, to the extent that he had to go into hiding in the hills. The last time he wrote to me was to tell me that he and four French missionaries had gone off to some hermitage or other called Nostra Señora do Peña.[^p499-20] So I can say of him just what he says, or people say, about the Emperor: what has come out and been made public has not come from the depths of himself, but perhaps has its source elsewhere and in someone else’s point of view. If that is the case, as I am very much afraid it is, I have no doubt that when he has been informed of the true state of affairs by Your Very Reverend Paternity, he will undergo a complete change of heart and mind; at the beginning he did have a different opinion of Your Paternities, and indeed was hoping to obtain his permit with your help; he wrote to me that I should make friends with Fr. Romain Hinderer, of whom in his letter he spoke very highly. The previous letter addressed to the Most Reverend Fr. Commissary was written by him on 10 March 1708. I will take Your Very Reverend Paternity’s advice and write to him, asking him to let me know how things are, and telling him that, whether what he says happened was true or false, he should be more careful not to spread unsavoury stories about his neighbours. I can testify, though, that the Most Reverend Father never boasted in Shaanxi that he had opened your letters when he was in Linqing fu; indeed, he flatly denied it. For when the Most Illustrious Bishop encouraged him to stay there with your Father in peace, he replied “I will do that.” There was just one occasion, <!-- PDF page 500 | book page 491 | manuscript page 1071 --> he said, when there was some suspicion that he might have opened a letter of his. “But,” he said, “it was not true: I had opened only the Chinese envelope, as he had given me leave to do, to retrieve my own letters.” This I know because he told me so. So far as I am concerned, I hope that I will never do anything of the kind, nor will I send letters either through yourselves or anyone else. Our Most Illustrious Lord is grateful to you for sending the letters of the aforementioned Father to him from Shaanxi, or allowing them to be sent. He trusts that the fire of Your Very Reverend Paternity’s charity will not be extinguished by these waters! Indeed, the Most Illustrious Bishop himself has something similar to put up with on his own account: a letter of his sent last Easter destined for the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch by way of the Very Rev. Fr. Antoine Thomas, was returned only in December, opened by the Abbate di S. Giorgio in Macau. The Bishop did nothing about it, other than to say he was thankful that at least he had got it back.

### 12 About Fr. Antonio Frossoloni

I am glad that Fr. Antonio (Frossoloni) arrived safely in India from the mission, and that he is praying for those responsible for the ruin of the mission. He does well, to be sure, to add a prayer after fasting during the voyage: prayer is always better in conjunction with a fast. I admit that the said Father is of the opinion that it is your Fathers who are bringing about the ruin of the mission. He says that if, in 1700, the questions about the mission had not been communicated to the entire Court, and even to the Emperor himself, then neither the Emperor nor the courtiers would ever | have repeatedly inquired of His Most Excellent Lordship what was being said in Europe by Europeans about the Chinese Rites and Confucius, and so the Lord Patriach would never have been forced to speak about the Rites nor to propose the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon to the Emperor. He concludes that this was the first and most serious cause of the probable ruin of the mission. But my prayer is that, far from being destroyed, the mission might rather be strengthened, which I hope will happen when the Supreme Pontiff’s reply comes back. From my heart I wish you the best of health, etc.

So far, the letter of Fr. Carlo da Castorano, whose letter, word for word, I have given here exactly, errors and all. That it agrees with the original, I so witness. Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic[^p500-21]

### 14 A missive from Fr. Appiani

On the 22nd of this month, Fr. Appiani, who was asking for Volume Four of Bucelin, profited from the occasion to write the following note to Rev. Fr. Kilian:

I am pleased to say that today I have finished reading this volume (the third, which he was returning), and God has granted the fruit that you had in mind when you wrote recommending it. That gives me the opportunity of begging Your Reverence that, if you are not already committed to another intention, you might offer Mass for me tomorrow. It is the second anniversary of my capture, since when this unworthy servant has been excluded from the ministry of the word; so that, helped by the sacred mystery and by the blood of the Son of God, I might bear not just this inconvenience, but many others until death, with that courage and joy with which St. Clement accepted and put up with exile and condemnation to hard labour, not as a labour but as a grace: Not because of any merits of mine has God sent me to share in your martyrs’ crowns. As God has seen fit, so it has come about; I wish for nothing more than that God’s will may be fully accomplished in me. His message came not only through a certain gentile in Sichuan before I was brought here last year, but also since my arrival here I heard from a Christian who told me without my having asked or even thought about it. As I truly wish, so may my detention here profit me right up to the summit to which God has called me, and so, from the depths of my heart I pray the Great Giver of all good gifts not to lay up this sin against those who happened to connive at or order it. Not with any insincerity (for what purpose would that serve?), but in sincerity of heart I confess to Your Reverence that I count this imprisonment as among the greatest of God’s gifts to me; perhaps I was heading into some danger[^p501-22] I know not of, and in addition would have <!-- PDF page 502 | book page 493 | manuscript page 1072 --> been deprived of various insights which the Light of God and a suitable time have brought to me. May God have a care for his useful and faithful servants and turn away his anger from this people, lest those things come to pass which some days ago were related to me concerning the imperial feelings towards developments[^p502-23] in this vast mission. O that they may realise and understand that the troubles and cares which have shaken these northern ways in these months do not come by accident; they were sent so that those who have meditated a vain thing against the Lord and against his Christ might desist from disturbing the people of God. But enough. I shall be obliged if these writings of mine should remain with Your Reverence and not be passed on to anyone else. I shall weep for the ills of this mission and for the damnation of many souls, until the day of peace dawns in which those who know not shall have knowledge, since there is none other that fights for us but God our Lord who alone does wonders in heaven and on earth and grants understanding to those who gaze upon Him. I await Volume Four, and if you would be so kind and it is convenient, | some sheets of paper for me to take down whatever might be useful for me. If Your Reverence promises to keep a secret, I shall write you one word which I would not wish to get around. It is of little importance and concerns my situation.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

### 15 Fr. Kilian’s reply

November 23rd. Fr. Kilian sent the book which had been requested, together with the following reply.

Very Reverend Father,

I am sending you Volume Four. I have said the Mass you asked for, and I wish, God willing, that all your holy desires may be satisfied, that you may draw that spiritual fruit from your reading for which I sought and am still seeking for you. I do not altogether understand what you wrote—I have cast my eye over what you say rather than got to grips with it. Some of the remarks in Your Reverence’s note are unintelligible to me; I believe Your Reverence speaks sincerely, and I beg you to believe that I do so as well. ### 16 A letter from Fr. Appiani

November 23rd. The Reverend Father Appiani in confidence entrusted to Fr. Kilian some business which was weighing on his mind. In fact, it was not a secret matter at all, as will be apparent on first reading. This is what he wrote:

Were I to write less than sincerely, I would not write at all. For I know that I shall have to account for all my actions to God who scrutinises reins and hearts, and I would be truly worthy of damnation in my present position, which God has opened for me to exercise virtue, if I were to reckon it a burden rather than as a joy, possession of which I shall not renounce until I render my last breath to God, and that with a joyful heart and not under constraint. One thing does weigh upon me, which I shall explain below, and that is what I proposed to write about in confidence. I am not sure what it was in my previous note which was less clearly put; if I had been aware of it, I would have tried to be clearer. What was reported to me from the Court of the Fourteenth Son is this: the Europeans are to be sent back where they belong, and to be excluded from this kingdom. When I heard this, I was very sad for the destruction of this mission and the loss of so many souls.

I turn now to what regards me personally. I tell Your Reverence in confidence, and in the light of the affection with which you treat me. I fully—and I mean fully—realise that I am a burden upon this house and upon the missionaries who depend upon it, even though they are spread around the various provinces, because of the expense involved in keeping me in food and in other necessities at a time when things are so expensive, despite the fact that, as they say, they have received nothing from Europe for several years. So, I admit that I cannot possess my soul sufficiently in peace. Indeed, I hope that everything will be repaid to you, but it may well take some time. Now I make it my rule not to write to anybody about my condition without praying to the Lord earnestly that he may prevent me from writing down what has happened to me; that is why I shall not write to the Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord Patriarch, nor to the rest of them. I had thought that, once he knew where I was, he would have sent on what the Sacred Congregation had arranged for me to have, but I discovered that he has not done so. So, my one request of Your Reverence is that, if it is all right with you, since this is the season for writing letters, you might drop a hint to the Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord Bishop of Beijing <!-- PDF page 504 | book page 495 | manuscript page 1073 --> to write to the Sacred Congregation on my behalf—or even to the Lord Patriarch himself—for the French Jesuits to have their expenses refunded. I wish I had some opportunity to earn my bread by the work of my hands even in some remote area. But there is none. Since this is not God’s will, his will be done. I do not refuse my prison.

That from Fr. Appiani.

So much so that he once said that, once he had himself achieved the summit of his own highest hopes nothing else would please him more. Moved by such kindness, His Most Illustrious and Reverend Lordship was determined to put me in charge of one of the three provinces of Guizhou, Shaanxi, or Nanjing. He raised the matter with me more than twenty times. On each occasion I replied to His Most Illustrious and Reverend Lordship with a modest disclaimer, urging my own inadequacy and that I had to consider my vows of religion and so allowed not the slightest desire for such things to take root in me.

### 29 The first argument: the honour constantly refused

There are two pieces of evidence for the truth of this claim. First: my constant and continuous refusal to return to China in the company of His Most Illustrious and Reverend Lordship. Certainly, the first time he left I remained in Manila. Secondly, when the winds made the journey possible, and it appeared that His Most Reverend and Illustrious Lordship no longer was willing to deal with me by issuing invitations and encouragement, but resorted instead to command and authority, making the point that the business of the Holy See was more important than any activities I might undertake in Manila to help our <!-- PDF page 517 | book page 508 | manuscript page 1080 --> mission, and that he needed my help to have a peaceful entrance to China; only then with God’s favour did I do so. But who could believe that someone is panting for preferment who made every effort to distance himself from its source?

### 30 The second argument

Let this be the second argument. When we reached Guangzhou, and His Most Illustrious and Reverend Lordship was pressing me even more to agree, then with even greater diffidence, as best I could, and with due deference to such a great prelate, I spoke my mind about some of the issues concerning the mission. This upset the Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord very much, and he told me to get out of his sight and never to come back. I would not have behaved like this had I been inclined to accept dignities; I would have behaved like a yes-man, carefully considering every thought of His Most Illustrious and Reverend Lordship and praising it, or at least keeping my own thoughts to myself. I am aware that the late Most Reverend Fr. Carlo Turcotti[^p517-55] wanted me to live in the province of Guizhou, since because of his poor health he was not allowed to go there himself. I also learned from him that he had already written on this matter to Rome, since he could at that time see no other way of assisting that province which had been entrusted to him, where there was neither church nor Christian. But when the zeal of the French Province of the Society had, at great expense, relieved him of this worry, he insisted with His Most Illustrious and Reverend Lordship that I should not be sent there. At that time, however, the Most Reverend Father Tirso Gonzales, the Jesuit General, had not as yet been informed of the help given by the French Fathers, and had ordered the superiors of the mission not to refuse to assign me to Fr. Turcotti when he wanted to use my help in undertaking that expedition. However, at that point the Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord Patriarch seemed to hesitate between sending me to the province of Shaanxi or the province of Nanjing, and was perhaps more inclined to the latter. I admit that in one or two private conversations with the Most Rev. Fr. Turcotti I had indicated that, if he was not allowed to turn his back on the burdens, I would help him, preferably with the first of the two rather than with the second, lest I incur the anger of my Most Serene King (since there is no argument but that the latter falls within his patronage) and I did not want to fall foul of the whole nation of Portugal. | I have no doubt that Father Turcotti on my behalf asked the Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord to send me, if anywhere, then not to the province of Nanjing but <!-- PDF page 518 | book page 509 --> rather to Shaanxi. Anybody who appreciates the difference between them, and how little Shaanxi has in comparison with Nanjing, would certainly not brand me as ambitious!

### 31 The conclusion of Fr. Laureati’s letter to the Father Visitor

That this was what happened I swear before God, the most wise judge of us all. I testify on the word of a priest, that I have always had an aversion to honours, whether inside or outside the Society. Nonetheless, if, as goes without saying, it appears to Your Reverence that I have indeed fallen into the crime of unlawful ambition (which I do not recognize, and indeed detest as one of the worst things one could find in the Society or in a Professed Father[^p518-56]) then burn me and cut me to pieces that God may pardon me in eternity. It was to be hoped that the most kind parent of our mission[^p518-57] would nevertheless have overlooked me, since I am the only one trying to prop up the crumbling mission here in Fujian. The Churches belonging to the clerics and to the Dominican Fathers have been snatched away by the hands of pagans, along with the Most Illustrious Lord Maigrot’s episcopal palace; I have to put up with very severe persecutions and troubles both from the mandarins and from a hostile people, for the glory of God and the protection of the faith, and I hope that in future these may be even greater.

Since Your Reverence requires me to send to the Fr. General an account of this matter, I sent one the day before yesterday to Guangzhou and another yesterday to Manila.[^p518-58] I humbly ask for Your Reverence’s blessing. Fuzhou, 29 November 1708, Your Reverence’s servant and subject, Giovanni Laureati.

That this agrees with the copy of the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic
