I do not doubt that the reader wishes to have this same decree here, nor would I wish to frustrate this desire, except that the Prince had often in the past changed a draft (retaining a few of its words, which had come immediately from the lips of the Emperor), and he put off until the end of May the handing-over of it to the Libu, which finally communicated it to the provinces on June 17th, and gave us a copy only at the very end of the same month. We will provide a translation of it, with appropriate notes, in that place.

### 1 Fr. Müllener enters Beijing

On April 2nd, at the third hour of the afternoon, the Rev. Fr. Müllener arrived at our Eastern Church in Beijing, without the courtesy of any previous notification (just as he had done in all our other churches which lay on his way); nor did he wait at the door of the house while he was being announced, but went right inside, going along the corridor which led to the hall at the end, where he found Fr. Antoine Thomas. He said to him that the Lord Bishop of Beijing had given him this address and told him to go straight there. The Father sat down with the unexpected guest, and briefly explained to him the reasons why it was not expedient at this time for him to be received into our houses. He persuaded him to go to a lodging house, and invited him to have some food in the meantime, while suitable accommodation could be found. But Fr. Müllener refused the food, asking that it be explained to him more fully why he might not be admitted. The Father replied that on the following day the reasons would be given to him in writing, and asked that he not allow himself to be at all upset by this refusal on our part, which could not <!-- PDF page 163 | book page 154 | manuscript page 890 --> be avoided; and so, the Reverend Father went off to a house near the gate of the city, which is called the Hada men, to spend the night there.[^p163-2]

### 2 The reason why the Fathers were unwilling that he stay in their houses

On the following day, April 3rd, a letter was sent to him, which I append here.

Very Reverend Father, the Peace of Christ.

The present circumstances of our times and the situation we are in do not allow Your Very Reverend Lordship to be received into our houses in the way that, up to this time, we have received all missionaries coming to Beijing, and the reason is that you come in the way that you yourself explained in your letter of March 2nd written to Fr. Simon Bayard of our Society. Even if you were to be received you would not remain safely hidden, for the zhixian from Huangzhou fu has already had recourse to the principal mandarins that in Chinese fashion they might seek out Your Very Reverend Lordship throughout the 15 provinces; already, from 9th to 11th March, news has been sent to Beijing that the guards who were in charge of Your Very Reverend Lordship have been beaten in punishment at the tribunal. Fathers Noëlas and Hervieu have also experienced their problems when they were called before the court because of Your Reverend Lordship’s flight, and Father Bayard himself has been forbidden, in a friendly way, by the buzheng shi in the city of Wuchang fu to move about his mission until such time as definite news is received that Your Very Reverend Lordship has been found. It certainly would not be possible for the Emperor to receive news from us concerning the arrival of Your Very Reverend Lordship, and if he asks about this matter we will not be able to reply anything other than what we know to be true from the letters of Your Lordship, and the letters of Fathers Bayard, Hervieu, and Noëlas, and from what will soon be well known to all here in the government departments through the report of the mandarins from the province of Huguang, namely that Your Very Reverend Lordship, having been sent back by the Governor and zongdu from Guangzhou to your church in Sichuan, was treated kindly on the journey, but rashly made your escape from the hands of the guards and came here. If all this were to be said in the presence of His Majesty, as it should truthfully be said if he should ask, and would be the more appropriate thing to do since the alternative would seem to be | to make an accusation against Your Very Reverend Lordship rather than extending our protection to you, the result would be fresh anger of the Emperor <!-- PDF page 164 | book page 155 --> against the Europeans, who would be seen as bold and shameless people who are contemptuous of well-ordered authority in this realm.

### 3 Letter of Fr. Kilian to Fr. Müllener[^p164-3]

Your Very Reverend Lordship cannot be ignorant of how much trouble has been caused by your arrival in such a manner while the Very Rev. Fr. Appiani is still detained in military custody. The Emperor may consider himself treated contemptuously in the person of the mandarins by one who was for so many years the companion of the Very Rev. Fr. Appiani, both in sharing a dwelling with him and in holding the same views. Nor are you ignorant of how great a disturbance there will be throughout the churches of the 15 provinces when the new inquiry, or rather persecution, takes place. You will certainly have known in Guangzhou, and throughout your journey, how greatly disturbed the Christians were, how badly the missionaries were regarded, how many churches have suffered from violence and pillaging at the hands of the gentiles, not because of the flight of the Rev. Fr. Cróquer but because of a casual and harmless interruption of his journey.[^p164-4] Hence, if you first give satisfaction to the mandarins, and so avert the dangers you have caused to both missionaries and Christians, then our house will readily be open to Your Very Reverend person, and we will be happy to offer you all that charity requires. However, Very Reverend Sir, while still enjoying the complete freedom of your person, you need to consider what is most expedient for yourself, for the Rev. Fr. Appiani, and for the Christian enterprise, since it is not permissible for any of us Jesuits to give advice inclining the mind of Your Reverence either to the right or to the left. May you receive this letter as a token of the sincere love of the Fathers for you, and not be bothered to reply either to me or to any other, since it is to the advantage of yourself and of ourselves that we abstain from the exchange of letters, and, as soon as possible, have concern for the welfare of those whose temporal peace and spiritual advantage are unjustly damaged and, as far as one can foresee, will certainly be damaged even more severely in the future.

Beijing, 3rd April, 1708. Your Very Reverend Lordship’s servant in Christ,

Kilian Stumpf, sj, Procurator of the Vice-Province.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary

### 4 Fr. Müllener’s servant comes to the Fathers: what conversation they had with him

On the morning of Wednesday April 4th, the doorkeeper came to my room bringing a packet from Fr. Müllener containing two letters with an open envelope inscribed, “To Father Kilian the Procurator.” I replied that Fr. Kilian was not at home. I ordered the door-keeper not to receive the letters, but to bring to me the one bearing the letters who said he was a Christian. I suspected that he was, in fact, an attendant of Fr. Müllener. When therefore this servant had been brought and he entered my room, I spoke to him in this way: “Fr. Kilian is not at home, and even if he had been, how could he receive letters from your master? Is it permissible to escape in this way from the hands of those conducting someone by order of the mandarins? Already the Fathers and the Christians and the servants of the churches where you came from are suffering on account of you people. Some have been flogged by the mandarins, some have been summoned to the courts because of your fleeing here. How would we dare to speak about you in the Emperor’s presence? What would the Emperor say when he heard that you did not obey instructions of the mandarins which are not contrary to our holy religion? What would the Prince say, a man dreaded by all? Truly you have much to fear. Indeed, your master does not know the Chinese customs; he is to some extent deserving of pardon, everything will be imputed to his servants. Have you not heard what the Christians suffered last year, when the mandarins wrote to the provinces (although untruly) that some Europeans had fled named Ai (Fr. Cróquer),[^p165-7] Ba Lumao (Fr. Carvalho),[^p165-8] Meng Tini (Fr. Montigny),[^p165-9] etc.? And now in Huguang Fr. Bayard writes that he, Fr. Noëlas and Fr. Hervieu are suffering. Have you not heard?” He replied: “We have heard.” I continued: “Do you understand then that you and <!-- PDF page 166 | book page 157 | manuscript page 891 --> your companion, since you are Chinese, are seriously exposed to punishments by the mandarins, who will be more inclined to excuse your master, saying that he was deceived by you?” The servant replied | that his master wished to go back to Huguang; that he was ignorant of Chinese customs, since he had come to this kingdom not so many years ago;[^p166-10] and that he was now, in fact, returning to the province of Sichuan, not because of any transgression on his part, but only because this was demanded by the mandarins. Saying this he gave me to read a copy of the wenshu, which was given by the Governor and zongdu of Guangdong, and had to be shown from town to town as they changed escorts so that Fr. Müllener, by the direction of the magistrates, might arrive in Sichuan. Nothing was contained in it except the demand of the Prefects of Sichuan that, since this European had left their territory without their having been informed in advance, he should be sent back by the Guangzhou authorities in accordance with the usual regulations, and that the Governor and zongdu of Guangdong wished this to be carried out in accordance with their demands. I then said to the servant: “Well then, your master is not accused of any crime, and he is not being commanded to do anything that is against our holy religion, so why do you make him take to flight? Have you heard that three Chinese, and among them Peter Wang, have been severely punished and sentenced to exile? For (as the Emperor said) they, as Chinese, must obey the laws of China, and teach Europeans who order anything different what the laws of China are.” He replied that he knew this; that his master wished to return as soon as possible to Huguang.

### 5 Fr. Müllener’s servant is warned of the danger threatening himself and his employer

“Well,” I said, “do you fully understand that we would be glad to welcome a friend coming from far away? It is very hard for us to send him away like this, but what are we to do? Here we are near to the Emperor; at the wall of our house are the guards of Fr. Appiani and the spies of the Prince. Yesterday the Prince himself came here in person, and he is accustomed to do so quite often. What would have happened if he had found your master with us? It was your good fortune and ours that he did not come to our house.” The servant asked: “Does the Prince not yet know of our arrival?” I replied: “He does not know. If he had known, I am not sure that your master, and especially you yourself, would still remain at peace.” He replied: “What I have done, I have done in obedience to my spiritual father, but we will go away at once.” I said: “Consider among yourselves, while you are free, what confusion there would be for us <!-- PDF page 167 | book page 158 | manuscript page 892 --> if once again some old European were taken captive, as Fr. Appiani was!” He said: “Can Fr. Appiani be seen?” I replied: “If you are not afraid of being flogged, brought before Prince, and sent into exile—or if you wish your master to be taken captive, and Fr. Appiani to be questioned once again, since his case is not completed—then yes, as I have said, you can go to him.” “I am not,” he said, “thinking of doing this but of our return; when we have returned, what shall we say in Wuchang fu?” I replied: “Whatever seems to you to be good to say.” “But what does Your Paternity think we should say?” I replied: “Your master could say that he undertook this journey to ask for an imperial permit; but on the way he heard that the mandarins were looking for him, and so he returned in order to comply with their demands and prevent troubles coming upon the innocent.” “I beg you,” said the servant, “to write to the mandarins.” I replied: “There in Wuchang fu is the buzheng shi who is friendly to the Europeans; the zongdu also knows the Europeans. We will write to the Europeans who live there asking them to help, as far as they can.” “Good,” said the attendant, “we will leave at once.” “The sooner the better,” I said. “We cannot guarantee that the affair will not become known. We have many Chinese domestic servants, and they speak where and when they wish. That is why I called you to speak to me alone. Now when you leave me, do not tell our servants what you have heard from me. If the presence of your master becomes known, the result can scarcely be avoided that he will suffer, and you above all because you are Chinese.” He asked: “What will I say about these letters?” I replied: “It will be enough to tell your master that Fr. Kilian is not at home, but the Father who is there warned you that this business is very bad; there are consequences to be feared which threaten your master in particular, because he was an associate of Fr. Appiani. And you, and your fellow servant, since you are Chinese, are liable to receive still greater punishment. But now time is in your favour, while you are still free. Fr. Ba Lumao (Carvalho) with Fr. Mezzafalce, came as far as Zhangjia wan, six leagues outside Beijing; he accepted the good advice we gave him, and went back unharmed. Fr. Mezzafalce was confined almost as a prisoner to a house belonging to a certain courtier. Again I say to you, do not tell our servants what you have heard from me. If any of this should come to the ears of the Prince, there will be no excuse for the misfortunes that will follow, and if some royal command is proclaimed, then there is no remedy.” Thus instructed the attendant went away, carrying back with him the replies from his master which he had brought.

On the same day, the 4th, the same attendant brought letters and some silver coins to the Eastern Church for Dr. Giovanni Borghese, who was staying there; | the letters were said to be from the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing, and both the letters and the coins were duly handed over to Dr. Borghese.

### 6 Fr. Müllener departs from Beijing

On April 5th a servant was sent by us before the sixth hour of the morning to ask whether we could be of service to the Fr. Müllener in some other way. But he had already departed, without leaving any indication of the road he had taken; nor were we able to hear a whisper about the mystery of his destination until Father Le Couteulx wrote from Huguang that he had completed his return journey there at the end of April, as will be narrated under June 14th.[^p168-11]

I readily appreciate that, in the matter of the story of the Rev. Fr. Müllener, the reader may wish to have explained to him the reason why this Reverend Father exposed himself and others to such great troubles, almost to the point of violence insisting on attempting to obtain a permit. Already he had come from Sichuan to Guangzhou, the journey taking three months; hence it seemed that he wished to leave China, and not to want to get a residence permit. In Guangzhou he was asked to return the way he had come and he would have no problem being accepted in Sichuan, and so for the time being a permit was not, in fact, necessary for him; why then, without regard for the expense and scorning the dangers, did he hasten to the Court, disregarding the orders of the magistrates? I would explain the intention of the man if I knew the true reason for his action but, since I do not know it, I ask the reader to consider himself satisfied by my appending two letters of the Illustrious Bishop of Beijing, in which he relates how Fr. Müllener explained this obscure business in his presence. If one were to consider the exact sequence of events, these letters should be deferred to a little later on, but I will nevertheless set them out here by way of interpolation, and then we can return to the proper chronology. This is how the Most Illustrious Lord writes to Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial on April 9th.[^p168-12]

### 7 Letter of the Bishop of Beijing about Fr. Müllener

On Palm Sunday I received the most welcome letter of Your Most Reverend Paternity written on March 22nd with the two letters enclosed of Fr. Bayard and Fr. Müllener. I don’t know what you want me to do about this matter. The said Señor Müllener arrived quite unexpectedly in this Church on March 21st. He told me that he was travelling to Sichuan, and that he seized the opportunity to give the slip to the chairen and to set off in the direction of Beijing with the intention of spending several days there. Having examined his intentions I learned <!-- PDF page 169 | book page 160 | manuscript page 893 --> that he did not want to leave China freely without being forced to do so and that this was at the request of the Lord Patriarch, whom he had consulted. And not being able to stay either in Guangzhou nor in Sichuan without a piao he had resolved to present himself to His Majesty to see whether he could request one or would have to leave China. In the circumstances, I was unable to give him any advice. Afraid to stay here, he left for Beijing on March 26th. I could do nothing other than recommend him to God, fearing that his coming could do me no good without special help from the Most High. I greatly value the efforts of the Fathers of Huguang and Your Paternities, yet I fear for myself that I will be sent away in the same way as Fr. Antonio Frosinone. May God be praised eternally in everything and may Your Very Reverend Paternity be preserved for many years.

That this conforms to the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary

The letter of Your Very Reverend Paternity, dated March 31st, came into my hands yesterday. I understood from it the purpose of the journey to Beijing undertaken by the Rev. Fr. Müllener, when he secretly abandoned his journey to Sichuan and left his guards near Wuchang fu, thus causing hardship to the missionaries and no small disturbance to the Christian mission. The said Fr. Müllener came to me on March 21st, and on the 26th of the same month left here to go to the Court. He did not stay any longer, nor did I dare to detain him further, firstly because he was afraid that the guards would pursue him, and secondly because, when I had ascertained his intentions, I found that he was determined to present himself before the Emperor to try to obtain a royal permit, or at least to establish definitely that he was being banished from the kingdom. He himself did not wish to depart from it voluntarily, just as he had not entered it of his own accord. This was also, he said, | the view of His Excellency, the Lord Patriarch, whose mind he had ascertained through letters. Furthermore, nowhere was he permitted to remain in safety without a royal permit, nor did he expect to be allowed to stay in Sichuan. He had left there on account of the very great vexations he suffered at the hands of the mandarins. And so, he took <!-- PDF page 170 | book page 161 --> the opportunity which was offered to him near Wuchang fu, by a considerable delay on the part of the guards accompanying him, to take the direct route to the Court, seeking to be spared the expense and trouble of a very long and useless journey and to avoid further troubles among the Christians of Sichuan. There was nothing I could say in response to all this; although I do consider to be acting badly those who focus on their own banishment when there is reason to fear the exasperation of the Emperor, the distress of other missionaries, and no small trouble for the Christian cause. Hence all that remains for me to do is to ask God to sustain the Chinese mission by the strength of his right hand, and to prepare me for more troubles.

### 8 Some observations on the letters of the Bishop of Beijing

If at this point the reader puts aside Fr. Müllener and asks why the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing, perceiving the result of bad advice turning and spreading in his direction, did not at least try to dissuade him knowing how many evils were experienced by missionaries, Christians, and the Church through a case less serious than this and the Lord Bishop himself had experienced the unrest amongst the Christians over the expulsion of Fr. Frossoloni and was not yet free from fear, and even after a whole year still further inconveniences were arising on account of the same case. If, I say, the reader should ask this, I can make no response, since nowhere in his letters do I find an explanation for such silence and useless modesty except that given in his reply to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon when questioned why he should swear that one should believe the Lord Patriarch’s statement that the Lord Patriarch had in himself the oracular powers of the Supreme Pontiff because he had a special faculty to interpret pontifical decrees etc. which he swore covered the present case, he replied that he did it to avoid incurring harassment from the Patriarch’s people. When I consider that response it seems to me that the Illustrious Lord Bishop out of respect and fear did not dare to try to dissuade Fr. Müllener from it, but rather wanted to approve whatever the patriarchal party were advising and what His Excellency the Lord Patriarch had approved when consulted by letter. The Illustrious Lord Bishop himself will reveal the true reason when it pleases him. Perhaps yet another note about this will be given under July when Fr. Müllener more openly than in these two letters from the Bishop written from Linqing reveal the real nature of this journey of his.

Good reader, to satisfy you in this matter of Fr. Müllener I went to great lengths to tease out what you were seeking about Fr. Müllener, and I have not <!-- PDF page 171 | book page 162 | manuscript page 894 --> succeeded. Now, once again, I ask only one thing of you, to try to find rhyme and reason in what follows and to make your own judgment, if you are man enough.

### 9 The series of things done by Fr. Müllener in his various journeys and encounters

1. From the original letters of Fr. Basset we find that at the beginning of 1707 he together with his fellow clerics, on the first news of the necessity of receiving a residence permit, replied to Frs. J.F. Gerbillon and Antoine Thomas that there was no way they could reply to the Emperor by promising what he asks of them but neither did they dare offend the Emperor by making a protestation as the Patriarch ordered, so they were resolved to leave the mission and as soon as possible to make their way to Guangzhou.

2. The facts themselves confirm this since afterwards, when these reverend gentlemen were finally prohibited by the mandarins from leaving, they actually left at some time around the month of August 1707, taking the direct route to Guangzhou and carrying with them not only everything they possessed but also Chinese boys to be taken with them to Europe.

3. It is public knowledge in Guangzhou and Beijing, according to letters written by those residents in Guangzhou, that the aforesaid gentlemen made a deal with the English Captain Harrison for berths on his ship about to return to Europe for themselves and the Chinese boys, but it was prevented by the death of Fr. Basset, the deadly illness of Fr. Belluar,[^p171-14] and the detention of Fr. Müllener by the mandarins.

4. It is established that when Fr. Müllener was told that, at the instance of the mandarins from Sichuan, he was to be sent back there by the Governor of Guangzhou, he flew into a rage, and, against the full authority of the Chinese, he wished to depart, or experience being chained, however violently, rather than return to his church; so that not only did all the reverend clerics strive to pacify him in order to avoid greater evils but they requested other missionaries also to urge him to return, persuading and entreating him. |

5. It is agreed that, from the gates of Rome to Chongqing fu, in the province of Sichuan, Fr. Müllener was the companion of the Fr. Appiani living together with him, sharing opinions and preaching; this association of the two men could have been known to the Emperor, and ought to have been, after so many investigations by the mandarins of Sichuan into the life and actions of the Fr. Appiani, which have all been reported to His Majesty.

<!-- PDF page 172 | book page 163 -->

6. Therefore it is clear that this Reverend Father was quite determined to depart from the Chinese mission; he was quite unwilling to remain; he was, beyond all the others, the least likely to receive a permit from the Emperor; rather, he was the most likely to provoke the anger of His Majesty against himself and his colleagues.

And on top of all this, Fr. Müllener, coming with the knowledge and consent of the Lord Patriarch, had the temerity to deceive the mandarins on the way and attempt to present himself to the Emperor. His purpose was to compel the Jesuits in Beijing to help him as he tried to break through the barriers preventing him from receiving a permit, or else accept a refusal with a stout heart, and be banished from China. What do you think of this, dear Reader? You make your judgement. I do not judge the prudence, uprightness and good faith of these gentlemen, but with my companions in Beijing I earnestly pray to God to assist Fr. Müllener, and all of us, with a very special grace, when, with the permission of the mandarins, he comes to Beijing from the province of Sichuan at about the end of October, or at whatever other time it may be, to carry out the plan which he began as a result of letters from the Lord Patriarch. These letters he brought with him from Guangzhou, and for many months he has carried them about China with him.

From the letter of the Rev. Fr. ### 12 The mandarins are trying to expel all the Europeans

April 11th. By speedy courier, complaints came quickly from Sichuan, Songjiang, Shanghai, etc. Of these, some arrived in March, as well as more recent ones dated April 2nd, 4th, and 6th; they came from various places, and indicated that some mandarins had reached such a level of audacity that they treated with contempt the residence permit of the Emperor, which they said was false; they even placed their authority over that of the Emperor himself in order to expel the Europeans. Thus, the zongdu of the provinces of Zhejiang and Fujian published his edict, in which he said that, by command of the Emperor, all the Europeans (except one, Guo Duoluo, Fr. Muñoz[^p173-17]) are excluded from the Empire. This was sent throughout all the provinces, so that even the mandarin in Shanghai for example, although of a much inferior rank, produced a document commanding the neighbours to surround the church with a guard by day and by night, speaking boastfully and using the words feng zhi[^p173-18] as if he were prescribing this as someone having a direct mandate from the Emperor. On account <!-- PDF page 174 | book page 165 | manuscript page 895 --> of this document the churches of Frs. Mendes,[^p174-19] Jacquemin,[^p174-20] de Sá, Magalhães, etc. were put at risk.

So | on April 12th, at a general consultation of the Fathers in Beijing, it was decided to beg Divine Providence, with extraordinary works of piety, to speak a word of command to these great winds and storms, breathing gently over them, so that the humble petition which we would present to the Emperor might have a happy outcome.

The petition was composed by Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial José Soares, to be offered in the name of all the Fathers in Beijing. A translation of the petition follows:

### 13 The petition to be offered to the Emperor in the name of the Beijing Fathers

We, Filippo Grimaldi, and the other Europeans, with all submission, reverence and truth, bring to the notice of Your Majesty matters that lie deep in their hearts and are a source of sorrow to them, entreating that Your Majesty may have pity on them. We are scholars who have also received the great benefit of permission freely to preach the Christian Law; for this purpose, Your Majesty has granted to all the other Europeans dispersed throughout the provinces that they may come to your Court, into your presence, and to these you have then given your imperial permit which enables them to live wherever they wish, in peace of mind and without molestation. Having considered these things, we have found that, from ancient times until now, there has not been an Emperor who has treated men from outside the country with such great consideration and provident care; hence we and the other Europeans, having received such great benefits, were convinced that we would have nothing to fear in the future. However, this benefit of Your Majesty was just made available to us when provincial officials, in Shandong, Jiangnan, Huguang, and elsewhere, ignoring Your Majesty’s beneficence, have begun to set up official inquiries without authorization concerning this permit, querying whether it is authentic or not. They have often had recourse to the Ministry of Rites concerning the truth of this matter, and the response always was that the Ministry knew nothing of such a permit of Your Majesty. Having received this response, the Governors more and more doubt the genuineness of the permit. Further, the Supreme Prefect of the Provinces of Zhejiang and Fujian, named Liang Nai, has put out an edict which has been communicated to all the governors of the Empire. In this edict it is officially announced that all the Europeans are to be <!-- PDF page 175 | book page 166 --> exiled from the Empire, except Guo Duoluo, who is permitted to remain in one church in Guangzhou. No word is said in it about those who are allowed to remain in China because they are protected by Your Majesty’s imperial permit.

### 14 They ask that the mandarins of the provinces, who have doubts about the authenticity of the permit given to the missionaries by the Emperor, be informed concerning it

The other provincial officials, seeing such an edict, are becoming more and more confirmed in their doubts, and do not cease daily to set up more and more investigations. Hence, we consulted the laws and customs of the Empire, and have discovered that provincial officials do not acknowledge and accept any certificates unless they are accompanied by a wen or authentic rescript of some ministry of the capital, which guarantees the genuineness of the certificate. If it does not become known throughout all the provinces that the Europeans are considered worthy to be admitted to the presence of Your Majesty and to be granted your imperial permit, it will be impossible according to the procedures of the Empire to establish authoritatively that the permit has been really and truly issued by Your Majesty. Having read in letters, sent from every quarter, of the molestations which the Europeans innocently suffer everywhere throughout the provinces on account of the official inquiries of the mandarins concerning the permits, we now find this very difficult to endure, and hence we cannot refrain from having recourse to Your Majesty, to let you know the position and to entreat you to supply a remedy. Such a remedy we think would be this: that Your Majesty deign to grant that, in all the provinces of the Empire, it be publicly proclaimed by the Libu (that is the Ministry of Rites) that the said permit has indeed been issued by Your Majesty, and hence that the provincial officials are to treat well the Europeans who are to receive the permit, and those who have already received it; and, that there is to be no more doubt about the authenticity of the permit. Thus, not only we ourselves but also all the other Europeans will continue to rejoice in Your Majesty’s great favour and patronage. Therefore we, as vassals from afar, have drawn up, with the greatest reverence, this humble petition, entreating the Great Prince to be a mediator for us all by presenting it to Your Majesty; we hope that an imperial decree concerning this present business may be made public everywhere.

Prayers having been offered to God, as has been said, the text of the petition as given above was read out to the Fathers, who were familiar with the situation and knew the language. They cast their votes as to whether anything should be changed, or whether it seemed best that it be submitted as it was. All readily agreed concerning the substance of the petition; as to submitting <!-- PDF page 176 | book page 167 | manuscript page 896 --> it, some were fearful, having been taught by experience how closely particular writings by Jesuits | are scrutinised by adversaries seeking to extract an evil sense out of innocent words with the result that the Jesuits are at once subjected to calumny, as though this is already proven. For this reason they preferred that the matter be proposed by word of mouth.

### 15 The Fathers decide that the petition should be offered in writing, not by word of mouth

This advice was seen to have the advantage that our good name would be better preserved and our innocent petition would be less open to hostile interpretation; but it was considered, with good reason, that an oral petition would be less likely to succeed. On March 6th, requests made by word of mouth to the Prince vanished with the wind, and it was presumed that other such requests would vanish in the same way; meanwhile the evils would grow and worse evils would arise in the near future which would be beyond the reach of all remedies. So it was agreed by common consent that, unless a more suitable opportunity should occur, a written petition would, after a few days, be presented to the Prince and, through him, to the Emperor. Then, when the will of Father Visitor was made known by letter, this was done on the 26th of April of the current year.[^p176-21]

I made my third attempt and after my high hopes I had to accept further dishonour seeing that Mr. Bian, when I presented my tiezi or visiting card, would <!-- PDF page 179 | book page 170 --> not accept it but to further insult me sent to the Church the buyi of the siya[^p179-33] to throw me out as if I were a thief or something of the sort. The Christians, since it was already night, sent them away without telling me. They returned once or twice the following morning, which was Sunday, while I was hearing confessions and preparing to say Mass, and with shouts and insults threatened me to try to make me leave. They had no written order from the mandarin to do this nor even purely verbal instructions and this was the greatest insult or act of contempt on the part of the zhizhou Mr. Bian. I took out the longpiao[^p179-34] that I had received, and since there was no opening to presenting it to him personally, I sent him in writing my personal details and information about the said longpiao. He would not accept even this and, furthermore, following the report of his minions he was fully informed of everything that happened since he was obliged to find out the truth, yet still persisted in his determination to attack me and throw me out by means of the said buyi. At that very moment along came his predecessor as zhizhou, Mr. Tong,[^p179-35] to pay his respects by visiting me. When he came in to me, I presented the case to him. I showed him the imperial piao and asked his support and help. He excused himself since he had lost his mandarinate and this Mr. Bian was not in a happy frame of mind (his son was heavily in debt, was running wild and had been thrown out, had respect for nobody and owed a lot of money to a grand mandarin of the Libu and his uncle, the Governor of Jiangxi). He could do nothing of what I was asking but he promised to do the little that he could. In fact when he had left the house he immediately called on the Siya, told him about my longpiao, pointed out that it was not a good idea to send more chairen to bother me and recommended that he should pass all this on to the zhizhou, Mr. Bian. The minor mandarin when he learned about what had happened to me and the harsh words of the said zhizhou, his superior, left hastily making his excuses. Mr. Tong when he saw this sent me a message to tell me what he had done and advised me that there was nothing for it but to leave here of my own accord within two or three days before I experienced worse harassment. This advice seems to me harsh. Firstly, because it is ridiculous and inconvenient that a longpiao should be subject to the mockery and disrespect of the most vile individuals of the buyi without the pingchu[^p179-36] of the mandarins and before an examination of the authenticity of the claim. Secondly, for me to leave without such an investigation will confirm <!-- PDF page 180 | book page 171 | manuscript page 898 --> for the gentiles the false rumours that have been spread about me, that I am an imposter and that the said longpiao is imaginary, | a matter very prejudicial to the Holy Law. Thirdly, such circumstance of my leaving would be interpreted as flight and prejudicial to the souls of these tender neophytes, hence I decided to wait a few days and meanwhile to continue to attend the men’s and women’s confraternities.

On April 24th, without being hindered by it being a rainy day, the zhairen of the buyi returned four times with more abuse and acts of disrespect to persuade me to leave at once, threatening that otherwise they would flog me and drag me away from there. In fact, they grabbed hold of one of my servants nicknamed Jiang, Chinese name Xi Fan, to take him as a hostage to the tribunal of the zhizhou. ### 24 What function was allotted to Father Osório by the Emperor and who he was

1. Therefore let the reader be reassured that the Beijing Jesuits did not know and do not yet know of such an order as is being alleged to have been sent from the Court to Guangzhou. We have publicly testified to this by letter both to those concerned at Macau and at Guangzhou, and not only once, and we have cautioned Father Osório that as best he may, he is to decline such a task, which before this the Emperor wished to impose on Fr. Ferdinand Verbiest and later, in more recent times, on Fr. Filippo Grimaldi, and which has been declined by the Jesuits.[^p186-48]

2. By some, as for example by Fr. Buonaventura and others, it is being written that such references are being required by order of the Emperor. Others are saying it is by order of the Great Prince. The Beijing Jesuits cannot make a statement about either of them, because they had no previous knowledge of either, and it is not appropriate for them to make any enquiries about this at the court.

3. It is indeed certain that the Emperor, when he was sending the Manchus, Foioró and Pagaró, to deliver the Lord Patriarch to be kept in custody by the authorities in Macau, had ordered them to say to Father Osório that His Majesty remembered him as a man who was a jobkon, that is, as a man in good standing, of proven character and talents, and that he wished that if any new arrivals came that year, he, on account of his long-standing fidelity in his service, should investigate which of them were suitable to serve him and should notify them to be prepared to travel, as well as the mandarins and the Court itself. And the Father actually did this in regard to Frs. Gaspar Castner and Leopold Liebstain and Br. Franz Stadlin, and immediately after being chosen they were escorted to the Court. He did the same also in the case of Frs. Luigi Gonzaga and Roman Hinderer and Br. Miguel Vieira, who were then summoned. As a result of this it may be conjectured that perhaps the Prince wrote to the mandarins <!-- PDF page 187 | book page 178 --> that henceforth, to avoid complications with the Emperor, the Europeans who were to be sent to the Court should first present themselves to Fr. Osório, since the Emperor had faith in him and he had proved he was trustworthy both in the information and the personnel he had sent. But all this did not touch on missionaries, but rather only those who were destined for the service of the Emperor. No one at all knows what the real truth of the matter is.

4. Father Osório, a Portuguese national and dear to the Emperor on account of his excellent character, had lived previously in Beijing with the Most Illustrious Father Ciceri, later the Bishop of Nanjing, with whom he had come to the Court. There, by the express command of the Emperor, he learned the Manchu language so that he could remain there and be of assistance, and personally report in both languages what the Emperor wanted. But his health could not for long stand the air and water (as the Chinese say) of Beijing, and he would have departed fairly quickly if the affection for his person which he aroused in His Majesty had allowed him to leave earlier. After spending about seven years at the court, he returned to Macau, where for many years on end he resided in the infirmary and gave an example of religious patience, remaining aloof from involvement in all matters of business.

### 25 The Chinese name of Father Osório

5. The Chinese name of this Father was Guozheng with the surname Li.[^p187-49] The three words Li Guo Zheng are always written in Manchu and in Latin with the same letters without changing the meaning, but when written in Chinese they can be represented by thirty or more different characters which indicate different names and have a different meaning, as is obvious to those who know Chinese. Whence, unless the characters proper to such and such a person are used, even though the name is pronounced in the same way, nevertheless it may not designate that same person, just as, for example, Thamaris and Damaris are the names of different persons. Moreover, the document which was said by the mandarins to have come from the court was written in Manchu, and the secretary of the Governor of Guangzhou provided in Chinese characters the name of that European whose testimony and guarantee he wanted; in fact, he used characters which were not properly those of Fr. Osório. Hence questions were asked in all the churches at Guangzhou as to who this Li Guozheng might be, and they all said that they did not know any such European, although everyone’s guess was directed towards Father Osório. ### 26 Letter from the Franciscans to Fr. Manuel Osório

My dear Father Manuel Osório,

Since the 22nd of February of this year 1708, when the Religious of St. Francis, missionaries in China, who are signatories below, presented a petition in the office of the Governor of Guangzhou[^p188-50] requesting permission to go to Beijing to present ourselves to seek from the Emperor a permit to reside in China, so as not to be exiled from the kingdom for lack of it according to the order that the same Emperor has imposed, our petition has been forwarded to get the necessary clearance from the governor to the ancha shi.[^p188-51] And this mandarin handed it over to his subordinates, and when it was on the point of being approved he received advice from the mandarins who said that to grant this permission it required the approval of our people by a Xiyang ren[^p188-52] named Li Guozheng.[^p188-53] Without such approval, permission would not be given, and this was the order which had come from Beijing, sent by the Eldest Son of the Emperor, the da wangye, the judge appointed by the Emperor to examine the European missionaries who were preaching in the kingdom.
