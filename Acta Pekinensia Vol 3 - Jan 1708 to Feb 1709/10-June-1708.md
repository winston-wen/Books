### 1 Three of the Beijing Jesuits are sent to Tartary by the Emperor

Under May 21st[^p208-1] it was said that the Emperor had decided that a geographical map of Tartary,[^p208-2] which was extensively under his control, should be made by Europeans. For several days, there were different opinions as to who were to be chosen for this task, but finally Fathers Joachim Bouvet, Jean Régis, and Pierre Jartoux, all of French nationality, were designated by His Majesty. And from the beginning of the month, they were ordered to find and procure the necessary instruments, and were graciously lodged in the Royal Villa.

After being admitted into the presence of His Majesty they were questioned on practical geometry etc. and their answers were noted.[^p208-3] Fr. Bouvet judged that he should grasp the opportunity to present his study on the very ancient combinatory art of the Chinese, which he had worked out with great and unremitting zeal over many years. His purpose was both that he himself might penetrate to the depths of that abstruse canonical book, the Yijing, and also to restore the rightful employment of that praiseworthy combinatory art and undo the abuse of the moderns by which the sect of the Daoshi[^p208-4] and countless others are distorting that combinatory chart which was devised by Fu Xi, the original founder of the Chinese nation, and elucidated with commentaries by the king Wen Wang, his son Zhou Gong, and Confucius, and are turning it to fortune-telling and other illicit uses.[^p208-5] Wherefore when the Emperor asked <!-- PDF page 209 | book page 200 --> him why his face was lacking its natural colour and pinched, he answered that in fact he was in good health, but that the pallor was due to the books which he was reading through with unrelenting industry. He said that as a foreigner, greater effort had to be made by him when reading Chinese books and seeking to penetrate the books of the ancients, especially the book called by the Chinese the Yijing or “Book of Permutations.”[^p209-6]

### 2 Father Joachim Bouvet presents the Emperor with a book that he himself has written

In doing this, he said, he thought that he has done something worthwhile and that with his own hand he had written something about the right understanding of it and its correct use. Shortly after this, he brought out from his gown the study he had produced with so much labour, and with due deference, he put it into the hands of His Majesty. The Emperor accepted it graciously and unfolding it briefly said that a longer and more careful examination would be required. At the same time, he gave orders for it to be kept and investigated in its own good time.[^p210-7]

June 4th. The three Fathers departed with three or four mandarins and several others selected from the members of different government departments. Their itinerary was arranged in this way: that from the gate of the Palace they were to travel in a straight line to the eastern sea coast, and from there should go up to the beginning of the Great Wall and, following its course, travel to that place where after some days the Emperor was to set up a permanent camp in Karahodon, a place in Manchuria not far removed from that wall, the miracle of walls.

### 3 Fr. Appiani asks to be transferred from his prison into our house

Fr. Appiani had heard of the sending of the three Jesuits on this enterprise, and of the journey of the others who were to accompany the Emperor; therefore, in a note which he presented, he wished to express his congratulations, and hinted at his great eagerness to be transferred into our house.[^p210-8] But it was not within the powers of the priest who desired this or of the Jesuits to arrange it, although they were making every effort to do so. However, when he found out that they had departed, he let it be known that he was suffering stomach trouble and loss of appetite, and that now he was thinking that arrangements should be made for him so that in the event of death he should not be deprived of the sacraments. When he was asked whether he wanted any medication from our pharmacist, Brother de Rhodes, he refused medicines and asked for foods but of the kind which should be avoided, according to all the rules of medicine, by those who were experiencing stomach trouble. Nevertheless, they were sent to <!-- PDF page 211 | book page 202 | manuscript page 914 --> him with two warnings, one that he himself should be advised that he should not do anything to aggravate his illness, and the other that the salty and sharp foods which he was requesting should be served in such a way that the quantity at least should not be harmful. On the following days Father Appiani explained himself more clearly through one of Father Kilian’s students, Ignatius Cui.[^p211-9] He was openly demanding that the Jesuits should negotiate with the mandarins so that, since he was ill, he might be permitted to move from his prison to our residence. The student was saying that this must be done with all haste because the Prince after a few days was to set out for Manchuria with His Majesty. The Father replied briefly that since Father Appiani was indisposed, he should arrange for this to be communicated by the guards to the mandarins who were in charge and to whose care he had been entrusted. He said that they would immediately report it to the Prince and that the Jesuits would stand close by so that they could corroborate what the mandarins were saying, | and make a petition on behalf of the said priest. However, he said that the first notification of the illness, if made by the Jesuits, would certainly be disregarded by the Prince, and he would give orders that, following the ordinary procedure, we should permit the mandarins to carry out their duties in communicating the matters which it was their duty to communicate. The student happened to mention that one of the mandarins was present, and the Jesuit immediately stood up to go to the mandarin to inform him of Fr. Appiani’s indisposition. But the young man objected and asked the Jesuit not to go, saying that he would arrange for Fr. Appiani to send one of the guards. Fr. Kilian agreed to this, assuming that the young man was acting on the instructions of the priest.

Two days later, on a second occasion, through the same person, Father Appiani insisted that he could no longer manage without medical assistance, but that he did not want Chinese doctors. The Jesuits should find out in what way they could help so that he was not left without loving care and the help which the Church provides. He was given the answer that we are ready to help, but that it could not be provided except in the normal way, which was that the mandarins were to inform the Prince, and the Prince would send a doctor, and that after hearing the opinion of the doctor he would listen to our requests. Therefore Fr. Appiani was requested not to make any difficulty in admitting a Chinese doctor appointed by the mandarins or the Prince. For this could not be avoided, although he was not obliged to use the medicines provided; but he was not to refuse a request to feel his pulse and to make the other examinations <!-- PDF page 212 | book page 203 --> customary with Chinese doctors. But it pleased Divine Providence to dispose of things in such a way that Fr. Appiani was both able and willing to manage without this assistance.

### 4 Father Müllener returns to Wuchang . What he did there

June 14th. A letter arrived from Father Le Couteulx[^p212-10] from Wuchang fu saying that towards the end of April fu Rev. Fr. Müllener returned there to surrender himself to the mandarins, but wished to visit the buzheng shi to entrust his case to him. Fr. Simon Bayard was absent, working at the De’an mission, but Father Le Couteulx was present. He persuaded the reverend gentleman to postpone everything else and to finish off his business with the local mandarin. This is the one whose responsibility it was in the first place to receive him; if he waited, he would be delivered to him by the guards. Fr. Müllener approved of this and, after presenting his visiting card, requested an interview. But the mandarin rejected both the card that was presented and the person who presented it, saying that the man had not yet been delivered into his jurisdiction and it was not his responsibility either to correct what had been done before, or to follow the matter up any further unless normal procedures were followed. Therefore, he should return to that governor whose guards he had eluded. If he should order him to be taken to Wuchang fu he would receive him and send him on his way to Sichuan.

Fr. Müllener did not refuse to return to the place where he had begun to deviate and when he had paid his respects to the mandarin in writing, he explained that through ignorance of Chinese customs, after leaving the guards he had wanted to go on to Beijing by a shorter way, thinking that this could be done without prejudice to anyone at all. Now he realized his mistake and wished to restore himself to his jurisdiction, lest the mandarin or anyone else should suffer any inconvenience. He was treated with indulgence. The mandarin himself set out to put the whole matter right by means of a convenient cover-up and a bit of Chinese artfulness. First, he forged a petition supposedly presented to him two months earlier by Fr. Müllener when he had been brought there by the guards. In this the said priest had requested permission to interrupt his journey and to remain there until, after being cured from some minor indisposition, he should be fit to continue his journey. The zhixian[^p212-11] annotated this petition so as to make it appear to have been presented at that time; and soon <!-- PDF page 213 | book page 204 | manuscript page 915 --> produced another document in which he said that this European while coming from Guangzhou had previously requested and obtained a delay for the cure of an illness. Now, however, he says he had been informed by the doctors that he is completely cured of the former illness and is capable of continuing his journey. Therefore, he is giving orders that he is to be conducted to Wuchang by his subordinates according to Chinese custom. From there he is to be passed on further from hand to hand according to the approved practice in Guangdong and the request of the people in Sichuan. And in this way the whole affair was concluded peacefully to the edification of the officials who saw a herald of the Gospel subjecting himself to | the obedience due to the civil arm and to be correcting, with so much inconvenience to himself, the mistake he admitted to have made.

In addition, Fr. Müllener told Fr. Le Couteulx that he knew for certain that his zhairen, the guards whom he had evaded, had been beaten with rods and that the siya mandarin had been deprived of his office. He had given all of them compensation in the form of gifts and he had expressly requested that the one who had been deposed should be restored to his former official position. In this way Fr. Müllener at last continued the journey he had begun to his church in Sichuan, from which we are expecting he will come to Beijing if it is really his intention to request the piao and to remain in the mission.

### 5 The journey of the Jesuits to Manchuria is delayed

June 15th. The result of this business of Fr. Müllener was reported to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing by Fr. Kilian in a letter sent to Rev. Fr. Castorano, the assistant to the Lord Bishop. On this same day, June 15th, the court was surprised that the day appointed for the Emperor’s departure to Manchuria had already passed, and as yet no one knew the date to which it was to be changed, and this was most unusual with the Emperor. The reason for the deferral was that a certain courier sent by the Supreme Commander of the army from Hangzhou had come with a message to be communicated only to the Emperor. He had covered the whole of that distance of easily three hundred French leagues in less than three days with such incredible speed and to the point of such exhaustion that he fell into a faint in the Emperor’s presence.[^p213-12] The information was communicated by his father to the Crown Prince alone, and His Majesty wrote the response with nobody present but him. It was immediately handed to two

<!-- PDF page 214 | book page 205 -->

Manchus and one Mongol to be delivered to Hangzhou likewise within three days and with this instruction: that wherever one of them should fall by the wayside through exhaustion, he was to remain there, while the others or one other was to take the letter and continue the journey. And the outcome of this very hurried expedition was being awaited before the departure to Tartary. It was not clear to anyone what business was being transacted. Rumour had it that a member of the preceding imperial family had been discovered there and captured. Some Chinese at the urging of the bonzes and certain bandits were openly forming associations on the pretext of a famine and wished to put him back on the throne. Not just a few of these had been apprehended at the beginning of this year, and some of the bonzes did not escape the hands of the Manchus, and some of the ringleaders among them were thrown into prison.

### 6 The Emperor declares that the granted to the missionaries is genuine

June 17th. Finally, after so many delays, a decree of the Emperor was sent through the Ministry of Rites to the piao Ministry of War and by this throughout the provinces. It was issued through the Great Prince[^p214-13] so that a guarantee could be given to everybody that the permit which certain Europeans said had been provided to them was genuine, and that by virtue of this permit they could say that they enjoyed civil liberty in their own houses, while travelling and in their activities, etc. If I were to speak with complete frankness about this matter my pen could scarcely keep itself within the normal bounds of writing. For the present I will defer an explanation of the individual points until the end of the month, when a copy of the decree which has been mentioned was delivered to us from the Libu.

June 20th. A letter of Fr. de Sá arrived from Suzhou. He was breathing a sigh of relief at this decree, as a result of which he was confident that he and his Christians would have some peace.

June 23rd. Father Bayard wrote that he had pacified the pagans to good effect and had put new heart into the Christians of De’an. But on the other hand he wrote that he had come across new disturbances in Suizhou where some of the yamen guards, after assembling a mob of other men, were parading through the neighbourhoods and crossroads with the sanction of the governor of the city and were striking real terror into the hearts of the Christians, taunting them on account of the banishment of the European priests such that many of them removed the outward signs of our holy religion from their houses.

### 7 The letter from Father de Sá

Fr. de Sá says: The French Fathers advised us today that all Your Reverences presented to the King on April 27th through the dawang a bian[^p215-14] regarding our piaos, asking that he inform the mandarins of the authenticity of our piaos. But until today, that is June 2nd, nothing has shown up here, not even the slightest notification in the government offices, even though already much time has passed and | several judgments have been given and we have had a number of discussions. Our four piaos were snatched from our hands by force, that is mine and those of Fathers Mendes, Magalhães, and Jacquemin, and have been held here in the yamen of the buzheng shi for two whole months, and that of Fr. Melon for a little less without either bringing a case against us or discussing restoring them. They are in the possession of the shuban Shen[^p215-15] mentioned earlier and shut away in a corner as I have been told by someone who has seen them, with the intention of not returning them at all until compelled. But the main reason is that he, seeing that we hadn’t given him money as he wanted, was always thinking up some new story since he had no other way of justifying to the buzheng shi what that yamen had done … Finally, each time things got worse, the piaos did not succeed in defending us but rather created difficulties for us and in the end were taken from us by force etc.

### 8 The letter from Fr. Bayard to Fr. Vice-Provincial

In Fr. Bayard’s letter he says the following to the Fr. Vice- Provincial:[^p215-16] After I had left the Suizhou mission on my return, that is on the first day of the fourth month,[^p215-17] there arrived two buyi (policemen) from the zhizhou[^p215-18] who said that the Emperor had expelled from China all the Europeans and would not permit the Chinese to profess the Holy Law or Tianzhu <!-- PDF page 216 | book page 207 --> jiao. ### 9 The Christian religion is in a very bad state in Fujian. Fr. Laureati comes to its aid in its difficulty. Father Laureati’s success in Fujian

The Christian religion in the province of Fujian after the expulsion of absolutely all the missionaries was in the very worst possible state. With regard to the condition of the churches of the Reverend Dominican Fathers, this is made clear from a letter of their Provincial Vicar, Rev. Fr. Fray Francisco Cantero. The churches of the Reverend Franciscan Fathers and Brothers suffered the same fate, since they had no missionary and were left in the hands of the mandarins for the time being. As for the churches of the reverend clerics it is enough to reflect on how much help to them was the case of the Most Illustrious Bishop of Conon and the confusion caused by Father Guéty. To safeguard their own faith and their churches, the Christians of Fujian sent messengers to the neighbouring missionaries in Hangzhou and Suzhou etc. Also, in letters to the Beijing Jesuits, they desperately asked for help to be provided for the souls and churches which were being destroyed. But this mission was in such a state of confusion that any remedy which a monthly or bimonthly visit from any one individual could provide in a state of such complete chaos seemed obviously useless, but would require the continual and permanent presence of a priest. But the same situation which existed more or less in all places militated against this, for from whatever place a missionary was to be transferred to Fujian, there the same difficulties were to be feared as were evidently going from bad to worse in Fujian. Eventually Father Laureati was found | and Father Antonio de Magalhães was substituted for him.[^p217-27] By this time the latter had acquired the fluency in the language which was necessary for general use and he believed that he could administer the church in Songjiang, which would be somewhat more peaceful than the region of Fujian.[^p217-28] Such being the case, the superiors entrusted this difficult, Spartan task of rebuilding the ruins of the church of Fujian to Fr. Laureati, who took up the burden with a generous spirit and set about beginning it in a rather dangerous fashion, but also successfully, as he himself relates in his letters to Rev. Fr. Vice-Provincial José Soares on April 14th, and again on the 25th of the same month.[^p217-29] Let us listen to what the Father himself has to say:

### 10 The letter from Father Laureati regarding his mission in Fujian

Rev. Father Vice-Provincial.

On the 3rd or 4th of February,[^p218-30] I indicated to Your Reverence that I was leaving Songjiang for Fuzhou fu according to the command of my superiors, and that on the way I would visit the churches which I encountered, giving them all the help of which I was capable. I stayed in Quzhou[^p218-31] for nine days and from there I wrote to Beijing of the happy meeting I had there with the supreme commander of the army or zongdu, Liang Laoye, to whom I was introduced by Hou Fuzhang, the tidu.[^p218-32] I also passed through the churches in Pucheng xian,[^p218-33] Jianning fu,[^p218-34] and Yanping fu,[^p218-35] in each of which I spent four or five days in administering the Most Holy Sacraments to the Christians. I was well treated by the mandarins and from them I obtained favourable legal interpretations according to the rescript from the Board of Mathematics, which the zongdu himself promised would be respected. My journey was not free from dangers: three times the boat in which I was being transported was wrecked and three times my baggage was saved from the water. On my first evening in Fuzhou, it was unclear whether my arrival was fortunate or unfortunate but I think that from the outcome it must be called a success. The Christians in that city had been harassed in many ways. The residence belonging to the Most Illustrious Bishop Maigrot together with the church were, by the arrangement made by the mandarins, restored to the former owner who had sold them. The residence belonging to the Reverend Dominican Fathers had been leased at a price as a warehouse for goods and our house was to be converted by the government exchequer into a yixue or school for the pagans. In the church belonging to Bishop Maigrot the literati had not placed either an image or tablet of Confucius as rumour had it earlier, <!-- PDF page 219 | book page 210 --> but the family of the gentile owner lived there as if in an ordinary house.[^p219-36] The Christians had made a real effort to prevent our residence from actually becoming a school, and when they received the reply from Your Reverence that you were to send to them a priest who held a residence permit and who would continue to look after their interests, they had two pairs of signboards made, painted and gilded, on which these characters stood out in large size: Taixi xueshu and Jinming chuanjiao.[^p219-37] They set these noticeboards up, as if they were drying and still requiring finishing, outside the painter’s workshop on the street along which, as a rule, the principal mandarins would pass fairly frequently and would see them.

April 4th. When I came on shore at the place of disembarkation, I found waiting there some Christians on foot and several horsemen with a procession arranged for the delivering of the royal permit. It consisted more or less of the four above mentioned signboards, several banners, four bamboo poles tricked out with gold, and two litters in one of which was to be carried the permit and in the other a roll of silk presented by the Emperor. There was a parasol which Li Dutung[^p219-38] had sent to be ready for me, and an address of welcome on my arrival together with light refreshments and a Tartar drink.[^p219-39] As soon as I saw all this, I shuddered. I was afraid to accept any of these things, because, since I had not visited the place before, I did not know the mind of the mandarins. The zeal and the good of the Christians hardly allowed me to reject them. If I accepted them, and the officials approved, I could proceed and innocently contribute to the glory of God and of the Holy Law; if I rejected them offhand, the Christians would have felt offended and despised and would have been shamed before the pagans. If I had left the ceremony there to visit the mandarins first, time would have run out and the mandarins would have been suspicious that I was inviting them to join in the procession. If they were to think this way, I would be going too far in allowing the procession; if they wanted to avoid it, <!-- PDF page 220 | book page 211 | manuscript page 918 --> perhaps they would issue a prohibition to the Christians, to their great embarrassment and mine.

I spoke with some of the better informed Christians and was advised by them for the present to proceed straight to the church, and then to visit the mandarins. That was satisfactory, because on the way, I had had a discussion with the zongdu, who had personally written to the sub-prefects about my arrival. | Therefore I proceeded and by now I was very close to our church when, from the opposite direction, there came the Treasurer, whose name was Jin.[^p220-40] He passed the leading horsemen, who did not dismount on the grounds that they were the bearers of imperial gifts and that they owed honour to them rather than to the mandarin, and he passed the main body of the procession. When he reached my chair, he said: “If that permit bears the imperial seal, then I as a mandarin should get down from my chair.” I replied that he was the one who knew the laws and the customs. He responded that as yet he did not know whether the permit was genuine or forged and therefore, he would come to the church to investigate it afterwards. I replied very briefly: Sui bian, that he could do this at his convenience.[^p220-41] But he began to complain that those at the head of the procession by not dismounting had treated him personally with disrespect, and gave orders that they were to be apprehended and handed over to the governor of the city and condemned to suitable punishment. The Christians were arrested and on being questioned they replied that they had been bearing imperial gifts and for that reason they were unable to pay respect to the mandarin without appearing to show disrespect to the Emperor. One of them, because he was a commoner, immediately received 20 stripes; another, because he was a bannerman, was forced to endure 16 lashes. And when a relative of his spoke up for him on the grounds that he was not guilty and suggested irregularity in the hastiness of the judgment, he was forthwith condemned to 80 lashes. And the Christians were threatened that if they did not renounce this Law, or if they adopted it again, they would be treated in the same way and even more severely.

### 11 The complicated beginning of his mission to Fujian

When I learned this, I hastened at once to the dwellings of all the mandarins, on the pretext that I had the obligation of paying them a visit. All accepted my visiting card except the Treasurer, who excused himself on the grounds that he was occupied with his guests at a banquet. In this way he deprived me of the opportunity of presenting reasons why he should free the Christians from their imprisonment and allay the fears of the others. Their alarm was increasing because the pagans did not regard my arrival with indifference and spoke openly about the hostility of the Imperial Treasurer towards me, as well as towards the Christians. Also, they sent messengers to the neighbouring towns to expose our Holy Law to ridicule and hatred.

While I was thinking about and anticipating this it was with a sudden blow that the Treasurer cut off all my hope of restoring the ruins of the church of Fujian. I could find no way to help it. If I were to have recourse to the Court, the remedy would be uncertain, and too late, for immediate and effectual help was required before the converts abandoned hope and perhaps their faith. Therefore, after imploring divine assistance, I decided to use bold means to restrain this bold fellow. On the following day, April 5th, I dressed myself in a robe I had with me which had dragons embroidered on it.[^p221-42] I took the imperial permit in my hands and took my place in my chair, and with a suitable entourage I approached the tribunal of the Treasurer. He was not at home, being busy, according to what they said, with the Governor. I sent a message to him that I had come to show him the imperial permit. He arrived soon after, whether by chance or in deference to me I do not know. As soon as he saw me, he asked whether it was convenient for me to come down from the chair, and we both stepped down at the same time. He dismissed the throng of his entourage, and entered the hall with me. He asked me to show him the imperial permit, as it <!-- PDF page 222 | book page 213 | manuscript page 919 --> were in passing. I refused to show it to him anywhere but in the audience hall, and when we arrived there, I requested the preparation of a table with incense which with great reluctance he caused to be brought. He declared he would not bend the knee. I replied that I would show the reverence to imperial gifts, and that he should do whatever he considered he was bound to do. And so, I made a reverence, genuflecting nine times with my head pressed right down to the ground, and the Imperial Treasurer did the same after I had finished. As I stood up, I saw that a huge gathering had filled the hall to witness the outcome of the affair.

But I acquired fresh courage, and in a loud voice I declared the reasons for my complaints; with some force, I asked why he had despised the imperial permit. He replied that he was not yet convinced that it was genuine; I added forcefully: “It is not established that it was a forgery, therefore you have passed judgment unjustly |and you have punished the Emperor’s subjects unjustly. And for this I call you to answer today before this great crowd gathered in your tribunal.” In his reaction to this, since he was in a state of uncertainty, I added: “For ten years I have been active in China, and wherever I happened to go, I have been well and honestly treated by the imperial officials, although I did not have the honour of an imperial permit; now when I have a permit, I am badly treated by you as if I were not presented with the imperial permit for my protection but for subjection. Come now, examine the permit and if it is a forgery burn it; but if it is genuine, return it to the one who gave it to you. Look,” I said, “I am leaving this to you, and I am withdrawing completely.” Having said this, I was preparing to leave. But the man who in other respects had been so self-assertive, having now been accused of disrespect for the Emperor before such a large audience, forgot his pride and began to ask me to keep the permit myself. He said that he did not wish to examine it and that he was more than satisfied that there was nothing forged in it or fictitious; that I should refrain from further embarrassing him. When I saw that he was saying this with some distress, I embraced the fellow and I bade him to be reassured about his position since it had never been heard of in China that a commoner or an official had been denounced to a judge by the Europeans. Let him pardon my impetuosity, and I said I was pardoning his mistake. Let him permit the Christians to live in peace and I would allow him to exercise freely his authority in his tribunal and I would treat his person with respect. He dismissed me honourably and immediately took his place in his chair, and following after me visited the church and with great politeness offered to make amends. I said that the best way to make amends was to forget the past and from now on in sincere friendship to devote ourselves, by assisting one another, to looking after the personal and public good of each of us, and I said that that was what I would do.

### 12 The Imperial Treasurer was conciliated after being treated with firmness combined with prudence[^p223-43]

This particular treasurer had caused himself to be hated by the mandarins both for his extremely haughty attitude and for the unrestrained severity which he had employed in dealing with even the minor officials of the tribunal of the Governor. And so, he had reason to fear not only me but also everyone else, lest if the opportunity present itself for any other reason at all he might be accused of disrespect for the Emperor. Truly, since this is the character of the man, I am rightly afraid that he may not be able to stomach the bitterness caused by the remedy I used and that his resentment may burst forth in a more serious attack on the Christians. Therefore, it seems necessary for Your Reverence to obtain some protection for me from the Court, mainly because it is being said that he and the Governor have had recourse to Beijing and that the former has sent a servant from his household to friends of theirs, and the latter has sent an inquiry about the permit and myself to the Ministry of Rites.

Reverend Father, I am aware that this action of mine is so complicated that even if it turns out well, it still leaves room for criticism. Deign therefore in accordance with your good judgment and paternal affection to come to a decision about the whole business, and if it seems to deserve punishment, please administer it honestly feeling assured that I will not offer resistance. But please have compassion for the Christians, I implore you, and please make every effort that they may be safe from a petty tyrant of this sort. Perhaps this could be done without any difficulty if there were a letter sent to the Governor to say that I am well known at court and have enjoyed the patronage of the imperial family. In fact, the peace of everyone else throughout this province depends on this since the officials in the other cities and towns are following the example of those in the metropolis. The missions of the Dominicans in this province have suffered grievous persecution. ### 22 The truth of the Franciscans’ report cannot be established from what the Bishop of Beijing says

12. The reply of the Lord Bishop, which we cited faithfully above, is insufficient to show that the report of the Shandong people is accurate. This is why: Either one should verify it on the authority of the Lord Bishop, or else from the reasons which the Lord Bishop gave. However, it cannot be proved simply on his authority because he was absent from the ceremony at which the permits were given, as is clear from his admission in his letter to Fr. Régis, where he insists that he cannot speak about what was done at the ceremony itself because, although there were eight priests present before the Prince to receive the permit, he himself stayed at home, since he had already been provided with a permit. The testimony of someone who was not present is not valid in law, or at any rate is less to be relied on than the testimony of the two priests, Fathers Bouvet and Jartoux, who were present from start to finish and whom the account itself states to have been present. Nor can it be proved from the reason given, as can be seen from the argument which the Lord Bishop gave in his letter to the Bishop of Ascalon. “Your Reverence has heard the report made by the eight priests after they came back to their church (or house) from the ceremony for the reception of the permit, so it can be taken to be correct.” As a proof, this is useless. The place is of no consequence: the fact that it happened to be in the bishop’s house where the Franciscans were staying is of no more relevance than, for instance, the ship in which the two Jesuit priests made their report. The time is of no consequence either: it would have been the same for both parties; the number of persons makes no difference, since they are equal; and even if we concede that the report made by Fr. Frossoloni was a joint one, since the other seven signed it, their signatures validate only that the report is an accurate account of what was said. In the whole report nothing is said explicitly or by implication of what was being said in Guangzhou, namely that the Emperor himself granted the permit to the Franciscans in Guangzhou despite the fact that they had protested that they would observe the rule made by the Lord Patriarch, and that they could not continue in their churches if they ceased to perform their ministry. (From the Report of the Legate.)

### 23 Under what agreed conditions may it be said that the permit was granted to the Franciscans by the Emperor

13. If the Lord Bishop wants to prove that the Franciscan Fathers at least by implication insisted that they would observe the rule of the Lord Patriarch, and in so doing had excluded the condition of not preaching against the Chinese Rites until the Pope’s reply had come, how will he explain away what he wrote on 22 September 1707 in answer to the requests of Fathers Bouvet, Régis, and Jartoux? He wrote, “The Franciscan Fathers in Guangzhou may say that they have accepted the imperial <!-- PDF page 238 | book page 229 | manuscript page 927 --> permit without any condition &c; I can say nothing about that, since I was not present.” This could be taken as stating that it was accepted by them without any condition, or as that what he wrote on 22 | June 170874 was confirmed by what he wrote on 22 September[^p238-75] and vice versa; that the whole truth of the report depends on the fact that the Franciscan Fathers were asked the same questions as the other missionaries and replied in the same way as the others— the permit was offered to them under the same condition as it was offered to the others; or that those others had accepted the permit with a mental reservation, in accordance with the decree of the Pope which they assumed had already or would soon arrive. On the other hand, though he might perhaps accept this kind of reservation in theology, what force could it have in law to prevent any intelligent person from concluding that the permit had been accepted in the terms in which it was offered? It is surely clear from what the Lord Bishop wrote on 22 September[^p238-76] that the permit was offered on the same condition. It therefore cannot be concluded that the report is correct if it seeks in turn to deny this.

### 24 The weakness of the arguments of those defending the truthfulness of the report

14. The Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing saw the weakness of his argument and did not want to rely on it. Changing his approach, he said: “So far as I am concerned and as my judgement goes, I say this: the Emperor will have given the certificate to those Franciscans because he was moved by pity for those poor little neophytes.” But that is no more than a pious guess which proves nothing except that there is a certain fittingness, as one might devoutly assume, in God having taken pity on the neophytes and having arranged that others would pity the poor Franciscan Fathers, and leave them undisturbed in their acceptance of the accuracy of the report. This might indeed have worked, had it not been that its falsity was prejudicial to a third party.

15. Similarly, it is only by taking a conjectural argument for granted at the outset, even when it has none of the rigour required in law, that His Lordship the Bishop can assert that one should deem that the Emperor gave the permit when he promised he would, three months earlier than June 12th; unless we follow the method of pious spiritual writers who say God can be said to have given us Christ to be our Saviour at the moment when, according to Genesis 3, <!-- PDF page 239 | book page 230 | manuscript page 928 --> he made the promise to Eve and threatened the serpent with the words, and she will crush thy head. My point does not rest on the similarity of the issues, nor on the person of the giver, which is irrelevant: my point concerns the date of the promise and that of the grant itself.

Again, if the permit was given at that time—March 9th—then it was certainly given unconditionally, since on March 9th neither the Emperor nor the Franciscans knew of the promulgation of Lord Patriarch’s regulation; so, the condition about not preaching against the Chinese Rites until such time as the Roman reply came had not yet been laid down. But there was a condition; it required the observance of the ancient practices of Matteo Ricci now and in future. Fr. Frossoloni and five other Franciscans gave this undertaking to the Emperor, signed in their own hand. My question is, did these Fathers revoke the acceptance of that condition on June 12th? If they did, there is no need for the Lord Bishop to go back to what was said on March 9th; he could simply have said that they received the certificate on June 12th, on which day they revoked the earlier condition, and in so many words refused to accept the second condition. If on June 12th they did not revoke their previously accepted undertaking, the report remains inaccurate, for to promise in future to follow the old customs in accordance with the rescripts of the Emperor is tantamount to promising not to preach against the Rites, and indeed to do so for an indefinite period of time.

### 25 Some further arguments are refuted

16. A further double argument was adopted by His Lordship the Bishop, partly based on the testimony of a servant, partly on a register of the Marshal of the Royal Palace, purporting to show that the Emperor granted the permit on March 9th; but it is inconclusive. The servant in question was a young boy, not in the service of the Prince but someone else’s servant whom the Prince had taken along to serve him on his travels about a month previously. Even if we grant that he did see the register containing the names of the persons who had received the permit and had counted eight Franciscans there, the count was certainly wrong, for the Lord Bishop and his secretary were certainly written in that list, and only five received the permit on March 9th (since Fr. Frossoloni did not, and the Bishop and his chaplain who were promised it in February actually received the permit on March 7th). To go back to the 12th of June, then: on that day only seven | Franciscans received the permit, because in addition to the previous five from Guangzhou two others had arrived from Shaanxi; if we leave Frossoloni out once again, that leaves seven. The young lad could perhaps have seen a register in which the missionaries were recorded according to their hui or religious order, together with a note saying whether the permit had been refused <!-- PDF page 240 | book page 231 --> or accepted; what he could not have seen was a record of something that had not yet happened. So he will have seen under the title Bodulu hui[^p240-77] the names of the expelled San Giorgio, Hervé, Montigny, etc.; under Sheng Duominge hui,[^p240-78] the expelled fathers Cavallero, Cantero, Astudillo, etc., and Fr. Muñoz, who was detained in Guangzhou. Similarly, under Yesu hui,[^p240-79] those who had been removed to Guangzhou—Fathers Monteiro, Ferreira, Pereira, etc., and those to whom the permit had been granted, Frs. da Silva, Dentrecolles, Bayard, Barelli, and others. And under Sheng Fanjige hui,[^p240-80] the Lord Bishop who had received the permit, Fr. Castorano, and, lower down, other Franciscans who received the permit on their way back. But the boy did not see that that was the occasion on which the permit was granted, because it was not granted to eight Franciscans, however you count them; the fact remains it was never given to the eighth, Fr. Frossoloni. The register of the Marshall of the Royal Palace, which records some of the Franciscan men above the Jesuits who received the permit in May, proves nothing. So, nothing at all can be inferred from the order of names in the catalogue which has been mentioned in the present dispute.

### 34 Why Bishop Maigrot was expelled from China, along with other allies of the Lord Patriarch

1. From the decree of the Emperor Kangxi, issued towards the end of December 1706, it is abundantly clear why the Reverend Lord Bishop of Conon, Charles Maigrot was expelled. He admitted and indeed had been shown to be ignorant of Chinese affairs and the meaning of Chinese words, but he nevertheless refused to accept the interpretation of the words and of the Chinese Rites which was given to him face to face by His Majesty. He was not even willing to accept them for the time being, until now that the explanation has been sent to the Holy See, the Pope himself should decide whether the Rites and the Chinese words, explained in a way which is totally authoritative and beyond all question, can or cannot be harmonised with Christian Law.

2. The same decree makes it clear that Reverend Lord and Vicar Apostolic Mezzafalce was exiled because, when His Majesty through the Crown Prince had asked whether he agreed with the Reverend Lord Bishop Maigrot’s view about the Chinese rites and characters, he refused to disagree with it.

3. The same decree makes it clear that Fr. François Guéty was banished from the empire because for several years he had followed Bishop Maigrot in his view and decision against the Chinese Rites, and he intended to stick to that version, contrary to the Emperor’s interpretation.

4. The same decree issued by the same Emperor makes it clear that he had required all missionaries to state that they were following the longstanding practices which accorded with his royal decree, and that on this understanding they would accept the permit, allowing them to remain in the Empire until a reply had been received from the Pope. If they refused to accept the permit on these terms, they must either leave of their own accord, or they would be expelled by the officials.

18. It is clear, indeed all too clear, that these and other similar events had become known throughout China. It is therefore clear how appalling was the turmoil into which the whole mission was thrown and the terror in every one of the 15 provinces which accompanied the fatal news of the Europeans who had been expelled and were to be expelled in future, and the Law of Christ along with them.

### 37 The China mission left desolate

19. Europeans will discover the plight of the Holy Gospel in the midst of all this from the letters of others. I wish my pen to avoid the least exaggeration. But it is certain that in the Province of Yunnan there remains not a single missionary, not a single one in the province of Sichuan—not a single one—and in the province of Fujian none except one sick man. In the provinces of Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Jiangnan, etc. many were thrown out, and almost all the rest subject to harassment. Some had their churches take from them, some were forbidden to preach, some were driven under military guard from village to village, | forced out of towns and cities despite the fact that they had already obtained the imperial permit.

20. How can I enumerate the number of churches which were demolished or looted by rioting pagans—how many were taken over by the mandarins for profane purposes, or transferred to the state coffers? How many neophytes defected, how many fled? How many catechumens did so too? How many of the faithful faltered; and how many sufferings had to be endured by those who professed their faith unwaveringly?

### 38 Three directives of the Viceroys regarding the Dominican Fr. Cróquer

In addition to the other calamities great trouble was caused by the directives proclaimed by the Viceroys. Three in particular became known through the whole empire. Firstly, a search was being made through all the provinces for Fr. Cróquer, a Dominican. They said that when he had received the imperial sentence of exile he had fled so as to be able to stay in China clandestinely. Secondly, there was news of the Governor-General[^p252-90] of the provinces of

<!-- PDF page 253 | book page 244 -->

Zhejiang and Fujian who, (mistakenly) believing the imperial decree ordered the expulsion of all Europeans from China, was attempting to throw all of them out except Fr. Muñoz, the Dominican. Thirdly, the Salt Commissioner in the province of Jiangxi was attacking the Christian religion, subjecting it to the hatred and persecution of everyone, as if (as was cruelly said) it was the most despicable of depraved sects.

21. So, calamity followed upon calamity, and the Holy Faith was heading for extinction with nobody to offer any help. The missionaries of every order kept asking the Jesuits in Beijing for help, either on their own personal behalf or on behalf of their churches or their neophytes. The Beijing Jesuits had recourse to their ordinary, the Lord Bishop, and to the Illustrious Bishops of Macau and Ascalon, as can be seen from their letters. The bishops, especially the Bishop of Beijing, tried and tried to get the Lord Patriarch, as the only person who could come to the rescue of the dying mission, to agree to look with compassion at the sufferings of the missionaries, and the spiritual ruin and persecution against the Holy Gospel, and agree to provide for the safety of so many people in the way that he, and he alone, was able to do.

### 39 The advice of the Lord Patriarch’s supporters

22. The Lord Patriarch saw fit to give no answer to these prayers, to pretend that not much harm was being done, and to put off trying to remedy the situation, while his cronies rubbed their hands at the demolition of the mission itself, with the utterly appalling remark that there were no tears to be shed over the annihilation of an entire mission in which an apostolic commissary couldn’t do whatever he liked. Then, when the time came when the ships provided the opportunity, they all wrote to Europe, giving the impression that they were ashamed of saying or doing anything more critical, and that they were trying to speak as gently as possible and to soothe people’s ears; as if that were an excuse for their own negligence in finding any remedies, and as though they were reining in their minds and their pens in the hope that who knows what Higher Providence, through whose power they read the future, might make human remedies unnecessary. The Emperor was a pagan, an atheist, an idolator, and a politician who was not interested in the Rites. He was giving and had given in the past permits to people who openly protested against the Rites. With others he pretended indifference as to whether they asked for a permit or didn’t bother to; he did not want to upset all the Europeans, since he enjoyed <!-- PDF page 254 | book page 245 | manuscript page 936 --> and needed their skills. He nourished two designs in his heart, one of which was effective, to tolerate the practice of Christianity in his Empire; the other ineffective, to get rid of the missionaries who opposed the rites of the country.

### 40 Decisions of the Beijing Jesuits

23. This advice of the Patriarch’s people did nothing to alleviate the situation of Christianity; everything continued to go daily from bad to worse. For this reason, the Jesuits living in Beijing decided that, as in any ordinary fire, there was no time to be lost by waiting to be told to bring water or where to direct it; whatever help could be given had to be given without further delay. Since they no longer had access to the Emperor, they sought a remedy elsewhere. They began with measures which would be effective but relatively low-key and less open to the biting calumnies previously directed against them. So, to bring help to the point at which the blaze was fiercer and driving people away from the new little Chinese church, of all the provinces they made an effort first to help Fujian.

24. To this end, Fr. Filippo Grimaldi explained to the Bureau of Mathematics the scope of the imperial decrees | and which missionaries they covered; in contrast, he pointed out how unfairly officials were misusing these decrees to vilify the very Holy Religion upon which the Emperor had nevertheless long ago conferred civil rights and wished to protect. For that very purpose two priests had been sent both as legates to the Pope to whom the Emperor had submitted his interpretation of the terms and rites. Upon hearing this, the Bureau of Mathematics decided to write to the Chief Military Commander of Fujian,[^p254-91] not because they had authority over him, which they had not, but as an act of friendship and patronage, asking him not to act or allow anyone else to act in a way that went beyond the imperial decrees to the detriment of our Holy Religion which the Emperor had left completely intact, attacking only those missionaries who had been so bold either in their actions or their beliefs as to pay no attention to the rites of the Empire.

### 41 The Fathers’ effort to protect religion was productive

25. The recommendations of the Bureau of Mathematics were not fruitless, at any rate in the provinces of Fujian and Zhejiang, to the extent that the Governor-General tried to undo the considerable harm done by his previous directives, recommending the mandarins under his authority not to go further than the imperial decrees permitted and to leave the churches, Christians, and those missionaries <!-- PDF page 255 | book page 246 --> who were in possession of a genuine permit in peace. The recommendations of the Bureau of Mathematics began to come through towards the end of September, and had all been sent by the middle of October. But the Governor-General’s warning had little effect beyond preventing the mandarins who had begun to do the damage from doing their very worst; their hatred was for the time being kept on the leash; they did not return what they had stolen, but neither did they take any more of the things they coveted. The churches which had been closed remained closed, and those which had been taken into private hands remained sequestrated. Among these were the church of the Most Reverend Bishop Maigrot which had been handed over to its former pagan owner, and Fr. Cróquer’s which had been allotted to traders, and our own church in Fuzhou fu which the city authorities had allocated for a school.

26. The Christians and the Jesuits however were able to make use of these events so that just as the directives of the Governor-General in Fujian had created difficulties for the Christians in other provinces, so the recommendation of the Bureau of Mathematics was helpful to them; they described it with some exaggeration as a buwen[^p255-92] and as such amounting to a revocation of the directives which the Governor-General had issued. As far as possible they made the document known in other provinces: the mandarins were not so easy to convince and much less quick to disavowing their own previous behaviour or desisting from harassing Europeans and Christians. They had already learned to get profit or pleasure from so doing.

27. Meanwhile November passed, then December, and January of the new year 1708 had nearly arrived, yet still the Lord Patriarch was doing nothing more to help the mission; all this time, his people were intent upon how they could, in China as well as in Europe, turn all the hostility attendant upon the ruin of the mission against the Jesuits, beguiling people’s minds with useless fantasies about the good things to come and covering up the current misfortunes.

### 42 The treatise on the Emperor’s twofold purpose; why it was written, and by whom

And so, in addition to the snide remarks they made every day, a complete treatise on the Emperor’s twofold intention came out, into which the Augustinian Fr. Fontanilla[^p255-93] put a lot of work, as if he thought that by arguments elaborated within the confines of his study he could repair the <!-- PDF page 256 | book page 247 | manuscript page 937 --> damage already done to the mission, or with his subtle debater’s points could withstand the iron-clad battlelines of the imperial decrees. The Illustrious Lord Bishop of Ascalon has already written his views on what we should think of Fr. Fontanilla’s futile academic exercise; how weak was the basis of its argument has already been shown by what actually happened.

### 43 The Guangdong mandarins without justification claim authority over the missionaries

For just at that time the Guangdong mandarins in their treatment of the European missionaries were acting not as lower ranking officials, but as lords. When the Bishop of Ascalon wished to leave, they refused to allow it; Fr. Porquet, sj, and six Franciscan priests who wished to apply for the permit were not allowed to set foot outside the province of Guangdong, but Fr. de Goville, sj, who was getting ready to spend some time in Guangdong was ordered to go back to his church in the province of Jiangxi, and Fr. Buonaventura da Roma | was refused permission to go back to Jiangxi. Fr. Müllener, who wanted to take ship for Europe, was sent back to Sichuan in the charge of two guards; the Dominican Fr. Luxán, along with Frs. Baluère, d’Henry, Lirot, and Fr. Fontanilla himself were expelled to Macau. They confiscated the Jesuit church in Xinhui xian and razed to the ground another church belonging to the Augustinians in the neighbourhood of the city of Shaojing. And in places where there were no missionaries at the time, the Governor allowed the mandarins to do with the remaining churches whatever suited their personal convenience or desire.

### 44 Amid the devastation and utter ruin, the Lord Patriarch speaks his mind

28. In the middle of this most violent storm, when the Franciscan commissary urged the Lord Patriarch to find a solution by allowing them to obtain a permit, the Patriarch speaking from his Elysian fields in total peace of soul had no more to say other than that he and his people in all their afflictions praised and magnified the Emperor for his clemency, and suggested that others might find in it the same comfort. He claimed that those who were bathed in favours at Court were exaggerating the imperial anger to instil terror in the mission.

29. Whatever is to be made of that kind of mental calm on the peak of some Mount Olympus high above the turmoil of winds and storms, it is nevertheless clear that it was of no more practical use than would be a stoic imperturbability in a ship tossed by the waves when the mast has been shattered and the rudder smashed; what is needed was not contemplation but hard work, particularly on the part of those to whom the ship and the lives of its passengers have been entrusted.

### 45 Attempts by the Jesuits to recover the property they had almost lost

30. Just as in Guangdong the missionaries were treated in a very disturbing manner by the mandarins, so during those same months many were appallingly treated in other provinces too. The officials were now more daring, not hesitating before their tribunals openly to subject the imperial permit itself to examination, interpretation, alteration, and contempt—on which point, see the letters of the missionaries and of His Lordship the Bishop of Beijing. Those among the officials who were more shrewd than most of the others and were cooking up something more bitter for the Europeans, including the governors of Guangdong, Fujian, Zhejiang, Shandong, Huguang, and Shanxi, referred the matter in a memorial to the Ministry of Rites, seeking from it a decision about the genuineness of the permit. The Ministry, at first, most damagingly for the Europeans, replied that so far they had heard nothing of the Europeans’ permit; later when the governors had deviously taken the permits from one or two missionaries and sent it to the Ministry, it referred them to the marshals of the Imperial Household, since they bore the seal of the Imperial Household.

31. Since everything was thus collapsing around them, and the earlier remedies had proved ineffective, it was decided to take steps to ensure that at least the civil rights of those missionaries were protected, since through the permit the Emperor had made them immune from expulsion. Since nothing less than imperial authority itself would have been of any avail, and even that had been compelled, at the insistence of the governors and governors-general, to work through the Ministries as is customary in this country, the Beijing Jesuits for the first time on March 6th verbally petitioned the Prince. Since the governors had had recourse to the Ministry of Rites about the genuineness of the permit, and it had referred the matter to the marshals of the Imperial Household, who in turn had referred it to the Prince himself; would the Prince then be so kind as to expedite the matter, with His Majesty’s approval, for the sake of restoring public order for the missionaries and their religion? To this petition they added the suggestion that the Ministry of Rites should not be given authority over preachers of the Gospel.

32. The Jesuits’ petition was listened to, but all it achieved was a reply from the Emperor saying that there would be no difficulty about the matter and it would soon be dealt with. But without anything being done, he went hunting on the following day and would have forgotten all about it when he got back, had not some way been devised to remind him. And indeed, during the hunt itself Fr. Parrenin did ask about it, and got a brusque reply from the Prince that there were no two ways about it: all those who did not accept the interpretation of the Rites which had been given would be expelled. | There seemed therefore only one recourse left to us, which was to ask once more, and in writing, <!-- PDF page 258 | book page 249 --> for indemnity for those who had accepted the permit or wished to accept it and had been prevented from doing so only by the improper actions of the mandarins. This plan Fr. Vice-Provincial and the others communicated to the recently appointed Visitor, Fr. Giampaolo Gozani; if he approved, they would take the risk of putting it into execution.

### 46 The petition is presented by the Fathers to the Prince

33. Special prayers were arranged by Rev. Fr. Vice- Provincial for a happy outcome in a matter of such importance. After these, he compiled a petition in Chinese to be submitted to the Prince, or translated into Tartar if the person who was to read it might so prefer. I have filed a Latin translation among the Acta Pekinensia, and will give it again here for the readers’ convenience:

34. “Filippo Grimaldi and the other Europeans, with all deference, reverence, and honesty, present to His Majesty everything which afflicts and occupies their hearts, petitioning that he would deign to have pity on them. We are foreign scholars who have received the extraordinary favours from His Majesty of being free to preach the Law of Christ—a favour granted also to all the Europeans, dispersed through the provinces, by which they can come to the Court into his presence and by which he further granted his imperial permit, enabling them to live wherever they chose with a peaceful heart and free from molestation. When we consider those benefits, we can find no Emperor in history who has treated foreigners with such kindness and provident care. Having received such enormous favours, we and the other Europeans were persuaded that we need have no fears in the future. Yet hardly had your favour been received by us than the provincial governors in Shandong, Jiangnan, Huguang, etc. ignoring Your Majesty’s benevolence, have begun to conduct random investigations casting doubt upon the genuineness of the permit. They have on many occasions made inquiries about its genuineness before the Ministry of Rites, which has consistently replied that it knows nothing of such a permit of Your Majesty’s. The governors, in the light of this response, have become more and more dubious about the genuineness of the permit. Now the Governor-General of the Province of Zhejiang and Fujian, a man called Liang Nai, has laid down an edict which was communicated to all the provinces of the Empire. This edict insists that all Europeans, with the exception of Guo Duolu,[^p258-94] who is allowed to remain in a single church in Guangzhou, are to be excluded from the Empire; it makes no mention whatever of those who are in possession of an imperial permit given by Your Majesty and are therefore <!-- PDF page 259 | book page 250 | manuscript page 939 --> allowed to stay in China. The rest of the provincial officials, in the light of an edict like this, are becoming more and more confirmed in their doubts, and every day initiate one inquiry after another. Our study of the laws and customs of the Empire informs us that the provincial officials will not have any confidence in or acceptance of any certificates unless they can at the same time see a wen (that is, an authentic rescript) from some ministry of the central government[^p259-95] confirming the genuineness of the permits. If it is not understood throughout the provinces that the Europeans have been graciously admitted to the presence of Your Majesty and have been granted their permit, it will be impossible in accordance with imperial custom to establish its authenticity as having been truly and in fact granted by Your Majesty. In letters sent to us from every direction we read of the harassments to which innocent Europeans are being subjected in the provinces because of the mandarins’ investigations about their permits. This is all but intolerable, and it is for that reason that we have no alternative but to have recourse to Your Majesty, to inform you of these matters and to beg for relief. We believe that what would bring about such relief would be for Your Majesty to make it known through the Libu or Ministry of Rites that that permit had truly been given by Your Majesty, that Europeans who intend to accept it are to be well-treated by the provincial officials, and that those who have accepted it should have no further doubts cast upon its authenticity. | In this way not merely we ourselves but also all the other Europeans will continue to rejoice in Your Majesty’s favour and patronage. To this end we, your subjects from afar, with the greatest reverence, have compiled this petition, begging the Great Prince as a mediator on behalf of us all, to present it to Your Majesty, from whom we hope for a decree concerning this matter, to be published everywhere.”

35. This petition was at once submitted to all the Jesuits living in Beijing for their advice, and although there was nothing in it they thought should be changed, they were afraid of the ensuing calumny; so they hesitated for some days as to whether it might be better not to submit it, in case we might seem to have provided a justification for interpreting the petition in a most unfair way with very severe consequences for the Society. In the end the decisive reason was that even if, following the example of other people, we kept quiet and suppressed the petition, still if what happened to the others was anything to go by we would not escape criticism, and many people would blame the collapse of the mission on our refusal to speak out. So it was concluded that it would be <!-- PDF page 260 | book page 251 --> better to strike out strongly to save the mission and to endure severe calumny, if only the mission could be spared. The agreement and encouragement of Fr. Visitor in a letter supported the same conclusion, and so it was unanimously agreed to submit the petition and to commend the outcome to St. Joseph, patron of the mission, and to all the others of the heavenly hosts.

### 47 It is brought to the notice of the Emperor, who gives orders that the Jesuits’ request be approved

36. On April 27th of this present year, the Fathers presented the petition to the Eldest Prince as he sat in his villa in the midst of the mandarins who were in charge of his affairs. He accepted it, read it, conferred with those around him, and with his companions went up and showed the document to the Emperor while giving his own view of the issue it raised. His Majesty acceded to the requests. What the Prince said in his audience, and how he said it, was included by him in the decree to be made public from the Ministry of Rites. Here is the translation:

37. The toro zhi jun wang (the title of the Prince);[^p260-96] the Wuying dian Prefect of Works, Yuan Weilang;[^p260-97] Heseken and Zhang Changzhu,[^p260-98] and the Yangxin dian Prefect of Works Zhao,[^p260-99] on that day, the 7th of the intercalated month,[^p260-100] met, <!-- PDF page 261 | book page 252 | manuscript page 940 --> consulted together, and determined whether the preceding memorial should be presented to the Emperor by which all the Europeans living in the churches throughout the provinces and engaged in preaching religion who have already received the permit may freely live wherever they choose, and the officials of the cities may not in any way make this difficult for them. But those to whom the royal permit has been denied should be forbidden to live in their churches, and should be deported to Macau. Those who have just arrived, or who have been staying in China for a considerable time and wish to come to the court to obtain a certificate should not be prevented from doing so by the provincial officials; the officials, however, should not allow them to plan for stops along the way, but should encourage them to come as quickly as possible. In future, the names of those to whom permits have been granted together with the names of those refused should be written down and given to the Grand Minister of the Imperial Household,[^p261-101] so that they can be sent from his office (called the Poiyambas) to the Ministry of Rites; and the Ministry of Rites (or Libu) should ensure that they are published throughout all the provinces of the Empire.

The Prince had these words together with his own exposition and our petition bound together in a single folio, as is the procedure in ministerial business, and sent it written in Manchu to the marshals of the Imperial Household, who made the following additions before they had it sent on to the Ministry of Rites:

Herewith we send you the number and names of those Europeans who have previously received a piao under the seal of our department. In future, those Europeans who are in possession of a piao must not | be prevented from living peacefully in their churches; but those who have not been given a piao must not be permitted to remain but should be sent off to Macau. If however there are some who wish to receive a piao, the city officials should not allow them to delay on the journey but should urge them to go to the Court as soon as possible. For this reason we <!-- PDF page 262 | book page 253 --> have made a list of their family and personal names, and are sending them to your Libu offices so that you can notify and inform everyone in Beijing and the other provinces of all these matters.

40. Vice-Provincial respectfully sent it to the Lord Bishop of Beijing in Linqing, before the Prince had a chance to revise it. ### 50 A summary of what has been said so far

41. Before I continue with my account, I suggest that the reader cast his mind back to have an overview of what has happened so far. It remains only to say how the execution itself went, and what result it achieved.

Between April 27th and May 21st the imperial decree had not yet been promulgated, and it was looking as if there would only be a fortnight left before the Emperor departed for Tartary to spend the whole summer and almost all of autumn there (though the journey was in fact postponed until June 28th because of trouble with some rebels). ### 52 The errors that the Prince had introduced while writing regarding time, persons, etc

44. When the Fathers realised what the Prince was writing, with a sigh they gently intimated that the result of such an appendix was to add a serious burden to the generous grant of the Emperor and to do great harm to the missionaries remaining in China. But the Prince paid little attention either to the sad expression on the priests’ faces or to the sorrow expressed in their sigh, but finished what he had started to write, his face flushed, his eyes flashing, in complete silence, and quite upset. That he was upset is proved by the many errors he made concerning dates and other matters. He says that the Dominican priests, Fathers Montigny, Le Breton, and Carvalho were expelled on the 8th day of the third month, and that Fathers Hervé and San Giorgio were threatened and exiled in the 4th month, whereas the latter were in fact expelled in the 3rd month and the former two in the 4th month, as can be established beyond doubt from the decrees themselves, which appeared at the time, as well as from the admissions or statements of the people who had been expelled. Again, he says that those expelled had to leave with the Lord Patriarch in the 5th month, but it was well known from the text and from the execution of the decrees that it was not “within the fifth month” but “within five days,” which had been decided upon.

45. Since the three Jesuits had been unable there and then to dissuade the Prince from his fierce resolve, several times in the days following, although they received a milder reception, they nonetheless came away with nothing but insults. They asked the mandarins of the Ministry of Rites whether, because there were some inaccuracies in the annotations made by the Prince, all of which added nothing useful to His Majesty’s decree, they might be willing to pretend they hadn’t notice, and leave these points out. But the officials were unwilling to leave out even a syllable of anything the Prince had added in <!-- PDF page 266 | book page 257 | manuscript page 942 --> his own hand and soon issued instructions to the scribes to have twenty copies made. Fifteen were needed in China and five others were to be sent to Tartary to the peoples living there who are now subjects of the monarchy of Kangxi.

### 53 The source of discrepancies between copies of the documents

It is noteworthy that when the missionaries were informed of the promulgation of the decree many of them had made arrangements to get from some office | or other in their city a transcript of some kind. These transcripts, as we could see from ones that were sent to Beijing, did not always agree with one another word for word, even though we have never so far noticed any substantial discrepancy. This must be put down to carelessness, whether deliberate or accidental on the part of the copyists, who, brought in from outside for unofficial business, hardly ever transcribe accurately. This is both so that they can easily excuse themselves before the officials on the grounds that they are not writing up the proceedings of the office itself, but only a rough version that they sold on for a price, and also so that if you want an accurate copy, they can enjoy squeezing some more money out of you. Now to recount the consequences of the promulgation.

### 54 How the Emperor’s decree was received in the provinces and ordered to be carried out

46. It was expected that the promulgation of this decree would have one effect so far as the mission and the missionaries were concerned, and another for, or rather against, the Jesuits in Beijing. And that’s just how it turned out in both cases, more or less exactly as expected. Letters from many people in the provinces reported that the officials, when they had received notice from the Ministry of Rites, had handed back the churches and published the permission granted to the Christians to gather for sacred activities there, and had restrained shows of disrespect from the pagan population. They had once again restored the missionaries to a position which made it publicly obvious that they enjoyed civil rights in China. I am unable to give more details on this point since we have not yet had any news from the more remote provinces. Not that the decree of June 17th sent from Beijing by the Ministry of Rites via the Ministry of War had failed to arrive in those provinces, but because mandarins living in more isolated places often delayed promulgating anything they got from the Court as they saw fit. Ample testimonies to this will be found in Europe from the letters of the missionaries in Guangzhou. I would give these details here if I had them to hand, but until today, October 19th, when I am writing this, no further information about the effect of promulgation of the decree in Guangzhou has reached us, except that <!-- PDF page 267 | book page 258 --> the mandarins inquired of Fr. Visdelou, sj, and of Fathers Giampè, Cordero,[^p267-121] and Bénard[^p267-122] why they did not want to go to Beijing to receive the permit. They replied in writing that they were not willing to follow the doctrine of Matteo Ricci, since it was subject to a prohibition from the Supreme Pontiff. These written replies of theirs by order of the mandarin had been contemptuously thrown back into the sedan chairs in which they had approached his office.

### 55 Complaints against the Jesuits

47. There was no lack of complaints against the Beijing Jesuits, even from their friends. These were unhappy to see that, where the Jesuits had previously enjoyed good repute, the results of decisions affecting others was casting a shadow of misconduct upon them. The way in which these criticisms were expressed was different, depending on whether they came from friends or opponents. Friends were saddened by the decree, which offered at once a peace and a certain humiliation, but in such a way that they nevertheless sympathized with the Beijing Jesuits; they took it that such a decree would never have appeared had the Jesuits not got into a damaging muddle about it, but they consoled themselves with the thought that perhaps the ignominy coming from elsewhere could be cleared up, the way mud can be removed from the feet of travellers. The opponents, on the other hand, laid the whole mix-up concerning the decree at the door of the Jesuits as if every word of its contents had been dictated at the Jesuits’ request. They accused them of bad faith for having the dishonourable intention to secure the expulsion of those missionaries who even without accepting the permit could still have remained in China; they had done more to prejudice the mission by arranging that the disgraceful decree of the Emperor, which had been directed at some individual missionaries, should be promulgated with the authority of the Ministry of Rites throughout the entire empire, than any benefit they had secured by keeping a few missionaries safe from the harassments of the people and the mandarins. Moreover, it had to be said that, from the point of view of good reputation and freedom to preach the Gospel, more spiritual damage is done if something dishonourable is widely said about even a single European minister of the Gospel than if one praises hundreds of others. I pass over many <!-- PDF page 268 | book page 259 | manuscript page 943 --> other such allegations which stem simply from the | bad habit of maligning the Jesuits, but I will answer these three.

### 56 Reply to these complaints

48. Firstly, if it is wrong to arrange that a few missionaries are expelled in order to retain the rest, it would be even worse to arrange for many to be expelled in order to retain a few or none at all. In any case, the Jesuits did not have to order the Emperor to be informed personally of events which were matters of common knowledge, that their Reverences Maigrot, Mezzafalce, and Guéty had been expelled. By then, the same sentence had already been passed on them all. No matter who it is who says they believe that, or who do not contest what the three men just mentioned said, it was not the Jesuits who said that it would be better if all the missionaries were forced to leave and the mission had been eradicated from its very foundations. If the Jesuits arranged that a few people had to leave whose presence would have made it impossible for the mission to survive, given the iron force of the imperial decree, they are less to blame than those responsible for many missionaries having left already, either of their own accord or because they had been or were about to be expelled.

### 57 Without the imperial permit the missionaries were unable to remain in China

[49.][^p268-123] Secondly, the Beijing Jesuits did not engineer the expulsion of any missionary who could have remained without having accepted the imperial permit. To hold the Jesuits responsible, examples must be given of some, even a single one, who was able to stay on without a permit. How weak the reasons—no better than guesses—of the people in Guangzhou and the Patriarch’s supporters are can be judged from what has already been said and will always remain obvious. Notwithstanding the arguments of these people, the man who was promoting them, Fr. Fontanilla,[^p268-124] was expelled without even the knowledge let alone at the instigation of the Beijing Jesuits, because he did not have a permit; this at the very time when he was convinced that his arguments were utterly valid and wanted them to be accepted by a wider public. In the same way were expelled from Guangzhou Fathers Lirot, de la Baluère, d’Henry, and Fr. Luxán; Fr. Basset was sentenced to expulsion but his death intervened. Again, the younger Fr. Caballero,[^p268-125] who had secretly reentered, <!-- PDF page 269 | book page 260 | manuscript page 944 --> was investigated and expelled. If these 6, and a further 16 who had been expelled already, had to leave because they did not have a permit, how could an empire-wide imperial decree concerning expulsion from China have been arranged to require either that a permit from which someone had been exempt had after all to be obtained, or that a dispensation would be given to Fathers Visdelou, Giampè, Cordero and Bénard? One could wish that this were possible, but there was never any hope that it would turn out like that for those four. That still does not prove that the Beijing Jesuits engineered their expulsion if they could have stayed in China without a permit.

### 58 For which points in the most recent imperial decree the Jesuits were, and were not, responsible

50. But, thirdly, neither did the Jesuits arrange for people to be expelled from China who had no well-founded hope of being able to remain without a permit. The Jesuits sought simply this, that it was recognized that a genuine and valid imperial permit had been and was to be given to certain missionaries; that, once the authenticity of their permit had been established, they should be allowed to live in peace, to visit the missions in their districts, and to engage publicly in the ministries of religion to their neophytes. Whom did they harm in so doing? Anyone who was willing could have enjoyed the same benefits which the Jesuits had secured, and if they were unwilling, it was certainly not because the Beijing Jesuits had made them so, even if the Jesuits had had any motive to make difficulties for them. Once the decree had been promulgated by the authority of the Ministry of Rites, the Jesuits could not have prevented anyone from being protected by the decree if in virtue of a permit they wished to remain in China. Anyone who wanted to stay in China simply had to go to the Court to be given one. I will not here give any of the reasons which positive Church law and even the strictest theological opinions allow in self-defence even when an unintended side effect is that someone else suffers harm, for I am writing here not for the sake of carrying on a disputation, but for the sake of recounting the events as they took place.

51. Fourthly, in case our opponents are unaware of it, the only part of the whole complex decree which is the responsibility of the Beijing Jesuits is their memorial and the words contained in the Emperor’s reply to it; they will never establish that we were the source for anything else. Let it suffice for me to say this now, and I shall explain later in more detail. The Beijing Jesuits | through their Vice-Provincial asked their Most Reverend Ordinary either to come himself or to send someone else with the power to make a legal inquiry into this affair, and to issue in legal form his findings on what the Jesuits had done, and what the Prince and the Tribunal had added. His Lordship the Bishop <!-- PDF page 270 | book page 261 --> of Beijing did not institute a formal inquiry, but said that the Jesuits should not be held responsible, not that they should be condemned without a hearing.

### 59 Any shame on the part of missionaries about that decree was unjustified, inappropriate and not due to anything the Fathers had done

52. Fifthly, some missionaries were ashamed that Christians and pagans should have heard that several of their European colleagues had apparently been named and expelled by the Emperor. Surely, if this is what one should be ashamed of, those who provoked such a decree against themselves should be the more and the longer ashamed; for it was because they did this that they provided the occasion and the grounds for the shame of others. But they deny that their foolhardiness was cause for shame, when they refused to agree with the Emperor’s interpretation; indeed, they think this redounds to their credit, and that they should be called and described in writing in China as confessors, almost as martyrs of Christ. Well, if they think it is a credit to them to have been expelled by the Emperor’s decree, they can hardly say that the publication of that decree— whether arranged by the Jesuits or coming from some other source—brings the shame of those expelled down upon their brothers remaining in China. I admit, however, that in the eyes of the simple people who judge only by appearances and the popular way of thinking about religion, it was more than a little damaging to our Holy Religion and those who preached it that information about the exile of the Europeans became public knowledge, since an associate of an exile is readily thought himself to deserve exile. Moreover, the reason for the exile is complex, so that anybody would get tired of hearing people railing against the exiles, and get quite confused as a result. But this unavoidable annoyance was not imposed upon the missionaries by the Beijing Jesuits, just as it was not they who had made it unavoidable that they had had to suffer the earlier harassment from mandarins denying the authenticity of the permit. In all this what was nastiest for the ministers of the Gospel came from the ill-will, or at any rate the inconsiderateness, of the Prince, which no amount of contact with the Fathers was able to counteract. Indeed, a reasonable assessment of the state of affairs would be that it was preferable for the missionaries, once they had been subjected to some slight short-lived disgrace in the eyes of the uninformed, that they should receive recompense for the subjection and vexation of the officials and the persecution of the people since they are now in a position to make a complaint. Some Europeans have been expelled, but those who are in possession of a permit cannot be expelled, nor prevented from the public exercise of their ministry, the conversion of Chinese, or the administering of baptism and the other sacraments.

<!-- PDF page 271 | book page 262 | manuscript page 945 -->

### 60 The same can be said about any harm that might have come from the same decree

53. Sixthly what has been said about the disgrace can also be said about the harm which some allege ensued from the publication. The harm follows on from the disgrace, and its extent must be measured in the same way. The cause of the harm too was the same as that of the disgrace. If then the disgrace was nonexistent or slight, the harm too must therefore also be nonexistent or slight. For where would the harm be, unless perhaps that some people whose faith is weak were scandalised by the exile of the Europeans, being afraid that there must be something bad underlying their teachings, or that pagans might conceive a revulsion towards Europeans and the Faith? Even if a hundred weak Christians and a hundred gentiles in each province were scandalised in this way, it still cannot be denied that more than two hundred thousand Christians were strengthened, and the road to faith remained open to all the pagans, once the Ministry of Rites had announced that the decree was not against our Holy Religion and that missionaries in possession of a permit had been granted leave to live and to travel in China anywhere they wanted to, preaching, building churches, and gathering congregations together. That some should have wavered because of the disapproval of exile openly shown in other provinces, we might believe. But are we to believe that there were no waverers at all in the provinces of Fujian, Zhejiang, Jiangnan, Yunnan, Sichuan, Shandong, and Guangdong when under their own eyes they saw missionaries expelled, churches closed down, and the ceremonies of our Holy Religion forbidden? But you will say, there was no need to add injury to injury, or to make those already wavering fall down altogether. That is quite true—if tranquillity can always be obtained without risk. On the other hand, to stop a ship from running onto the rocks something—sails, or oars, or something else—has to be put in motion, and while some are hoisted others are taken inboard; a small movement of the ship is counted as calm because it tends in that direction and brings calm about. |

### 61 Whether the expulsion of some missionaries from China was a matter of honour or disgrace is put in perspective

54. Seventhly, I will not produce more evidence to refute our opponents’ complaints, since I have even great difficulty in understanding what they mean. But to put it in a nutshell, they say that all the missionaries are hurt if one of them is dishonoured; in what way I cannot see, unless perhaps they wish to maintain that those men are dishonoured whose names were published in all the provinces with the information that they had been expelled for not having a permit and that their disgrace contaminates the others. But if that is the argument, then it comes back to the point made above: either they think that their expulsion disgraced <!-- PDF page 272 | book page 263 --> them and others, or did them honour. If it disgraced them, it is their own fault, since they were willing to be expelled; if it honoured them, then there should be no complaint. Perhaps some people think that to have suffered exile will bring honour to them in Europe, and don’t want it to be known in China that they were expelled. In Europe the calamity of being exiled will perhaps lead to some aid from the Patrimony of St. Peter,[^p272-126] whereas here being known as an exile, if it does not actually hinder, certainly does not help. But even here the same point can be made against those who wished to be exiled. So, they will have to prove that being exiled did not count against them, but that the Jesuits harmed them by not scotching the rumour about their exile despite the fact that to do so was not within their power. But in the end, complaining about their exile now really is flogging a dead horse.[^p272-127]

### 62 It was advantageous for those who were expelled to be known and named

I mean to say, which province did not know before 17 June 1708 that some Europeans had been expelled? In which case it was up to a point preferable that the names of those exiled should be published by the Ministry of Rites, since in that way rumours and lies were corrected, and it became clear to everyone that not all the Europeans but only a small proportion of them had been expelled, and that not because of their religion but because of their personal views. Our critics should remember what a spectre of exile the guidelines of the Governor-General[^p272-128] of Fujian and Zhejiang raised in every part of the empire by writing that all the Europeans had been expelled with the exception of Guo Duolu, or how threatening was the rumour occasioned by the notorious memorial of the Salt Commissioner. How much authority was given to false rumours about the slaughter of other Europeans through the topic being set in the examination of scholars in several provinces, when the chief examiners in the examinations for the degree of Licentiate were said to have asked the bachelors in that year to exercise their brushes in elaborating a suitable document of thanksgiving worthy of the Emperor’s eyes, because he had expelled the Europeans from the empire? What remedy was there for all this? This was what happened, was it not? Wasn’t it in many ways notorious and spread through the whole Chinese world? Nevertheless, the Jesuits did and still do have regrets that <!-- PDF page 273 | book page 264 --> they were unable to prevent the news being more widely spread, but they leave it to the world to judge whether something was not gained by the truth itself at last coming to light in due process, so that it was clearly seen that the people expelled were particular individuals, not everyone in general; a determined number, not everyone; and named European individuals and not an indiscriminate collection.

55. Let us now return to a more detailed narrative of events. As was said, on 17 June 1708 the decree was sent to the provinces. I have given the details of its various parts above, with the exception of the list of names, which the marshals and the Prince had mentioned, because I do not think it is of much use at this point. We received a complete copy of the decree from the Ministry, and Fr. Provincial sent a copy of it to our ordinary on June 30th. When this had been read in Linqing, doubts at once arose which the reader must allow me to add here for the sake of giving a complete account. So, on July 31st the Lord Bishop wrote in Spanish about these doubts which had arisen there.

### 64 The delusions of the Bishop of Beijing and his companion in interpreting the decree

58. There is no room for doubt that words like these were written by the Lord Bishop in a completely friendly manner. But one can easily guess from what he says that questions would arise and complaints which would greatly trouble the Beijing Jesuits. Three queries are raised here and there are said to be three people who raised them: the Lord Bishop, his assistant, and a <!-- PDF page 275 | book page 266 | manuscript page 947, 948 --> catechist with one or two other Christians. These three interpretations differ. So, what would thirty or three hundred others say? The Lord Bishop is evidently not yet completely familiar with the Chinese spoken and written language. He says that in the view of some people, the Emperor’s own words are these, fengzhi. But these are not His Majesty’s words, they are the title of the document, and have more or less the same sense as in our language “The Words of the King” would have for the French in accordance with the customs of that Court, or as in Rome a pontifical decree begins with the words “By Order of His Most Holy and Illustrious Lord.” Fr. Castorano was closer, but the only words which are the Emperor’s own are those which I explained at the end of section 37 above: “So be it; let the Libu be so ordered.”[^p275-136] It is not known in Beijing exactly what the catechist and his companions said, but it is fair to assume that His Lordship had no better understanding of what his catechist was saying than he had of the text, given that he took the title of the decree to be the decree itself, and on the basis of what was said to him rather than what he read, since he does not know Chinese characters. In any case, even if the church catechist and one or two of the Christian commoners imagined that everything from start to finish had been proposed by the Beijing Jesuits, nothing definitive could be drawn from it, for the assertion of people like that who are ignorant of curial style is no basis for forming a judgement. Nevertheless, from such a wide variety of opinions in the one house it was reasonable to conclude that just as in Linqing there was a group of three different judgements, so in many places there would be more and more differences of opinion, ever more bitter against the Beijing Jesuits until, relying upon the mistaken authority of the first, in the end they would reach an unshakeable verdict against them. So, to provide the quickest and most effective way of resolving the doubts and justifying what we had done, the Rev. Fr. ### 66 The same letter translated into Latin. It recounts the reason for seeking a decree from the Emperor, and how it was done

60. Most Illustrious and Most Reverend Lord.[^p276-138]

I kiss Your Illustrious Reverence’s sacred hand, and send you this letter, shorter than the urgency of the matter might have justified. Also, I enclose the series of Chinese texts relevant to our memorial and the decision which was taken by His Majesty on the 7th day of the 3rd intercalary moon of this year, the 47th of the Emperor’s reign. Just as I considered myself obliged to reply in this way to each doubt to which they gave rise and which Your Illustrious Reverence was kind enough to pass on to me, so that there would be no room for misunderstanding. To this end, I thought it essential to be careful that each section of the text was described in order, with the characters or words which come at the end of each noted in my own hand. I thought it was absolutely necessary to take this trouble to describe and annotate the text because I assume that the educated Chinese of his church are not clear about how far the wording of our memorial went and where our words ended; which words were conveyed to the Emperor by the Prince, and which were added by himself; and which words begin and end the instructions given by the Poyambis and which were part of the reply of the Libu; nor what this ministry testified about the permit which was to be published in all the provinces of the empire. On this assumption, then, I could think of no way which was shorter or clearer or less likely to raise still further doubts than to mark it up with notes in my own hand on the Chinese paper itself and to append at the end my official seal. This makes it clear where each of the sections starts and finishes, and the author of each. Furthermore, if those who are unsure say that they have still not been satisfied, may I humbly request Your Illustrious Reverence as strongly as I can that you be so kind to come to the Court yourself as soon as you can, or to send your assistant and notary to conduct a most careful examination on the spot, including each and every action performed and the circumstances surrounding the presentation of our memorial, from the first moment of its presentation until its final dispatch from the Libu to the Bingbu and from the Bingbu to all the provinces was completed.[^p276-139] I make this <!-- PDF page 277 | book page 268 | manuscript page 949 --> request so that the whole world can be sure of the unvarnished truth of the matter, and that the door may be closed to the quibbles of the disaffected so that we missionaries in Your Illustrious Reverence’s diocese can spend our precious time in caring for and saving souls rather than being forced to spend ages putting together explanations to prove that our conduct has been irreproachable, and defending our innocence which we wish to be unsullied by even the slightest shadow of suspicion. | For this reason, and to prevent suspicions from growing like weeds in the hearts of the ministers of the Gospel and choking or damaging or lessening the principal harvest of the Gospel, which is a love that thinks no evil, I solemnly swear to Your Illustrious Reverence before God, angels, and men that in the present affair concerning the memorial or petition neither I, nor any of my subjects, whether in person or through anyone else, openly or in secret, directly or indirectly, have sought, done, or arranged anything except what is contained explicitly in our memorial; we considered ourselves obliged to present the memorial to alleviate the burdens and troubles which were laid on the missionaries who were in possession of a permit. It was morally impossible to put an end to these unless it was made clear through every province, on the authority of the Ministry of Rites, that their permits were genuine and authentic. Our earlier attempts to find ways of achieving this result satisfactorily had come to nothing. Indeed, we came face to face with the hostility of the Libu Ministry in the replies which it publicly issued to the governors. It was quite clear from this that our efforts were in vain and would continue to be so. The only solution would be to have an imperial order in accordance with the customary order of precedence, published by that supreme ministry; and since the general misery demanded an immediate, effective, and universal remedy, we finally decided, having first prayed for divine assistance and focussed our intention on God and the salvation of souls, to produce this memorial to help to comfort the missionaries who were being disturbed by continuous harassment from the officials who persecuted them by every day casting fresh doubts on the authenticity of the permit they had received from His Majesty. Your Illustrious Reverence can give ample witness, since you have experienced at first-hand what was done, and how often repeated inquiries were instigated throughout the province of Shandong about the piao already received.

I have confidence in your sense of fairness and in the justice of my request, and trust that you as a good shepherd will not neglect to have <!-- PDF page 278 | book page 269 | manuscript page 950 --> a juridical and strict inquiry to discover the truth about us, your servants and workers in the vineyard of the Gospel, who are at the mercy of the uncontrolled whims of our opponents, who in every part of the world have torn us apart with their vicious calumnies, filling whole books with them, as Your Illustrious Reverence is well aware. In conclusion, I humbly kiss your sacred hands and seek your blessing and with my whole heart commend myself to your holy sacrifices of the Mass. Beijing, 22 August 1708.

In reply to your letter, I would say this: there was no need for you to take so much trouble to settle the doubts which had arisen because of this decree. As I wrote, educated Chinese in our church knew that the decree had taken several forms, and this is confirmed by the other text you sent me. ### 70 The Prince presented nothing to the Emperor in the name of the Fathers but in his own

63. In this letter His Illustrious Lordship explains what his previous letter had left unclear about the view taken by his catechist and one or two other Christians. So, we can believe that His Lordship had been given a more accurate account of how they understood it. However, they are still somewhat mistaken about one matter, or else he expresses himself badly, in claiming that they say that the Prince conveyed this or that to the Emperor in the name of the Beijing Jesuits. The Prince conveyed nothing in our name, and while he did present our petition, he said other things in the presence of the Emperor in his own name and in the name of his colleagues, as is clear from the text of the decree. The final additions were made by him on May 21st on his own initiative, without the Emperor’s consent and indeed without his knowledge, 24 days after our memorial had been presented to His Majesty and a decree had been agreed to. However, although His Lordship has given us quite a lot of support by swearing that he has no complaint about us and does not even have suspicions about us, as if we might have done something against our duty, or against justice, or something not consonant with charity, what comes across as a spontaneous and as it were private vote of confidence is less helpful than he would have been had he carried out the inquiry we asked for. Still, he did write to the Provincial to say that he was doing other things as well to protect our good reputation.
