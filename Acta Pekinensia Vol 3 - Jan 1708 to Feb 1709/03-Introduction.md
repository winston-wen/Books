The Acta Pekinensia, here presented in a translated and annotated form, is a mainly Latin document in the Jesuit Roman Archives. It is the work of Kilian Stumpf, a German Jesuit priest, one of the “Beijing Fathers”, who lived and worked in a variety of capacities at the Court of the Emperor of China known from his reign title as Kangxi (reigned 1661–1722). Stumpf originally worked in the imperial glassworks located in the French Jesuit establishment called the Beitang [Northern Church] situated close to the walls of the Palace. He lived with the French Jesuits and frequently worked on various tasks, scientific and technical, inside the Palace itself or in the Emperor’s Villa, his gardens outside the city of Beijing. As such, Father Kilian was a privileged observer of events. When the visit of a Papal Legate to China was announced he was appointed by the China Vice-Province as its Procurator, or negotiator with the Legate; and by the Jesuit General as an Apostolic Notary, a certified recorder of events. He was a privileged insider and his work is both an official quasi legal document, and, graced by his considerable literary talents, a lively account of the legation and consequent events.

The original document is a repository of the linguistic variation of a major international organization, the Society of Jesus, around 1700. The basic language of the Acta is the working Latin of the Catholic Church and international scholarship and diplomacy: a classical base overlaid with theological and ecclesiastical language, technical terminology, and local usage. The Acta is littered with Chinese names and terms in a not always consistent romanization but of interest to students of Chinese language, especially of dialect variation in the mid-Qing period. There are many additional documents that have been included, especially in this third volume, written in Portuguese, the lingua franca of the China mission, because China belonged to the sphere of influence of the Portuguese Padroado, as well as in Spanish and Italian. The translators have aimed at what we believe to be the author’s intention in producing a quasi-legal document, clarity before elegance, but Kilian Stumpf appears to have consciously used irony and humour, sometimes to the consternation of his ecclesiastical superiors and we have attempted to retain this and to provide in some cases more modern colloquial equivalents. It is our hope moreover that the final text be intelligible to a non-native English-speaking audience rather than a literary masterpiece.

The first volume of the published Acta covers the arrival of the Legate, Charles Thomas Maillard de Tournon, Patriarch of Antioch, in China in April 1705, his voyage, mainly on the Grand Canal to Beijing, and his stay there and <!-- PDF page 11 | book page 2 --> negotiations with Kangxi from December 1705 to his departure at the end of August 1706.[^p11-1] The second deals with the aftermath of that encounter: Tournon’s fatal decision to issue a Regulation condemning the Chinese Rites in February 1707 before he had received the decision of the papal commission on the subject, and the Emperor’s reaction, exiling supporters of the Legate and ordering the Legate himself to be held in Macau until the Pope replied to his complaints through his Jesuit envoys.[^p11-2]

The focus in this, the third volume, covering January 1708 to February 1709, shifts to the provinces with Stumpf reporting on the basis of extensive correspondence with missionaries throughout China on the fallout from the Emperor’s decision requiring a promise to support the Chinese Rites as a condition for missionaries receiving a piao or residence permit to stay in China and spread the Gospel. Stumpf on the basis of his correspondence with the Jesuits in Macau and Guangzhou describes the increasingly erratic behaviour of the Legate under house arrest in Macau where he clashed with the Portuguese authorities.

The viewpoint however is still Beijing as the “Peking Fathers” attempt to pacify the Emperor’s wrath against the Legate, and to deal with Kangxi’s hostile eldest son who has been placed in charge of issuing the piao. There is much here of interest to general historians of China. Missionaries who had received a permit found it disregarded by local officials despite—or perhaps because of—it being issued by the Imperial Household Department and not the regular Chinese bureaucracy. There are interesting signals of Chinese/Manchu tensions and some significant episodes such as the adventures of Giovanni Laureati, an Italian Jesuit sent to Fujian in an attempt to preserve what remained of Christianity there after the expulsion of the Dominicans for refusing the conditions of the piao, who made a triumphant but disputed entry into Fuzhou clad in dragon robes to signify his status.

There is, furthermore, much material which should provide historians with interesting insights into the intricate workings of the Chinese bureaucracy and glimpses into the politics of the Chinese court. The last months, especially, in this chronological account deal with a very murky episode: the succession crisis in which the Emperor deposed his nominated Heir Apparent and then restored him against strong opposition from the leading courtiers and the machinations <!-- PDF page 12 | book page 3 --> of the other older sons. All this is presented as seen by a privileged insider who witnesses activities inside the palace grounds and listens to the gossip of Kangxi’s courtiers.

These events have always been disputed because of the paucity of records, most probably ordered destroyed by the eventual winner of the succession dispute, the Emperor’s fourth son, Yinzhen, who became the Yongzheng Emperor in 1722. Stumpf gives an outsider/insider account of the crisis. He is a daily visitor to the Inner Palace although mostly kept waiting all day because of the breakdown of routine. He reports what he sees and what he is told. And his focus is upon the Emperor himself, his breakdown and withdrawal to his hunting lodge guarded by loyal troops, his horror at the factionalism and immoral behaviour of his sons, and his gradual return to health of mind and body.

There are some interesting glimpses into an ongoing conflict at the Court between European medicine represented by the Jesuit brother physicians, surgeons, and pharmacists and the Chinese doctors. This came to a head with the injury and eventual death of one of the Emperor’s favourite sons, his eighteenth, Yinxie, only seven years old. The clash of European and Chinese medical theories appears as an incidental but fascinating undertone. Kangxi appreciated practical European remedies. The land of the very house, the Beitang or French Church, in which Stumpf resided had been given earlier to the French Jesuits in gratitude for the quinine or “Jesuit bark” which they gave him for his recurrent malaria. On this occasion the court Jesuits relieved his melancholy by prescribing moderate doses of “Old Man’s Milk”, European wine, rather than large quantities of the kumis or fermented mare’s milk, prescribed by his Mongol advisers.

There is also some material of contemporary relevance on the Chinese involvement in the legitimation of the Dalai Lama and relations with Central Asia. Kangxi is a man with two faces, one Manchu, one Chinese. As a Manchu outsider he sympathizes with his European “foreign experts” who are threatened by Chinese xenophobia but he must appear to be part of, in fact the pinnacle of, the Chinese traditional order.

This volume offers many insights into the personality and behaviour of this very important early Qing dynasty Emperor who reigned from 1662 to 1722 and who was at the height of his powers in the period covered by the Acta but whose reign also exhibits many of the problems that plagued the dynasty: the problems of a smooth succession without primogeniture, the weight of government on the ruler as a highly centralized administration, the growing intrusion from outside—Russians by land to the Northwest, Western Europeans by sea in the Southeast and restless Central Asia chiefs; and the ever present resentment of Han Chinese against their Manchu rulers.

The main focus however is on the Chinese Rites Controversy, the argument nearly a century old by the time the Acta begin, about whether Christianity could or should accept, absorb, accommodate itself to Chinese customs and ritual practices, especially the cult of ancestors and rituals in honour of the cultural icon, Confucius. Allied to this was the “terms question”, whether Chinese terminology like tian (“Heaven”) could be used of the Christian God, and more broadly whether Christian missionaries should, so far as possible, become Chinese in dress, behaviour, and thinking. It was concern in Rome over these questions that had brought Maillard de Tournon to China. And because it was the Jesuits who had virtually founded the modern China mission, their many enemies in Europe had chosen this issue as a weapon in the struggle that was eventually to lead to the suppression of the Society of Jesus in the second half of the Eighteenth Century.

This struggle involved some colorful characters whose activities and writings are represented in this third volume of the Acta. Stumpf is remarkably reticent, although perhaps less so in this volume than in the earlier one where he had frequent and first-hand experience and relies on dramatic description, on the personality and motivation of the papal legate, Charles Thomas Maillard de Tournon, younger son of the Count of Nice who was Prime Minister of Piedmont. But his contradictory and inconsistent actions, his readiness to condemn and refusal to reconsider, his reliance on sycophants and outbursts of anger appear constantly in the letters from Macau recorded in the third volume of the Acta Pekinensia. We still badly need a full biography of de Tournon but the Acta will serve as a good introduction.

Other leading players also deserve a more detailed treatment than can be provided here, but the Acta give insights and telling detail that will aid future biographers, not least by providing a Beijing perspective.[^p13-3] There are the peripatetic missionaries who covered extraordinary distances in pursuit of often quixotic or still unexplained goals. Such are the German Lazarist Johannes Müllener and his journeys from Sichuan to Guangdong, to Huguang and back to Sichuan in his attempt to avoid the Sichuan officials but endangering missionaries and Chinese Christians enroute. Even more extraordinary are the travels of the Italian Franciscan Giovanni Buonaventura da Roma who in little over a year is mentioned by the author of the Acta in Nanjing, Guangzhou, Beijing, Guangzhou again, and Nanjing again. He had the unfortunate experience of carrying out the orders, as he understood them, of the Legate only to be excommunicated for disobedience.

The Bishop of Beijing, the Italian Franciscan Bernardino della Chiesa has been the subject of several shorter studies and his huge published correspondence figures in several volumes of the Sinica Franciscana, but the Acta Pekinensia sheds some new light on his activities. He appears as an irresolute and unfortunate figure, with little Chinese language, underfunded and excessively fearful of his Roman overseers, attempting to play the role of an Italian Lord Bishop in a foreign land.

One rather underestimated personage assumes a central role in the Acta where he is a kind of hero to the author and to his corespondents. This is Alvaro de Benavente, a Spanish Augustinian, missionary in the Philippines then China, finally Vicar Apostolic of Jiangxi and titular bishop of Ascalon. He had held high positions in his order in Madrid and Rome, was an experienced canon lawyer and prelate, and was outraged by what he saw as the high handed and disastrous decisions of the Legate. His writings show considerable knowledge of Chinese language and culture and his correspondence and the descriptions of his activities in the Acta might well, we hope, inspire a serious study of this key figure in the Chinese Rites Controversy. His death in Macau in March 1709, described in detail in the final volume of the Acta as he attempted to get to Rome to use his influence to counter the reports of the Legate and his party, was regarded by our author as a tragedy for Chinese Christianity.

Readers are referred to the introductions to the two previous volumes and to Stumpf’s pen pictures of them for background on many of the colourful characters whose activities are described here but perhaps the best approach to this remarkable work is simply to read and enjoy it. Its author spelled out in his note “To the Reader” which opens the Acta his fears that it might be confined to darkness but also his hopes that it might some day come to light “improved and developed”. We do not dare to claim that we have achieved this, but will be satisfied if after three centuries it is no longer hidden “within the prison walls of an archive” but reaches the wider audience it deserves.

As with the previous volumes we wish to thank the Ricci Institute of Macau, the Jesuit Historical Institute in Rome, and the Ricci Institute, now at Boston College, formerly at the University of San Francisco, for their encouragement and financial support. We also thank the transcribers of the text and the draft translators, especially Gerard J. Hughes s.j. who did the drafts for the bulk of this volume. And especially, we wish to thank Stephen Ford and Virginia M. Greeley for their indefatigable final editing of a very complex text.

Paul Rule Claudia von Collani
