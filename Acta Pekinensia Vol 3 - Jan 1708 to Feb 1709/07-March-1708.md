Matters that occurred this month can be classified more or less under three heads.[^p102-1] These will necessarily have to be kept separate in such a way that information relevant to each must be kept together without being too constrained by chronological order. So, the same dates will from time to time be repeated when several things of the 1st, 2nd, or 3rd kind were done on the one day. The record will focus primarily on what concerned the Beijing Jesuits; then on those things which related to Fr. Müllener, in connection with the Beijing Jesuits; and finally, the events surrounding the promotion of Fr. Visdelou to the See of Claudiopolis by the Lord Patriarch.[^p102-2] Since these have only a more remote connection to the Beijing Jesuits, they will be treated directly through the reports of others.

Concerning what happened in Beijing:

### 1 A document in favour of the missionaries was obtained from the Board of Mathematics and sent to the Supreme Military Prefects in the provinces

The reader will recall how the efforts of Fr. Grimaldi and others at the end of October 1707 succeeded in getting a ziwen document from the Board of Mathematics sent to the Supreme Military Prefects[^p102-3] of the provinces of Nanjing, Fujian, and Zhejiang. The hope was that this might provide some relief to the struggling Christians <!-- PDF page 103 | book page 94 --> and restrain the fury of the gentiles, which had been whipped up against the churches of God especially in those regions. This plan did do some good which was not to be despised though its effect was neither complete nor long-lasting. Missionaries from other provinces were asking for the same ziwen to be sent to their governors, etc.[^p103-4] But at the same time the governors were preparing their defence against such a document should one ever arrive. They had recourse to the Ministry of Rites, inquiring what line they should take about the permit which the Europeans had already been producing for some months. Hence the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon, Fr. Monteiro, and the Augustinian fathers from the province of Guangdong; the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing in the province of Shandong; and the Fathers in Huguang were all begging for that ziwen while at the same time in Beijing legal questions about the permits of the Europeans were being raised with the Libu by the governors of those same provinces.

### 2 The governors question the Ministry of Rites about the document

Here it must be understood that the Board of Mathematics did not send and indeed could not have sent its document to the mandarins in the provinces as if exercising authority over them. It had no standing in this case, other than as a mediator issuing friendly advice to the officials not to let the raging mob loose on the Europeans to whom the Emperor was showing favour at Court, and whom he wished to protect in the provinces by issuing the permit. In short, the governors could defer to that document if they found it acceptable, or ignore it if they did not. Extremely ancient practice gives the Libu (or Ministry of Rites) jurisdiction over all individuals and cases involving any kind of religion. For that reason, although it was inconvenient for the Europeans, the mandarins were doing nothing contrary to the law of the Chinese state in bringing their doubts before the Libu.[^p103-5] In the interests of <!-- PDF page 104 | book page 95 | manuscript page 858 --> brevity I will not describe here the memorials from all these individuals, written in Chinese, which the Libu assessors showed us; I intend to put them into an appendix. Everything was collected there which they said had been put to them by their subordinate officials in the towns, cities, and military posts: that an order had been issued by the Emperor in the usual manner through the Ministry of War and had been sent to the provinces and forwarded by Liang Nai, the zongdu of the provinces of Zhejiang and Fujian, and that Europeans with no certificate were to be expelled from China.[^p104-6] The assessors were also agreed on this point: that the permit produced by those Europeans who did have one was unknown anywhere in the empire, and when questioned about who had sealed and signed it, the Europeans replied that they did not know. They could produce no better evidence to support its genuineness except to say that they had asked the Emperor for the certificate as he made his way through the southern provinces, and it had been given to them by the Great Prince. Otherwise, they would not be allowed to continue to reside in China in peace. This being the case, the supreme military and civil officials considered themselves obliged to bring the case before the Ministry of Rites in their own name and in the name of their subordinates, requesting formal guidance on the substance | and genuineness of the certificate.

### 3 The response of the Ministry of Rites is hardly favourable

The Ministry of Rites responded that they had had no previous knowledge of the alleged certificate or of the whole affair; the officials should make it their business with all diligence to investigate the Europeans and the certificate from which they wish to benefit; then they should have further recourse to the Neiwu fu— that is, to the Board of the Inner City, or the Palace Ministers.[^p104-7] When the Fathers found out about this dry response, Fr. Grimaldi played the main part in dealing with the situation. He sent another siwen from the Board of Mathematics to the Libu to inform it of the whole train of events in the case, from the issuing of the imperial decree in the eleventh month of the previous year until the present moment, together with a separate explanation of the permit which His Gracious Majesty had given to some people to enable them to continue to remain in his Empire. This did not lead to any more favourable result, despite the fact that these memoranda had, at considerable expense, been accompanied by the customary presents. The Ministry of Rites showed itself openly obdurate and sent nothing more by way of response than to express its deep regret that <!-- PDF page 105 | book page 96 --> the Emperor in this matter had departed from precedent, and to say that there were good grounds for expecting that complaints from the high officials would multiply to the point at which the Emperor would be forced to put European Religious under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Rites, which, after all, by ancient law was responsible for all immigrant and native-born priests.

All this happened in February, but led straight into the events of March, as will presently appear. In the meantime, several misfortunes befell our holy religion, and from all quarters ever more distressing news began to wing its urgent way to Beijing, asking for our help. When the Viceroy of Shandong received the reply from Libu mentioned above, he promptly had recourse to the four Ministers of the Inner Palace.[^p105-8]

March 1st. His memorial arrived in Beijing, as one might expect since his province is nearby.

March 2nd. The Ministers or Marshals of the Royal Household informed the Emperor’s Firstborn Son. He replied that he was snowed under with business because of His Majesty’s imminent departure; His Majesty would be back from the Villa to the city on the following day, and would find a time when these matters could be raised. By way of preparing an agenda, he asked for the names of everyone who had received a permit up until that date to be collected from the Palace records, so that a list of them could be given to the Ministry of Rites.

### 4 Hence the fears of the Jesuits

One of our Fathers was present when the Prince gave this order in the presence of the Poiyambis (the Marshals of the Household); he informed the other Fathers who were all struck by a double apprehension. Firstly, it seemed as if this time the missionaries were going to be placed under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Rites, as are the bonzes and the worthless filth of ministers of the devil. Secondly, it was now hard to see any way in which we could extricate the missionaries from the corruption of the mandarins, unless the Libu itself should lay down what should and should not be done according to tradition. But our view was that this was unlikely to be allowed because of the many problems it would cause. Everyone in China knows that there have already been problems, even if not everyone <!-- PDF page 106 | book page 97 | manuscript page 859 --> wants that to be believed in Europe. Those people would prefer that the persecution be taken as a figment of the Jesuit imagination rather than themselves to be regarded as the ones whose fault it was that the tranquillity of the mission has degenerated into an internecine shamble. Much as I would prefer not to do so, I am compelled here to record some of the sad situations of the missionaries. I intend to provide later a more detailed account of events that happened in Beijing during this same month.

I think this is the third time I have written to Your Reverence about the letter of the Viceroy and here and now I will try | to conclude my account of the matter which has embroiled the whole of Xialu[^p106-10] (part of the province of Nanjing), and I fear will end up in all of us being expelled from here by order of the zongdu. ### 6 An altar thrown down, a tablet containing praise of the Christian Law burned

Fr. Tomás Hortiz, Vice-Provincial of the Augustinians, told Fr. Antoine Thomas (in the letter of 12 November which is given in full in its proper place)[^p108-23] that the mandarin of the city of Shaojing in another of his churches had burnt the tablet containing the proclamation of our Holy Law, and had thrown down the altar with its holy images, etc. The Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon relates the same event when writing to Fr. Antoine on 28 January 1708, saying in a postscript:[^p108-24]

Recently, near the city of Shaojing fu sentence was pronounced by the zhifu and the zhixian,[^p108-25] at the request of some pagans, that a church of the Augustinians should be destroyed; it was small, but they had built it with no expense spared. The priests there are taking the matter to the daoye, but this will perhaps be no use.[^p108-26] So if Your Reverence could get Fr. Grimaldi (to whom I greatly commend the matter) to have that same qintian jian[^p108-27] warning sent to those mandarins as was sent to Fujian, Zhejiang, and Jiangxi, Your Reverence will perform a service to God, and the infidels will not hurl abuse at our Law. None of the missionaries have had so much suffering to bear as the Augustinians in Shaojing.

This agrees with the original text. I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary

The Augustinians have been kept under arrest from then until now, shut up in the house as Fr. Hortiz described in his letter of 12 November and as will become clear from what follows.

That a warning from the Tribunal of Mathematics could not have had any impact in this case is clear from a letter of Fr. Monteiro, written on 23 February, in which he wrote:[^p108-28]

It is difficult to make any changes in personnel due to the hostility of this Viceroy, who on the occasion of his shengri[^p108-29] (his birthday) last (Chinese) year gave his consent that the zhixian (the magistrates of cities and towns) might <!-- PDF page 109 | book page 100 | manuscript page 860 --> occupy the churches. So, he will never | listen to a European, as we learned a few days ago from the efforts I made to prevent the Viceroy from occupying a church. To this he replied that someone had suggested to him—and it was a person very close to him—that he should let the mandarin of the town act. I beg you in the current situation not to involve any Chinese, which will just complicate matters, and I have no hopes of a favourable outcome. To a certain Christian who presented a petition in Shaojing in defence of the Church of the Augustinians the zhixian of the place gave 20 strokes of the stick despite his referring to the zhiyi of the King, and the wen of the qintian jian that Your Reverence got sent to Fujian and Zhejiang. And the church, I believe, was despoiled at that very hour despite the fact that I intervened on its behalf to the zhixian and the zhifu themselves in the name of the Fathers (Gonzaga and Roman Hinderer, who were passing through there having been called by the Emperor).

### 7 A sacred building of the Society taken over by mandarins

Again, the same correspondent on the same day in the same letter writes as follows about a Jesuit church which was taken over by mandarins:[^p109-30]

The Reverend Fathers who remain in the province with the piao cannot do enough to preserve their churches in these times since the current lot of mandarins are disaffected from our Holy Law and ourselves. Now we have lost the church of Xinhui xian[^p109-31] (in the province of Guangdong), which has been occupied by a certain zhixian who has turned it into an yixue[^p109-32] (a school) and thrown out the sacred images and everything which was in it and sent them to his yamen[^p109-33] (or tribunal) to take them to pieces and dispose of them as he wishes …. We five[^p109-34] can do so little that we doubt whether we can preserve this church in which we dwell; that of Foshan is in danger in the case that Fr. Capacci should be expelled with those who do not have a piao, unless from here we can persuade (the mandarins) that that church belongs to Fr. Osorio who is in Macau.

But this is enough for the time being until several other matters are explained to the reader. Meanwhile, the Beijing Fathers for the reasons given above were intent on doing something about it, when the departure of the Emperor was announced for March 8th …

On March 6th they all went to the Villa on the pretext of inquiring after His Majesty’s health as he left on his journey. They were received by the Prince and gave that as the motive for their coming, but went on to remind the Prince that four days previously he had replied to the Poiyambis, saying, in reply to the question put to him by the Viceroys about the permit held by the Europeans, that he would pass on the matter for the sake of maintaining public order. The Fathers begged that, if perchance this had not been done, he might be gracious enough to think about how little protection it would afford the missionaries were the Ministry of Rites to be allowed even the smallest chink through which to get its hands on them. The Prince replied that the matter had been brought to his father’s attention, and he had said it would be more satisfactory if the Europeans resident in China should remain within the jurisdiction of the Imperial Household Department, and that a reply was to be sent by it to satisfy the Viceroys. The Fathers did not take the Prince wholly at his word, having learnt from long experience that, however well disposed he might appear to this or that individual so as to promote his own ends, he absolutely would not do anything to promote the spread and peace of the Holy Religion. For that reason, when Fr. Pereira had the good fortune | to be admitted to the imperial presence that day, he repeated the same point, adding the information the

### 8 The Emperor had once and for all definitely decided to expel the Europeans from China if the Supreme Pontiff decided against the Chinese rites

March 8th. The Emperor went to the suburban game park called the Haizi[^p111-36] to engage in a little hunting; then three days later he went to the Bazhou[^p111-37] wetlands in order that he and his party could enjoy some shooting for water-fowl. This activity was extended to almost a month every year. While they were on this trip, the Crown Prince said to Fr. Parrenin, in the presence of Br. Paramino, that his father had once and for all definitely decided to expel all the Europeans from China if ever the Pope should decide against the Chinese rites.[^p111-38]

March 9th. The Muscovites finally obtained permission to open their doors, since up until now they had observed the custom of this country and their doors had stayed shut since the day they arrived. This year there was a rather longer delay than usual. The occasion for gaining permission was that on the 6th of this month the Emperor asked Fr. Pereira if the Muscovites had brought any European news, and the Father had replied that he had not yet dared go near them since they had been kept locked up right up until that day. The Emperor was not a little astonished to hear this, and said, “Just you wait a little while”; and after two days had passed he ordered the doors to be immediately opened.

March 10th. Fr. Kilian sent a letter to Fr. Frossoloni which I gave in its entirety under the month of July when I narrated the events surrounding the publication of the book by the Christians of Hangzhou.[^p111-39] The letter is dated March 7th, but it was handed to the courier only today, after it had been read and approved by the other Fathers in Beijing. This letter arrived in Guangzhou, and from Guangzhou had been forwarded to Macau through a merchant who was going there. It was lost on the journey because the merchant was attacked and robbed. When we heard about this in letters from the people in Guangzhou, <!-- PDF page 112 | book page 103 --> Fr. Kilian sent a second copy in July of this year 1708. But everything was based on a misunderstanding. Fr. Frossoloni had secretly embarked from Macau for the Indian Coast, intending to go on to Europe. Nobody in Guangzhou knew this, still less anyone in Beijing, until August 1708, when letters written by him from the Coast appeared in Guangzhou.[^p112-40]

### 9 The Bishop of Beijing’s letter to Fr. Castner

March 12th. The Lord Bishop of Beijing was kind enough to write to Fr. Castner, apologising for the earlier refusal of faculties to administer [the sacraments]. It runs as follows:[^p112-41]

When I saw your very welcome letter yesterday, I was delighted at what I take to be your full return to health, since I had previously heard from Very Reverend Fr. Antoine Thomas that you had had a relapse. Your Reverence reported you had been consoled by the letter I wrote to the Very Reverend Vice-Provincial, about permission to administer [the sacraments] as you had wished, although perhaps you had been more than a little saddened earlier; but you must believe me, your sadness was nothing in comparison to my own, since I would have very much preferred to agree to the requests made earlier, but reasons which you are aware of made it impossible for me to do otherwise. That was wonderful news in your letter, about the Sacred Congregation confirming that the excommunication of the Capuchin Fathers was null and void.[^p112-42] Even more wonderful and sweet is the news brought from Rome about the controversies which have plagued this mission for such a long time, in particular the statement from the Holy Father to the effect that the rumours which have been circulating about your practice siano ciarle—are no more than empty chatter43—and that he is not about to allow anything to happen contrary to the decree of Alexander vii.[^p112-44] If that really is true, then I hope in the goodness of God that he will not <!-- PDF page 113 | book page 104 | manuscript page 862 --> allow the China mission to perish. I salute Your Reverence from my heart, and embrace you in the Lord, commending myself to your prayers and sacrifices.

Linqing zhou, 12 March 1708.

### 11 A letter of Fr. Kilian to the Bishop of Ascalon

My Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord,

I am happy to accept your forgiveness, so kindly offered, for whatever fault I committed on 4 January 1708, nor will I make any mention of those matters in future since the balm of your warm heart bids me bury them once and for all. I have no doubt that Your Lordship knew of the petition we had made in Your Lordship’s name to the Emperor; we had done this before the arrival in Beijing of the letter in which Your Lordship expressed your unwillingness to make any petition on your own account, given that the Lord Patriarch had not responded to your appeal. He refused to do so on 27 May 1707 in Guangzhou, from where his refusal was sent to Nan’an. From Nan’an Your Lordship sent your own letter to Guangzhou so that it could be forwarded to us in Beijing. It could not be expected to arrive in June or July, largely because, to go by what all the missionaries were saying at the time, the mandarins were closing off all exchange of letters between Europeans. For the rest, Your Lordship seems to me to have generously given me permission to respond. For you wrote, as Saint Augustine your patriarch put it, that Truth alone should shine forth and be clearly apparent;[^p114-50] for you had set your mind not on winning the argument but on the right government of others in the Lord. I therefore confidently presume that I may write a few words of response, which might make some contribution to Your Lordship’s aim.

I had written a short while ago that by the words “I have followed” Your Lordship was not making any undertaking about the future; Your Lordship recognized that in the obvious sense that was correct, and that the Bishop of Beijing also wished it to be understood in this context when he produced his own interpretation of those words—not indeed when he spoke them in accepting his permit from the Emperor, but when the Lord Patriarch, after promulgating his <!-- PDF page 115 | book page 106 | manuscript page 863 --> decree, contested the use of these words. However, I do not wish to rest my whole case on this authority. My key point is that in no way, when in the month of August I took upon myself the honour of petitioning His Majesty on your behalf, did the Emperor understand the words “I have followed” to have any reference to the future. The reason is that the Emperor in August understood them to mean nothing more than he understood them to mean in April, May, and June, when almost 40 missionaries petitioned for and accepted the permit. At that time he neither took nor could have taken them to have any future reference, | as if the missionaries would ever just ignore pontifical decrees and allow or actively fight for the Chinese Rites.

### 12 The opinion of the Fathers and their willingness to obey the papal decrees

Nobody could still have any doubt about this once they knew how clearly and energetically Frs. Monteiro, Pereira,[^p115-51] Ferreira,[^p115-52] de Sousa,[^p115-53] de Matta,[^p115-54] Bouvet, and Jartoux, seven distinguished witnesses to Christ, protested before the Emperor, through the Prince, the obligation which they and all the others had to obey the definitions of the Holy See, and their readiness to shed their blood in order to carry out that obligation. Someone might perhaps maintain that in the Emperor’s mind those words, while indeed not constituting a “promise to follow” contrary to a definition of Holy Church if such were to be proclaimed, might have seemed to express a “promise to follow” contrary to the Patriarch’s mandate until a definition of the Holy See should arrive.

### 13 The Patriarch did nothing to explain what was in his mind and the meaning of his decree to those who asked him

I say that the Emperor’s words, which in the present situation are most unwelcome, should be interpreted narrowly, exactly as they sound. What they say is no more than that until such time as a response comes from the Pope, the missionaries should not preach against the rites. So the words I have followed, if they do refer to the future according to the Emperor’s intention, mean that I will not preach against the Chinese Rites until I shall be certain that a papal definition has reached China. Whether that is or is not permissible is up to the author of the decree to make clear. If he does not give an explanation, then according to the law it is he who incurs the <!-- PDF page 116 | book page 107 --> responsibility for his decree not being completely observed as he intended. This is all the more so because the Patriarch was consulted about the interpretation of his decree by 24 missionaries, and for 10 months had never got round to giving an explanation. Moreover, when a promise was made precisely about not preaching, it seems that to make such a promise was allowed by the Patriarch, since he had nowhere imposed upon the missionaries the duty of promulgating his decree to the Christians. For what else is the promulgation of the Patriarch’s decree if not an instance of preaching against the rites? But if the promulgation was not ordered, then it cannot be said that the regulations were proclaimed, and someone who promises not to preach against the rites cannot be said to violate anyone’s order.

### 14 The missionaries are not bound to promulgate the Lord Patriarch’s decree

We wrote on this point to our Illustrious Ordinary since we heard through Fr. Frusinone (or Frossoloni) that some missionaries were worried that they might have incurred automatic censures in that they had postponed preaching against the rites or the promulgation of the Patriarch’s decree, whereas he wished everyone to be bound by it by the word teach which is expressly in the decree.[^p116-55] But the Illustrious Bishop of Beijing replied to us that that word does not in itself oblige the missionaries to teach, preach, or promulgate unless they have an opportunity to do so without any danger. Until an official reply comes from Rome, there is no opportunity, nor is there an absence of manifest and serious danger. Point made.

So, with the understanding explained so far, some missionaries remain in their churches, waiting for the opportunity and the absence of danger, which they hope will come when the definition of the Holy See arrives, and put its full authority behind the information provided by the Emperor. But they administer the sacraments, they teach what it is essential to know and necessary as a matter of obligation. It is now more than a year since the decree was issued, and the person who is in a position to contradict this interpretation if he so chose chooses not to do so.

The words xiudao de are understood by some people in a way which is hardly correct.[^p116-56] The Emperor had been sufficiently instructed by the seven witnesses <!-- PDF page 117 | book page 108 | manuscript page 864 --> and by the others mentioned above that the missionaries were afraid that if they accepted the permit after all that they had promised in accordance with the decree, they would be suspended from the work of missionary by the Lord Patriarch. So, the Emperor asked whether in this new situation they wished to leave China at once without waiting for his Majesty’s case concerning vassals and missionaries to be heard in Rome, or whether they would prefer to put up with being suspended by the Lord Patriarch and not actively conduct their missionary work for a while. That is what the phrase “whether they would remain meanwhile as simple religious” means. Who will say it is wrong to do that? If the missionaries were in Rome in their convents, they could take refuge in Tusculo, intending to come back the next day.[^p117-57] But whether to leave China because the case is pending, and undertake journeys of twenty thousand leagues, more or less, and with the harm to so many souls, that is surely a much more difficult decision. | It is beyond dispute that if it is permissible for someone to desert the mission because the Emperor has forbidden people to preach against the Chinese rites, it will be all the more permissible for them to remain for a while notwithstanding the impediment we have mentioned, since, as has been proved, the Patriarch has not so far obliged anyone to preach against the rites; fear that he some time or other might do so is not a good reason for deserting the mission. The argument drawn by some from the jottings of Navarrete is no use.[^p117-58] I doubt if Your Illustrious Reverence has been shown either the question put by Fr. Navarrete or the answer of the assessors exactly as it was, so I have decided to quote it here. The 6th Question: Whether missionaries, when questioned by a tyrant whether they wish to remain in his kingdom provided that they swear not to preach the law of the Gospel, may licitly agree, promise, and accept such a condition? The response is that they may not so promise.[^p117-59]

### 15 The just complaints of the Emperor against the Lord Patriarch

Nobody thinks that the present Emperor Kangxi is a tyrant, or that he requires missionaries to swear an oath that they will refuse to preach the Gospel, as we saw above. He is a peace-loving prince who favours the divine law, who wishes it to be spread throughout his empire and to be accepted by his subjects. Everyone <!-- PDF page 118 | book page 109 --> knows how with almost Christian observance he had recourse to the Holy See, and is respectfully offering imperial interpretations, backed by the highest authorities, in favour of the rites of the empire, which have already been accepted by Alexander vii, and so far have never been prohibited by decree of any other Pope. He knows that to prohibit them would reverse the progress of the Gospel. The Emperor chose to have recourse in this way because he saw at first hand the representative of the Holy See exceeding the powers of his office, rejecting interpretations of the rites by qualified judges and accepting those which were biased, listening to people chattering at street corners and ignoring the views of the learned, paying no attention to the explanations offered by the Emperor, and stubbornly excluding authoritative interpretations. He refused to send the Emperor’s views to the Holy Pontiff when he was ordered to do so. None of this could be of help in answering the questions raised by Navarrete or drawing up a Roman response to those questions.

Your Lordship asks what I think of the way the Patriarch arranged for Fr. Buonaventura to be treated. You do not inquire, as if you do not know, how far such an action is legitimate and consistent. I would nevertheless answer those questions by referring as I hope Your Lordship may to the Tonkin case, in which a cleric accused his fellow clerics who were released by the Tribunal in 1706 once they had sworn an oath that in future they would not preach. That they did swear has been reported in writing by reliable men … I do not reproduce the details of the document here, since I presume that the whole case can be found in Guangzhou.[^p118-60]

To conclude, I pray Your Lordship not to decide either that he must flee from China, or that he has to come to Beijing. For according to what the Emperor decreed on 3 December 1707, the mandarins cannot prevent anyone who has decided to come to the capital to obtain a permit, nor arrest anyone who has decided to leave China. My words do not suggest there is any special urgency for Your Lordship; you may convalesce for as long as you need to, by which time the answers from Rome will not be much longer in coming. Whatever Your Lordship decides—to stay in China or to go to Rome via Manila—I have every confidence that Your Lordship will choose with the good of the mission in mind, knowing how sincerely you have devoted your whole self to the service of the Chinese church for so long, and even now in your old age will continue to dedicate yourself to it. It remains only to say that I shall offer very frequent prayers and sacrifices that Divine Providence may guide your decision, and strengthen you in carrying it out. On my knees I beg for your holy blessing.

Three points here are worth noting:

### 27 An observation about the dating of the Lord Patriarch’s letter

(1) Fr. Buonaventura ponders the implication of the date on which the Patriarch received his letter and his certain knowledge that the Father at that time, October 21st, was staying in Guangzhou, together with the date of the Lord Patriarch’s reply, which was given as October 23rd with the address “To Nanjing,” when, while he wrote, he certainly was not ignorant of the fact that Fr. Buonaventura was no longer there. This reflection, which is founded on fact, prompts a further reflection: it has not infrequently been noticed that a similar practice has characterised other letters of the Apostolic Visitor, and also his official records, and even his legal documents; dates are given which may or may not conform to the real dates. The reader will perhaps recall similar observations in connection with the Guo Zhaojin contract, or concerning the reply of Bishop Maigrot to the dossier of our procurator about the truth of the imperial declaration, or in his reply to the Father’s representation about the Patriarch’s prohibition, issued to forbid Fr. J.F. Gerbillon from holding a disputation in the presence of the Emperor, etc. Whether these mistakes were intentional or accidental, or to whom in the end they are to be attributed, is not completely clear: They may not be the fault of the Lord Patriarch himself, but the result of the inexperience of those to whom he has entrusted his chancery, auditory, secretariat, and executive. It may be that they, as some say, actually believe that the Lord Patriarch can do whatever he likes and even make laws, and is not restricted in <!-- PDF page 130 | book page 121 --> the fullness of his powers which here in the East renders him much like that the Pope himself. So it makes no difference whether the legal provisions and the details of the dates are correct or not, provided they make other people observe them. Perhaps it also comes from the fact that the Most Excellent Lord writes his letters and minutes using a random choice of amanuenses, sometimes a layman whom he calls his secretary, at other times a priest whom he calls his chancellor, at still other times using a Religious such as Fr. Frossoloni, and so on. As a result, when different people dealing with different cases, all of them working inaccurately and with little coordination, are trying to draft things in proper legal form, it can easily happen that one person unwittingly prejudices another’s case, putting one date instead of another, etc, thus undermining the authority of the Holy Apostolic Visitation. For although the Lord in Luke 16 praises the person who knows and acts like this, he did not instruct the college of the apostles to carry this practice into the Church, nor is anything of the kind to be found in canonical documents of the church, old or new.[^p130-91]

For the reader to read this letter with profit, some things have to be noted.

### 33 Fr. Porquet gets permission from the Viceroy to go to Beijing

(1) Fr. Porquet had been trying for almost half a year to obtain permission from the Governor of the Province to go to Beijing to receive the permit. Finally, the Viceroy did give him leave, probably hoping that he would be prevented by the zongdu as the Franciscans had been. But Fr. Porquet did leave, relying solely on the Viceroy’s permission. Rumour had it that he was supposed to have been brought back by some agents who were sent after him, but in the event he was not. However, he was detained by the tax inspectors in Shaozhou, and forced to hang on there for considerably more than a month until the mandarins had received a reply from the Viceroy and the zongdu, giving permission in writing. Anyone wishing further information on this should inquire from the Procurator of the French Fathers, to whom Fr. Porquet sent a detailed account.

### 34 A false rumour is concocted against Fr. Monteiro

(2) That it was not only the Beijing Jesuits who were liable to be slandered is shown by the malicious allegation made against Fr. Monteiro. This confirms the impression that the strict theology of certain clerics fosters the strongest suspicions about the men of the Society of Jesus; then quite unscrupulously they parade their suspicions as though they were facts.[^p137-119] Fr. Monteiro, who laid his life on the line in this same month last year in an attempt to keep the Lord Patriarch’s instruction, and indeed to keep the Lord Patriarch himself and his priests in China, now, for no reason at all, is said to be expelling the clerics!

### 35 Information about Fr. Fontanilla

(3) Fr. Fontanilla showed his ingenuity in other things which he did in China, and particularly in the paper he wrote in his spare time about the Emperor Kangxi’s two intentions: one of them effective, to keep the Europeans in China; the other ineffective, to not allow the Europeans to preach against Chinese laws and rites. However, Fr. Fontanilla found that the mandarins were just as ingenious. They made no reply to the request he had presented, but wrote to the city in which he said he had lived, inquiring whether people there could find anything being said against him. They found that it was denied that a European called Fan Jiguo lived there, so without any further investigation they forced the good Father to leave China.

<!-- PDF page 138 | book page 129 | manuscript page 875 -->

Europeans are too easily taken in when they have successfully and easily carried out some transactions with the mandarins. They put this down to Chinese dim-wittedness and imagine they can carry on without problems. Those in government office in China are every bit as wise as their European counterparts, and when wisdom degenerates into sharp practice, they are superior. They are deceived when it suits them to be deceived, and when they are not, they go to the limit in scrutinising the very last comma in a case. In this they are very praiseworthy, since they do not let their feelings be easily seen; and if they find one thing which is enough to twist to their advantage, they do not bother looking for a second or a third.

### 36 It is a crime in China to change one’s name

In the present case, I am not getting at Fr. Fontanilla for changing his name, and I do not know why he did so. But though it is easy to do it, and in European courts it is not a serious matter unless however it relates to the matter in hand, in China it is regarded as a criminal act to change one’s name, and leads to serious complications. Just recently we have seen what happened to our own Fr. Melon and some others. Through no fault or complicity of theirs, a single letter of their names was altered on the imperial permit. So when it came to Fr. Fontanilla’s petition to be allowed to go back to his church without a piao, and continue to live there in peace as he had done before, as though no imperial edict about expelling from the Empire any Europeans who did not have a certificate had ever been published, the petition was based on nothing but his own self assurance, which put into the heads of himself and others the idea about the Emperor’s two intentions.

### 37 The Franciscans’ mistake in repeating that they had been summoned to Beijing by the Board of Mathematics

(3)[^p138-120] The Franciscans can neither be blamed nor excused for claiming in their application | that they had been summoned to Beijing by the Board of Mathematics, even though they had no letter other than the one from Frs. Thomas and Gerbillon, and had heard absolutely nothing from the Board. This was completely due to a mistake which had been made long ago whereby the missionaries in the provinces were convinced that all Europeans fell under the jurisdiction of this Board. Sometimes a statement of Fr. Grimaldi’s is used, which was applied to the missionaries in the provinces; Fr. Grimaldi did not however say even by implication that they belonged to the Board, but rather that they are reckoned <!-- PDF page 139 | book page 130 --> among the Board’s clients. The matter does not need further explanation here, since their mistake has been sufficiently brought home to them by the unjust authority of the mandarins when they ordered the letter, which summoned them to be translated and was found to contain no reference at all to the Board’s authority. What can definitely be said about these Franciscans (and this is important) is that they were ordered by the mandarins to furnish authorisation from their own commissary and from the city of Macau. The authorisation of the commissary was brought to the mandarins, but those Franciscans refused to seek authorisation from the city of Macau when a dossier was offered to the mandarins making a public plea on the matter.

### 38 The Bishop of Ascalon’s zeal and magnanimity of mind

(4) In the Illustrious Lord Bishop of Ascalon there shines out a matchless zeal for justice and for acknowledging the truth, for the good of the souls of the Chinese. Here is a man in his sixties, afflicted by asthma and other bodily infirmities, who decided for the fourth time to embark on an extremely long journey across the western ocean, as though it were not enough to have travelled that long way three times. Completely undaunted as he is by the dangers and toils, it can only be the Spirit who burns in his aged breast. Others may argue that they have borne no less for the sake of the Chinese mission, or are surely about to do so; I will not argue, for as long as the human flesh clouds the mind, it is not so easy to distinguish between true and wrong-headed zeal, or between a sincere ardour of spirit and conflicting passions by which this or that person may be disturbed. The only test is that given in Matthew Ch.7 where it is said “by their fruits you shall know them.” Let the fruits of the zealots be compared with those which the Bishop of Ascalon has produced during his twenty and more years in China and taken to heaven, and then let the discerning world pass judgement.

### 39 Harassment is legitimately and correctly bought off by gifts

(5) The reader will recall the letter which had to be written by the Rev. Fr. Provincial of the Augustinians, Fr. Tomás Hortiz, to Fr. Tomás Pereira concerning the Augustinian church which had just been destroyed. The letter was finally sent on March 10th and was very late in arriving in Beijing; we shall recopy it in full in its proper month. If the reader cannot help smiling at the clock and lapdogs which were sought in order to redeem the church from the treasurer, they should remember that even in Europe, according to the ecclesiastical histories, if not for churches at least for good churchmen, silver was quite often given and accepted, as were horses, dogs, hunting rights, and so on. And in our own day it does not provoke a laugh when an annual payment <!-- PDF page 140 | book page 131 | manuscript page 876 --> of a white mare is made, because the king made off with one from Rome.[^p140-121] The reader should also notice that the expenses which the Jesuits have generously met over a hundred years in order to preserve their churches and those of others have not been insignificant.

While the events of March 23rd which I have just mentioned were being described in Guangzhou, a reply was being prepared in Beijing to the earlier letters from the people in Guangzhou, containing material which for the most part which would no longer have been worth mentioning if, on March 24th, the content of the letters posted on the previous day in Guangzhou had been known in the capital. The distance involved was the cause of many mistakes on both sides, and meant that enormous efforts were often a waste of time. I pray that the reader will not find it irritating to read about the concerns of the Jesuits in Beijing in a letter from Fr. Kilian to Fr. Monteiro, dated March 24th.

### 40 A letter of Fr. Kilian to Fr. Monteiro

Reverend Father in Christ,

If in my letters I do not very often ask after Your Reverence’s health, please believe that it is because I no less suppose you are well than that you say your prayers, and that being in good health you carry on the good work for a long, long time to come. ### 41 The harm done by being careless about accuracy in letters and records

Reports of several serious cases in Guangdong, Shaoxing, etc. have been sent to us, but without any pingqu (attestation)[^p140-122] or any mention of the name or the rank of the mandarin, nor when it happened, nor any of the other circumstances. What can we do, when matters are referred to us so incompletely? Several months ago, when we wanted to get the Prince to ask the Emperor that Europeans no longer be prevented from coming to obtain <!-- PDF page 141 | book page 132 --> the permit, the Prince’s come-back was quite devastating, pointing out that he could not possibly bother his father with such matters when we had not a single relevant document other than some fragmentary letters from Europeans!

What Your Reverence writes about the three views, or better, the three suspicions of the people in Guangzhou would need volumes if anyone were to try to shed light in the darkness. To be sure, large volumes have indeed been written on the subject, but they would never be enough to satisfy the people who want to waste their efforts and then an insistent desire to reinvestigate the investigations which have been conducted against the Jesuits. If there were such researchers in Manila or Goa or anywhere else except China, perhaps there would be no sin in their whiling away hours and days in such inquiries and conversations. But in fact they are deceiving themselves: for what use is there in spending weeks and months in such empty and useless conjectures? Should they not be getting on with their business and the work of the mission? One has to take a realistic view of things, and adopt the approach which is needed to deal with actual events, rather than weaving a chain of conjectural scenarios based on something which has never been known for sure, or which might conceivably happen some day.

If the Lord Patriarch allows it, and people have set their heart on trying to remain in the mission, then they should ask permission to go [to Beijing] and start on the journey without waiting for anything else. If permission is refused, or they are stopped on the way, they should request that the reason be put in writing for them. Do they not have the example of almost 50 missionaries who have received the permit? Anyway, the mandarins at every stage along the journey have seen a permit. If the mandarins refuse to put their objection in writing, let them have recourse to the Prince or to the Emperor, by sending us a joint petition, and then something can be done.

### 42 The Beijing Jesuits are unjustly accused of not being helpful enough to the missionaries

Your Reverence says that what comes from Beijing is not clear. I thought that I had sent enough lamps and flashes of lightning to illuminate you all, and what I wrote was written with the knowledge and approval of the other Fathers. But in Guangzhou they do not just want Fr. Kilian to write; in addition, they want this person, and that other person, and someone else as well, to write to them, ask them questions, send them invitations, smooth their path for them, and send them something for the journey. They really do not know what they want; but the Jesuits in Beijing would be delighted to do anything in their power to provide it. The more some of them ask for, the less possible it is in the circumstances. We asked them to receive the piao for weighty reasons; using our best judgement, we asked for introductions <!-- PDF page 142 | book page 133 | manuscript page 877 --> on behalf of the Bishop of Ascalon. Dear God, how disastrously that turned out! He asks us to undo what has been done, etc.; but who now could even whisper that to the Emperor? Even if we could do everything, and we did everything they dream of asking for, they would still find reasons to find fault and opportunities to make accusations against us. For it seems that the ones who talk about the Emperor having two intentions themselves nourish as many contradictions and more in their own breasts: they want to leave, but ask leave to stay; on the other hand, some of them are glad to stay when some people suppose that they are going to leave. It is absolutely impossible for us to push any of these matters any further with the Emperor since to ask again that the mandarins should not delay those who want to come will be interpreted by others as requesting that those who do not want to come should be expelled. If the mandarins’ behaviour is confused, | any fault there is should be laid at the door of those who have produced this unfortunate effect. If on this occasion there really is a way out and, notwithstanding the Emperor’s edict, the missionaries can remain, we cannot be sad at that—we should rejoice. I beg God to inspire everyone with a reasonable respect, and remove from their hearts the spirit of suspicion and mutual rivalry, which is like a hell from which alone has emerged all that has gone wrong between missionaries since the 80s.

### 43 The Lord Patriarch has mistakenly persuaded himself that the Emperor does not care whether the Chinese rites are observed or not

They write that the Lord Patriarch is saying:

(1) That the Emperor certainly has not staked his authority on observance of the Chinese Rites

(2) That he claims he himself has legal witness statements against the Beijing Jesuits

(3) That he sent Fr. Müllener to receive the permit in Beijing, and then, if it were possible, to be in charge of the residence in Nanjing.

In the first of these he is being consistent, since from the beginning, either because it was his own view or because he had been persuaded by others, he has been saying that in China people have a take it or leave it attitude towards the rites, much as people in Amsterdam have, to differences in religious belief. Fr. Appiani displays just the same mentality, so much so that when on 15 June 1706 the mandarin Henkama had a long conversation asking him to talk to the Lord Patriarch and stop him trying to undermine the rites, he said quite bluntly: “What does the Emperor care whether the Christians observe the rites or not?” The mandarin rebutted this suggestion most vehemently, pointing out that were the Christians and Europeans to be accused by the supreme prefects of violating the laws, or if the people demanded that they be punished for violating their observance, the Emperor would have neither the inclination nor the power to defend them.

<!-- PDF page 143 | book page 134 | manuscript page 878 -->

If previously anyone had thought the Emperor would put up with a violation of the rites without complaining, since there had not as yet been any public outcry on the subject, are we to still to take the same view of the Emperor’s mind now, when he has published decrees declaring his own subjects to be rebels if they act contrary to the traditional rites; when he has expelled some Europeans, holds others captive, and when he threatens others with expulsion or death? Of course, a gentile Chinese Emperor is going to abandon all these views without undermining his authority in his own kingdom, where, as in the kingdom of the Medes, a royal word may never be withdrawn! If an apostolic commissary cannot, without besmirching his honour, suspend a rule when the terrible sorrows we have experienced cry out loud, how inopportune that rule was; must we just accept it simply because it has been promulgated and bemoan the destruction of the mission rather than take even one step back from the brink? If they believe that the authority of the Apostolic Commissary is at stake, are they going to deny that the majesty of the Emperor is also at stake? If neither can give way, then inevitably one will be crushed by the other, as long ago in the month of August 1706 the mandarin Zhao predicted to the Patriarch.

What is the point of writing all this to Your Reverence? You yourself gave my pen the opportunity, and it has boiled over at greater length than is fair to you. I know that Your Reverence can do no better than simply join your tears to ours; let us bitterly bemoan the disasters which others could remedy but simply refuse to do so.

### 44 The Lord Patriarch’s criticisms of the Beijing Jesuits, and the criticisms they could make in return

Re: his second point, we have long known that the Lord Patriarch has tools to hand which he can turn against us, and he can swear that they have some veneer of legality. But he is never going to produce these in China, where they can easily be shown to be groundless and unworthy. We make no attempt to demonstrate their falsity now, out of respect to the Lord Patriarch, but if we are unfairly pressured, we can in justice produce our reasons, and if some people then find themselves pinned down by the subsequent thunderstorm, they will have only themselves to blame. And if anyone thinks the sound of thunder is just me farting, let me tell them, and I swear I shall prove it, that we have an authentic disavowal of the things that Fr. Sabino brought with him from Beijing in February 1707. I mean the things which were compiled by a violation of the seal of Confession and by an outrageous deception which was perpetrated in the course of the sacrament of penance while the Lord Patriarch, Fr. Appiani, and Fr. Frossoloni were staying in our house—the things which were extracted in the most disgraceful manner from Fr. Guéty’s servant while the Lord Patriarch | was being conveyed from Nanjing to Nanchang. <!-- PDF page 144 | book page 135 --> Many people would wish that these things were brought into the open now. But our primary responsibility is to save the mission. Were it to collapse because of those men who make such false charges against us, what good will it do to burden so many people with the results of their stupidity and plottings? If, by the grace of God the ship of the church is to be saved, what use will it be against the winds and storms simply to string together some accusations? All the same the weapon which can in justice defend us lies in our house, and anyone who deliberately engages with it has only themselves to blame.

### 45 Fr. Müllener’s plans, journey, flight, dangers etc

As for the third point, I maintain that that it was not written by the people in Guangzhou; not by Europeans, but by Chinese. And the only source from which they could have learnt it is from Fr. Müllener’s servants, who had spoken of it in the province of Huguang, from which he had fled. I am not concerned to discover whether the servants’ story was true or not, but I would like Your Reverence to know how the situation came about. On February 28th, Fr. Müllener without any previous notice burst into our church, where Fr. Bayard was. He stayed there that day, said Mass, and on the 29th said goodbye as though he was on his way to Sichuan, where it was said he had been sent by the mandarins. On the 1st and 2nd of March he divided up his luggage, intending to leave some of it behind and take the rest with him, and hired mules so that he could go straight to Beijing. He deceived the mandarins’ agents, however. I would prefer you to learn the rest from his own letter, an authentic transcript of which I attach. So will he come to Beijing, and with what success? Perhaps he will not come, but will be captured en route by some pursuing agent; and how inconvenient will that be for him? And what will be the repercussions for the mission, in every province in which the memory of the most unfortunate interrogations of Fr. Cróquer etc is still fresh?[^p144-123] And once again, the poor Beijing Jesuits! If, because of his flight, and the imprisonment of his colleague Fr. If, on the other hand, we simply let events take their course, and what happens to him is the same as happened to Fr. Guéty, what a hail of calumnies will descend upon us then? There is nothing left but to commend to God people who, if they do not quite tempt God, lead themselves into temptation, and bring misfortunes down upon others.

### 46 What the missionaries should produce, and what they should avoid

I will limit my advice to three things: (1) it seems absolutely essential that those who are set upon coming, and who are prevented by the mandarins, should send a document detailing the impediment which has been put in their way, or send their application dossier with a request that they should not be stopped by mandarins from coming. (2) By the bowels of Christ we beg everyone not to waste time in useless conversations and theorisings, and thus lose sight of the good of the Church of God. (3) As the Reverend Fr. Commissary of the Franciscans started to do, pressure should be put on the Lord Patriarch to produce the pontifical decree. If it has arrived and he cannot produce it because of the present danger, how in the same danger can the Patriarch’s own regulation be upheld? If it has not arrived, how can we be required to respect the Patriarch’s instruction, which has not been signed by the Pope, or be expected to opt for the destruction of the mission rather than, for the time being, suspend our observance of his mandate? Surely it is all too clear that if the decree has not arrived, the authority of the Pontifical Commissary has been prostituted in trying to derive support from something which does not exist.

(6) There is absolutely no point in thinking that the Emperor has retreated from the position taken in his decrees. ### 53 Further explanation of the flight and departure of Fr. Müllener

It will be difficult for the European reader to grasp the basis for the complaints contained in this letter of Fr. Bayard. If the case is considered in terms of the customs and laws of Rome, what harm has Fr. Müllener done? He outwitted the escort and gave lay authorities and gentile judges the slip; after all, he was not a fugitive from justice, since he betook himself to the Supreme Judge. Indeed, some people in China did argue exactly that, strongly defending the view that it served to protect the freedom of the Church and the sacred dignity of the missionaries. This is not a bad argument in itself, but the inevitable outcome in a gentile country like this is disastrous. I can see that St. Paul was let down in a basket from the walls of Damascus before he could fall into the hands of the pursuing prefect;[^p152-137] nevertheless, once, God permitting, he had been arrested by the forces of the law in Jerusalem or elsewhere, he did not attempt to escape. Instead, he looked to his interests by making an open appeal to Caesar. Why on earth did he do that, if not that he wanted to avoid scandalizing the weak, bringing trouble upon the innocent, and harming the Holy Law of Jesus Christ? If someone in a gentile country is not prepared to give up the canonical privileges enacted in favour of clerics for the sake of these three, by all means let him preach the word of God among Catholics, but he has no business to evangelize the gentiles in the name of the Lord. Until now, for more than a hundred years, what has most contributed to the spread of the Christian religion in China is the fact that that on so many occasions the pagans saw the constancy of the Europeans as they bore imprisonment and bonds undismayed, even on the occasions when they could have avoided them. Whereas last year, they were terribly scandalized by the mere rumour that just one European (Rev. Fr. Cróquer) had run away—though in fact he had not, but had merely left his permitted route without leave from the mandarins. In consequence, investigations were initiated in 15 provinces, missionaries subjected <!-- PDF page 153 | book page 144 | manuscript page 883 --> to harassment, neophytes taken into captivity by the mandarins, and several churches razed to the ground by rioting mobs. An eyewitness to all this can be consulted in Europe, Fr. Provana, who was himself brought to trial and lost his church because of that one false rumour. Because of that same rumour, Fr. Bayard had to spend six months dealing with it all, and was only just beginning to enjoy a brief moment of freedom, getting ready to shepherd his scattered sheep back into their fold, when here was a new disturbance all because Fr. Müllener had taken flight, or an unauthorized divergence, and, of all places, from the market town called Wuchang fu of the provincial capital, Hanyang. That is where the complaints and grim forebodings of Fr. Bayard come from and events soon proved that they were no empty fears. Here is what Fr. ### 58 Important points in these letters

(1) Note that in this letter it is said that Rev. Fr. Du Maij “left the church”: this is to be taken to mean that while he was coming back from Ji’an he refused to live either in the clergy house or in the house of the other Religious. In China the missionaries’ houses are called “churches,” the more important part giving its name to the whole complex, as is the custom in this country.

(2) The Beijing Jesuits wrote to the Illustrious Bishop, but not to Fr. Müllener. The reader might argue that they wanted the Bishop himself to advise him, and they would be quite right. For they could not doubt that Fr. Müllener would be going to ask the Bishop for advice, and that rather than friendly suggestions from Jesuits what he really needed was an authoritative decision from the Bishop. In any case, the Beijing Jesuits could not have offered any advice without doing themselves untold harm. If they were to advise him to come, the seven-horned calumny was opening its gaping mouth against them, as bad as or even worse than the rumours it had delivered in the past—such as that it was through their wiles that the Bishop of Conon had reached Beijing, Fr. Guéty had been interrogated, Rev. Fr. Mezzafalce had been called, Fr. Appiani captured, <!-- PDF page 158 | book page 149 --> the clerics expelled, life made difficult for the Franciscans, and so on.[^p158-148] If they were to advise him not to come, again the accusations would come at once—that they did not want the clerics to receive the permit, and so wanted them to be forced to leave the mission. It was therefore with good reasons that they wanted the Illustrious Ordinary to bear his share of the responsibility and point out to Fr. Müllener that what he had rashly started he should not foolishly complete with such damage to the mission.

Add to this what Fr. Müllener had said in his letter to Fr. Bayard, that he was asking advice from the Beijing Jesuits; in fact, he never wanted either to hear advice or take it. He went to Linqing as quickly as he possibly could, arriving on March 21st, when his letter about taking advice had reached the Beijing Jesuits just one day earlier. Neither did he wait for an answer though in his letter he had said he would; on the 26th he was off again towards Beijing, before any letter from the Beijing Jesuits could possibly have reached him.

### 59 About the danger to be feared from Fr. Müllener

(3) What the Beijing Jesuits were afraid of was not that Fr. Müllener would come to Beijing to receive a permit: the Emperor had long ago given that permission to him and to all missionaries of whatever status and order, and he had been ordered to do so fifteen months earlier by the Emperor, through Fr. Antoine Thomas and Fr. J.F. Gerbillon. The Fathers were afraid that, having first turned down a permit and gone to Guangzhou with the intention of leaving China, as others had done, and then having been required to come back by the mandarins of the province of Sichuan and at their instance being sent back by the high ranking officials of Guangzhou, he had by demanding to be presented to the Emperor by the Great Prince[^p158-149] deceived both groups—as though the Prince would get over his anger and welcome him as one of his clients in opposition to the mandarins and the most sacred rank of delegated government in China. What the Prince would do, nobody could be sure, but it was assumed that he would be less than content when he discovered that the mandarins had not prohibited Fr. Müllener from coming to accept the permit, and that all they had done was require him first to go back to the province which he had left without permission; this was not in order to keep him there on their own authority, for they did not have the power to keep him there without a permit. The main point was <!-- PDF page 159 | book page 150 | manuscript page 886 --> that Fr. Müllener’s companion Rev. Fr. Appiani was still under arrest in Beijing, and Fr. Müllener could easily become his companion in that situation too, if the inquiries conducted by the mandarins in Sichuan should come to the attention of the magistrates in Beijing. Nobody can fail to see the damage that would be done to the Christian cause and how many calumnies would be directed against the Jesuits as a result. For the moment, that is enough about Fr. Müllener’s journey, though we shall have to come back to it soon, under April 2nd, when he arrived at the court. In April too, the Lord Bishop replied to the letters which had been sent to him by the Beijing Jesuits, as will be seen in the proper place.

### 60 Fr. Visdelou is created Bishop of Claudiopolis and Vicar Apostolic by the Lord Patriarch

Finally, under March we come to the account of the election of Fr. Visdelou which was made public in Guangzhou on the 1st of that month and news of which was sent in writing on the 3rd and 5th by Fr. Monteiro to the Beijing Jesuits, in this way: “On March 1st letters and papers from the Lord Patriarch arrived here, stating that he had created Fr. Visdelou Bishop of Claudiopolis and Vicar Apostolic of Guizhou with administration of Huguang. The good fellow, wild | with joy, shifted into the house of the Lord Patriarch to live with Fr. Giampè without handing back what belonged to the Company.[^p159-150] He rushed around the churches to give thanks like one who has become a king by his own efforts, soliciting congratulations.”

I said that it was on 1 April 1708 that this election became public knowledge, because it was said actually to have taken place in 1706, and can be proved to have taken place before 4 November 1707. How this affects the Beijing Jesuits I shall briefly explain. When the Illustrious and Most Reverend Lord Patriarch arrived in Guangzhou in April 1705, he asked Fr. Visdelou about his mission, and the results of his mission. He replied frankly that he had not worked on his mission, since he had been occupied with other matters. The Patriarch did not look upon this answer very favourably, since he gathered that Fr. Visdelou in all his twenty years in China had not looked after any part of the mission. Fr. Visdelou thought this was a very harsh judgement. However, when he recognized what way the Lord Patriarch was leaning, he showed that he too was leaning in the same direction. He handed over his files in legal form, which were accepted by the Lord Patriarch to such an extent that from then on he <!-- PDF page 160 | book page 151 --> never took any account at all of writings from the side of those defending the Rites, other than formal documents. After that, when His Lordship the Bishop of Beijing from time to time tried to get him to consider the case, with not a little ill-feeling towards the Jesuits he presented the consecration of Fr. Visdelou as Bishop as something which would be helpful in getting the Rites condemned, either because he would achieve this by his own efforts, or would manage to get it done in Rome. In connection with carrying out the election, the first problem to be overcome was that for a Jesuit to be made a bishop needs the permission of the superiors of the Society; the second concerns the appropriate living and sustentation for a bishop according to the Council of Trent.

### 61 What the Lord Patriarch asked from the French Fathers for Fr. Visdelou, and Fr. Gerbillon’s response

Both matters were raised with Rev. Fr. J.F. Gerbillon, who at the time was the superior of all the French Jesuits in China. To do so more effectively, they were dealt with by the Lord Patriarch sending the Lord Bishop of Beijing on several occasions to raise it, since he got on with Fr. Gerbillon extremely well by virtue of his character and their longstanding friendship. The Father countered with his own objections, not unreasonably explaining why he was not permitted to agree and why it was not a good idea to triple the annual allowance for someone who neither had needed nor would need more than half that to carry out his work. When the requests became more frequent, saying that if he were unwilling to increase the normal amount for a simple missionary for someone who was to be made a bishop, he should at least obtain from France provision either for a supplement, or for the entire episcopal allowance to be paid, Fr. Gerbillon finally, to close the discussion at one blow, made it clear that he would never, nor could he in accordance with the Constitutions of the Society, agree to increase the allowance on his own authority, nor could he obtain the money from France. There were two very serious reasons why in conscience he was prevented from doing so. The first was that, in accordance with the decree of Trent, when there were so many missionaries worn out, yet rich in their harvest of souls, there was no way in which someone could seem more deserving who had over twenty years neither worked in the vineyard nor wished to do so. The second reason was that it was manifestly unjust that a member of the Society should be elevated to the episcopate and maintained out of their funds, when, unbeknown to his superiors, he made common cause with the opponents of the Society, and clearly was being chosen precisely because he was opposed to the Society. Indeed, were the Society to go along with that, it would be an invitation to any dissident to declare open war on the Society, knowing that they would then be rewarded <!-- PDF page 161 | book page 152 --> by a bishopric and be doubly honoured by the Society. This reply produced a long delay, until progress was eventually made in the matter, as the reader may gather from the accounts which will be given later, as they were sent by Fr. De Goville, the Procurator of the French Jesuits in Guangzhou.
