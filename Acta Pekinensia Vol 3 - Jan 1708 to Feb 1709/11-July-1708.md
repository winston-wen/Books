Some while ago the Jesuits in Guangzhou had written that the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon took it as definitely fixed that at the beginning of May he would sail to Manila; from there he would either go himself or, if it was more convenient, would forward to Rome the documents which he had prepared to further the cause of the mission. He had recently appeared before the Governor to ask permission to leave the country. So, there was little doubt in Beijing that His Lordship must have left long ago.

July 1st. Definite news came that that great man’s hope and any chance of his sailing in the near future had been dashed, and that he had been compelled to remain in Guangzhou to his immense sadness.

### 1 A letter from Fr. Kilian to the Bishop of Ascalon

On July 2nd, Fr. Kilian wrote to him as follows: For a long time, I have not paid you the courtesy of a letter, because since the end of February when I was last privileged to hear from Your Most Illustrious Lordship, every one of the Guangzhou Jesuits had assured us that Your Lordship was anxious to get away, had handed his petition to the Governor, and had asked, or was about to ask, for permission to leave China. Fr. de Britto confirmed all that while he was here, and unequivocally stated what he took to be the truth, that Your Lordship had already set sail for Manila. That explains why it was that the Jesuits here did not think that there was any need to inform the Emperor of the opinions expressed in Your Lordship’s last letter, where you said, “Your Reverence, or someone else, must inform the Emperor that I prefer to observe the (Lord Patriarch’s) decree rather than to follow his declarations unless the Holy See consents etc.” We took it that Your Lordship wrote in that manner to make it easier for us to obtain leave for you to leave the country from the Emperor himself. But when we knew for certain that Your Lordship was applying for that permission in Guangzhou and that your request could be granted, nobody here felt that it would be helpful to communicate to His Majesty that form of expressing the request, which could have opened the way to further difficulties. It should be noted that in April the Emperor drew up a new decree to be published by the Libu in all the provinces ordering the officials to allow Europeans who had the permit to live their lives in peace; those who wished to receive a permit should come to the Court, and the officials should expel from China those who were unwilling to do so. Once a decree like this had been widely promulgated, the very permission which Your Lordship wanted was granted, and the mandarins could no longer deny that you were free to leave the country.

<!-- PDF page 282 | book page 273 | manuscript page 954 -->

If they still wanted to prevent you because of the mistaken action I took last year when, misunderstanding what Your Lordship wanted, I promised or as good as promised in your name that once Your Lordship was in better health you would make the journey here to apply for a permit. I assure you that I am prepared—and I promise that I will do so—to see to it that Your Lordship’s words, as mentioned above, will reach His Majesty—whenever, that is to say, Your Lordship has definitely decided to leave the Empire. If you wished to remain in the Chinese mission freely and without serious problems for Your Illustrious Self and the Reverend Augustinian Fathers, these words could not be said, as Your Lordship knew from the beginning.[^p282-1] So I humbly beg you, after you have seen the effect of the Emperor’s new, or rather his renewed, decree and have had news from Europe which ought to be arriving soon if it has not arrived already, to review your decision about what you think is best for the general good of the mission as well as for your own sacred person, and be kind enough to write to any of the Jesuits if there is anything at all that you need to be done for you. Your Lordship must believe that all of us would join in the effort to do you any service you might wish and which could be done. On this point, I feel myself under a greater obligation than any of the others, since, mistakenly, instead of doing what you intended, I arranged for an application to be made to His Majesty in your name, an application which you complain prejudiced the Lord Patriarch against you and the mandarins as well so far as concerned your application to come to Beijing and apply for the permit. | To reinforce to some extent these assurances of mine, I want Your Lordship to know that this letter has been written with the knowledge and the approval of the Fr. Visitor and of Fr. Antoine Thomas, the Vice-Rector. He would be writing himself if his poor health had permitted it; for most of the time, he has been confined to his bed of pain ever since the Lord Patriarch left, and so prevented from honouring Your Lordship with more frequent letters. We are very much afraid in case the extraordinary heat-waves we have had this year might prove fatal to him. I shall finish here, and await on my knees Your Lordship’s holy blessing.

Beijing, 2 July1708.

That this agrees with the two copies of the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary

### 2 The Lord Patriarch’s new decree, under four heads

July 3rd. In Macau the Lord Patriarch issued the following decree: 1. Under pain of suspension a divinis,[^p283-2] no-one may enter or leave the mission without obtaining his permission in writing in advance.

2. Those who have entered without his permission must make good their delay within four months, under pain of the same penalty.

3. Under pain of suspension a divinis nobody may assume the office of superior unless the Patriarch has been consulted in advance, and has agreed.

4. He declares: should anyone accept and exercise the office of superior without the Patriarch’s knowledge and consent, then his subjects are released from their obedience in both the internal and external forum.

Charles Thomas Maillard de Tournon, etc.[^p283-3]

Note: this decree has been taken from an unofficial manuscript copy. I so testify. Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary[^p283-4]

To each and every missionary, members of the secular clergy as well as those belonging to any religious order, and to their superiors in this China mission, greetings in the Lord, etc.

Like an army in battle array, the Church is presented to us by Him who is the perfect and unchanging order of all things, without any shadow of alteration, so that, just as the soldiers of this world wage war under their tribunes, prefects, and generals, those who profess to do battle under the standard of Christ should also learn to obey their superiors. Indeed, since we are members of Christ, we should in accordance with apostolic tradition be joined to our head in the hierarchical order according to the function meted out to each member, hearing his voice and venerating his likeness in the prelates of Christ. The pages of sacred scripture teach us to obey even uninformed[^p283-5] superiors, offering to God obedience rather than sacrifice. If order is the first necessity <!-- PDF page 284 | book page 275 --> everywhere, it is especially necessary where it is proper that the holiness of the Christian religion should shine out in the discipline of its ministers, for it is in their office and their example of holiness that glory can be seen to reside and the good order that comes from the knowledge of God is made manifest. For what honour is there in the Gospel if “the sacred stones are scattered at the head of every street”?[^p284-6] Scattered indeed they are, when we see that the priests and members of the more perfect bodies,[^p284-7] incorporated into the solid foundations of God’s building for the edification of the Church, have been dislodged by their disobedience from the solid foundation of the divine edifice. We see them, forgetful of the rock from which they were hewn, throwing over the rights of government and openly attacking their superiors when they ought to be their visible adornment. Paying no reverence to the shepherd of the Lord’s flock, they make bold to break ranks and usurp his governance; paying no attention to him to whom the Lord said “Feed my sheep,”[^p284-8] and undeterred by the wretched condition of the one who enters the sheepfold other than through the gate. Since these scandals have recently crept into these missions under our very eyes in contempt of the apostolic authority which We enjoy, and, even more regrettably, perpetrated by certain Religious who are bound by their vow to an even stricter obedience to the Holy See,[^p284-9] we are bound to lament with the prophet, “How the gold has grown dim, and lost its excellent colour.”[^p284-10] Well does Gregory say how changed it is, when the good repute earned in the past by men who were thought to live religious lives is diminished.[^p284-11]

Lest our struggle should be entirely in vain, in virtue of the powers entrusted to us, unworthy as we are, by His Holiness Pope Clement xi and the power of the Holy See, and wishing to confront these abuses directly, we decree and lay down that any missionary or priest, whether secular or regular, belonging to no matter which order, even the Society of Jesus,[^p284-12] who has been led to these

Chinese shores by a desire to propagate | the faith, and who has presumed to enter the missions without obtaining the requisite permission from Ourselves in advance (which will be given in writing), or to undertake the work of a missionary without such permission, or to leave without requesting and obtaining permission, shall be suspended a divinis ipso facto, irrespective of any indults and apostolic privileges, even those given to the Society of Jesus, all of which in so far as is necessary to achieve this effect we suspend and declare suspended in virtue of the special power given to us by the Holy See.[^p285-13] So far as concerns those who in such an irregular manner have dared to enter the missions,[^p285-14] in our kindness and good will we allow them a period of four months from the publication of these presents in order to expunge the delay, after which period unless they have repented and humbly obtained both pardon and permission from us, they shall be suspended a divinis ipso facto. And since in these cases their superiors cannot be regarded as blameless who, secretly taking up the reins of government without even informing us,[^p285-15] lead their subjects into the pit of disobedience by their dangerous example and, often enough, even advice; those against whom the Lord seems to complain through the prophet, saying “my sheep are pastured on what has been trampled upon by your feet”: indeed the sheep are being fed upon what is trampled by the feet of the shepherds, when subjects imitate the works of iniquity which they see.[^p285-16] For these and other reasons we require and command in virtue of holy obedience that no priest, whether secular or regular, even a member of the Society of Jesus, should <!-- PDF page 286 | book page 277 | manuscript page 956 --> without informing us and without obtaining our prior permission presume to exercise the office of superior in any form over the others in his congregation or order. And if anyone who is to be nominated as Visitor, Vice-Visitor, Provincial, Vice-Provincial, Commissary, or Vicar Provincial, or any other sort of superior acts contrary to this decree, besides suspension a divinis to be incurred ipso facto and reserved to Us and the Holy See, we now and for all time invalidate and nullify any action or command done or to be done by any such person, and we declare them invalid and null. We release and free subjects in both the internal and external forum from obedience to such a person, and so, using the authority entrusted to us by apostolic authority and our authority as Legate a latere, we decree, lay down, and order these things to be observed by everyone. For it is fair that the person who is in charge of the workers in the Lord’s vineyard, and has the role of head of the family throughout the earth, should recognize these people and should know under what superiors they do the work of the fostering of the Gospel.

Given in Macau at our palace, our normal place of residence, on July 3rd

ness Clement xi, by the Mercy of Divine Providence Pope. [Sealed here]

Charles Thomas Patriarch of Antioch, Apostolic Visitor. Witnessed by: Sabino Mariani, Auditor and Andrea Candela, Chancel-

### 3 Observations on this decree

The reader should note:

1. That the above decree is being cited by me on this day because this day is given by the Lord Patriarch as the day it was issued. Also, where it was issued, I do not know. I do know that it was printed in Guangzhou by the Patriarch’s people long after July 3rd, and was not published there until September 7th of this year, when it was communicated to the Franciscan Fathers. I shall say more about this under October 7th, because today (November 25th) I do not have the opportunity to copy it out of my notes.

2. It is fairly obvious that in this decree it is the Jesuits who are being made the culprits. From the end of June 1707 until 3 July 1708 no missionary came to China apart from one Jesuit, Fr. Jan Bakowski, a Pole. So much for the vigilant rod of the Lord Patriarch, raised and ready to strike! To enter as he did is described as “contrary to normal practice,” when there never was a “normal practice.” Nobody had previously asked permission from the Lord Patriarch to enter, nor had he ever ordered this to be done. Why then he says that those who have entered without previous permission in writing are allowed four months to “expunge the delay” is almost | incomprehensible. Where is there <!-- PDF page 287 | book page 278 --> a delay which needs to be excused? It is even harder to see why someone has to do penance and humbly beg for pardon under pain of suspension when they have done nothing to be penitent about and have violated nobody’s order or laws.

### 4 The results of this decree

3. At the end, it is stated that the purpose of the decree is that the Representative of the Universal Shepherd should be able to recognize his missionaries and their superiors by their faces just as he does the highest order of the hierarchy under him. I do not deny that this is a legitimate aim. But what will the decree actually achieve in practice? In the missionaries, a lethargy of soul and a coldness towards the mission, when they see that they are treated on a par with, indeed as even lower than, mercenaries, who at least have their keep paid for them while they look after their flock, and who furthermore have the choice to leave their paid post and commend the flock to the lord of the flock. But missionaries who are Religious who have left everything for love of their sheep have, at their own expense, left their homeland and voyaged to these distant parts, and from their own funds can hardly feed themselves let alone their flock. They are not allowed freedom either to come or to leave, under pain of penalties and punishment, and, what is more serious still, are denied these things by a man from whom they have never previously received the slightest assistance, nor hope to do so, nor ask to do so, and when they have deliberately debarred themselves from any ecclesiastical preferment. They should have been invited, should have been attracted by rewards, fostered with kindness, encouraged with praise, set on fire by exhortation from the man who proclaims that the flock has been committed to him. And what happens? They are compared to a thief who does not come in by the gate! All that remains is for him to say that they have come to steal, slaughter, and devour.

### 5 How heretics will react to the decree

Although the Lord Patriarch does not say this in so many words, it is exactly what some heretics in England who hear about this decree as hot news from China will say to the applause of their followers. For when the Patriarch’s decree is printed in China, they will be able to taunt us and accuse the Jesuits of desecrating the sanctuary of the Gospel, rising up against prelates of the church, showing contempt for the human and divine laws, coming up from some other place accompanied by demons, and worming their way into the mission.[^p287-17] And this is supposed to enhance the <!-- PDF page 288 | book page 279 | manuscript page 957 --> standing of the Gospel, that the ministers of the Gospel are defamed and made a laughing stock to the heretics—the very men who by their teaching, their labours, their preaching, and their conversions of the heathens have with distinction preserved the unique character of the apostolic church!

4. If the Patriarch’s people think that tears have to be shed because a single Jesuit entered China without first having sought permission from the Lord Patriarch, others have thought there is much more cause for tears in that the intervention of the Patriarch’s supporters should be the reason why more than forty missionaries in a single year either were expelled or voluntarily left China.[^p288-18] If it were a sin to have brought one missionary in to replace the loss of another, it was surely a forgivable lapse, hardly deserving of the lightning shafts of Jove, since there was an excellent reason for forgiveness. ### 6 About the Jesuits mentioned in the decree

5. That aforementioned Polish Father had special leave to enter China, given by His Holiness Pope Clement xi, along with indulgences, as can be authoritatively verified from the letters of Very Reverend Father General written at the command of the Supreme Pontiff. Indeed, four other fathers of our Society also entered: Castner, Gonzaga, Liebstain, and Hinderer. The decision that they should enter was not theirs, nor that of their superior; they were compelled to enter by order of the Emperor and were escorted by the local mandarins. Indeed, the third and fourth of these had the same permission from the Pope, sent to them in writing, and the first two were blessed by His Holiness with his own hands when they were in Rome and given faculties for the mission. Neither can it be said that at least there was sin involved in the fact that he and the other four missionaries did not defer to the Lord Patriarch in order at least to be honest and straightforward, even if it was not strictly required by the law. However much they might wish to offer the most extreme deference to His Excellency and at his feet beg for permission, that could not have been done, because at that time a military guard barred all access to the Patriarch’s palace | for several months.

### 7 A letter from Fr. Tomás Pereira to Fr. Hortiz

July 7th. From Father Tomás Pereira replying to a letter of Rev. Fr. Hortiz, Provincial Vicar of the Augustinians, <!-- PDF page 289 | book page 280 --> asking for help in the suffering and violence inflicted upon him by the evildoing of the officials, restricting the persons of the Fathers to the house, and destroying the church from its foundations up. Father Pereira’s reply is as follows:

Very Reverend Father in Christ,

The Peace of Christ.

On the 13th day of the current lunar month, the day after the Emperor left for Tartary, I got Your Reverence’s letter, full of justified and painful complaints. As I read it, I was doubly hurt, firstly because recently, when Fr. de Britto came to Beijing, I had no clear information about the totally unjust persecution inflicted by the mandarins; secondly, because your Reverence’s instructions arrived when His Majesty was already gone. So, on two occasions now the chance has been lost to deal with the matter as speedily as it deserves, and, unfortunately but inevitably, it now has to be put off for some months whereas at the time it could have been dealt with easily and in a very timely fashion, just as at that time, by the favour of Heaven, the business of the church in Xinhui was settled, as I imagine your Reverence must already know. At that time, Fr. de Britto had some vague information as he passed through, just as we had from a letter of Fr. Monteiro; but exactly where, from which mandarin, and for what reason your Fathers’ church had been attacked, nobody could work out. As a result, nobody could make any representation before His Majesty.

Now I shall set out briefly what we think ought to be done. Would Your Reverence be kind enough to obtain absolutely definite information: what is the name of that zhixian, what is his family name, where does he come from, whose family does he belong to; is he a qixia,[^p289-19] and if so which Banner does he belong to, Tartar or Chinese; and is he a client of any prince or magnate here in the Court? These are more or less the details which will most help us to move quickly. One could have wished that that every missionary throughout China had that information about his local mandarins, so that, when the need arose, they could write to me or the other Jesuits. Then, if you have collected all the information I have mentioned, don’t delay in letting me have the details. Meanwhile I will do all I can here to find out. The point is that it seems we can get much further by having recourse to the hostile mandarin’s parents, relatives, or patrons rather than by going directly to the Emperor; both because the Emperor is far away and because he is extremely unhappy at hearing such <!-- PDF page 290 | book page 281 | manuscript page 958 --> stories about the mandarins, and does not wish any of them to be punished in public by word or deed for the sake of European foreigners. Your Reverence can see this from the way the Emperor settled things in Xinhui: all he required was that Fr. Roman Hinderer should be left in peace in the church, and that the shrine of St. Xavier should not be damaged. He did this without uttering a single syllable of criticism of the mandarin’s wrongdoing in fraudulently appropriating that church and all its sacred furniture. Again, if the zhixian mandarin were to be denounced to the Emperor by us, he could defend himself by pointing out that your Fathers have promulgated the regulation of the Lord Patriarch against the Chinese Rites, and it is quite certain that we would never obtain anything we might think of asking for. On the contrary, the fear would be that the mandarin’s audacity would be tolerated, if not confirmed, setting a terrible precedent. For the Prince would easily assume that your Very Reverend Fathers were in the wrong both because the promulgation had been made and because nobody from the Augustinian order had turned up to apply for a permit in the last six months. I know how upset you Very Reverend Fathers are at still being held back by the Patriarch’s iron opposition, but they can easily see that such a reversal of policy and resorting to delaying tactics cannot help us here at the Court to promote the business of your Fathers; indeed, it could prove a major obstacle. Your Reverence will have heard more than enough about the Emperor’s recent decree by the time you get this letter; and, aflame with the zeal of St. Augustine, will easily see what is to be done in this situation, and will carry it through with steadiness and courage.

At Beijing, etc.

That this agrees with the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary

### 8 Some matters relating to Fr. Visdelou, and his acceptance of the episcopate

On the previous day, that is July 6th, Fr. Visitor, Giampaolo Gozani, began to write what it was decided was needed | concerning the matter of the bishopric of Fr. Claude Visdelou of our Society. Gozani had taken up the post of Visitor while he was in the city of Kaifeng fu in the province of Henan, on 29 February 1708, on the day before Visdelou admitted in Guangzhou that he had accepted the Patriarch’s offer of the bishopric of Claudiopolis, which he did on March 1st, going to all the missionaries’ churches, announcing his acceptance, and asking for their congratulations. At that time, indeed, Fr. de Goville, sj, Procurator of the French Fathers, opposed [the appointment]. The reason why Fr. Visitor did not write earlier was that the first news he had of it in the month of May was from a third party, and since it was rather vague, he was not able to do anything about it. He thought it enough to wait till he got <!-- PDF page 291 | book page 282 --> to Beijing, where the letters from all over the place could be brought together and he would have more information, and there would be no shortage of advice from others about such an extraordinary affair.[^p291-20] In Beijing Fr. Visitor got a letter from Father Dentrecolles, Superior of the French Jesuits, giving him more accurate information about what exactly had been done by Fr. Visdelou who, as soon as he had himself announced his acceptance of the bishopric had moved from the house of the Society to that of the clerics which had been built two years previously. When he heard about that, Fr. Visitor wrote firstly to Fr. Visdelou, secondly to the Bishop of Beijing, and thirdly to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon; to the last with thanks for having postponed the consecration ceremony which had been requested, and to the second requesting that, following the example of the Bishop of Ascalon and in view of the clear provision of laws of our Society, he too might refuse to consecrate him until the case had been heard; and to the first, issuing a canonical warning, and ordering him to return to the house and the obedience of the Society and quietly to wait for the agreement of Father General, in accordance with our Institute, the apostolic letters, and general canon law,[^p291-21] so that he could produce in due order all that the canons required him to produce. Almost all these letters were written on July 6th, but with multiple copies for the archives, some of them had to be described as signed on the 6th, others on the 10th, and others on the 13th. The reader may find all of them, together with a complete statement of the case and the official proceedings, at the end of December, where I shall present each of them accurately by way of an appendix.

### 9 About a certain Dominican whom the Emperor expelled from China, who came back and wished to stay hidden

Also, on July 7th Fr. Simões, the superior of our residence in the city of Nanchang sent the first news of Fr. Caballero the Younger, a Dominican, who was expelled by name from Hangzhou by the Emperor together with his other colleagues. He had secretly returned and crossed into Fujian Province, where he wished to remain undiscovered and minister to the Dominicans’ Christian communities. What Fr. Simões wrote on July 7th became known in the capital after August 7th and <!-- PDF page 292 | book page 283 | manuscript page 959 --> was confirmed by letters from other people as well; especially by the witness of Rev. Fr. Juan Fernández Serrano, the Pro-Vicar Apostolic in Jiangxi, and his companion Fr. Diego de Santa Rosa when they came to Beijing on October 29th to receive the permit. They said that Fr. Caballero had spent a few days as a guest with them in Nan’an, and in the first ten days of June had left for the mission in Fujian. The return of this priest would seem to be no concern of the Beijing Jesuits and I would happily pass over it in silence here, were it not that the accusations of our enemies compel me to speak. They suggest that, out of hatred of the Dominicans and hostility towards this man who has come back secretly, we obtained the final decree of the Emperor urging everyone to be expelled who did not have a permit. For this reason, I am sharing with the reader everything we know about the famous return, relying on the original documents in our archives.

By order of the Emperor seven or eight Dominican Priests together with some secular priests and Fr. Frossoloni were sent back to Macau, as has been said under April and May 1708. They were not expelled to Macau so that they could stay there, but so that they could sail home from there. Two of the Dominicans arranged passage with a ship and left for the port of Surat,[^p292-22] whence they could either continue their journey by land or travel on by sea to Europe, where they said that they intended to act on behalf of the Patriarch, or against the Jesuits. One of them (called Astudillo[^p292-23]) stayed with the Patriarch; the plan was that he would be his interpreter with | the Chinese if the Lord Patriarch had any Chinese business to conduct. Four waited for an opportunity to sail back to Manila. The Procurator of the City of Macau[^p292-24] by a pious deception hid them from the mandarins who were looking for them; they asked whether they had left on any of the ships which had sailed from the harbour, and he replied that they had. But, in fact, they had stayed on, thanks to his good offices, while a cutter was got ready to sail to Luzon. The cutter belonged to people from Macau, as did the cargo, but in addition it carried more than sixty Ethiopians[^p292-25] who had been bought and either somehow belonged to, or were being paid for by,

Don Miguel de Castro, a Spaniard, captain of a Manila ship which was loading in Guangzhou.

### 10 How it was that the Dominican Fr. Caballero, who had been expelled to Macau, came back from there to the province of Fujian

As April drew to its close, there boarded the cutter four Dominicans; one secular priest Carvalho, who came from Manila; and a Franciscan priest, Fr. Gabriele,[^p293-26] who had gone to Macau from the province of Shandong in order to recover from a mental illness. The cutter had hardly got under sail when a storm sprang up and threatened danger, while a mutiny in the bowels of the ship was stirring. The Ethiopians were many and strong, the kind of people whose masters had been happy to keep them out of their houses. Seeing that they far outnumbered the others, they refused to be kept in order, and began to think of escaping from their enslavement to others and enjoying their liberty if they could make landfall somewhere. The captain was not a particularly brave man, and felt that he had little hope of subduing a drunken mob of Ethiopians, so he treated them rather leniently. They, on the other hand, became more violent, elected one of their number captain, assigned people to sail the ship, handed over the keys of the ship’s stores, gave the contents to one of their number, and put the captain in irons. But when they found that they could not do without the captain’s help in running the ship, they chained him to the tiller. He, looking for any solution he could devise, at night let the ship go back to an island near Macau called Tien Pe.[^p293-27] It was an extremely rocky place, but for that very reason was especially suitable at the time, since for months and months there was no hope of moving the ship anywhere else. In the morning, the kaffirs[^p293-28] saw that they were close to land, and forced the Religious to go ashore. They then gleefully raided the foodstore, stripping it of everything edible or drinkable which the ship contained either for the voyage or in its cargo. Fr. Gabriele, caught up in this disaster, became even more mentally disturbed, and wandered about in that lonely place. Three Dominicans took their chance to go back to Macau, and in fact came back later in a small boat. Fr. Carvalho with Fr. Caballero went back to Guangzhou where they were welcomed in the house of the Italian clerics. It was in this place that the Dominican Fr. Muñoz was living, exiled by imperial command.

<!-- PDF page 294 | book page 285 | manuscript page 960 -->

A consultation was held, and it seemed best to Fr. Giampé and to the Dominicans, their provincial vicar, and the others, that word should be sent to Macau in the hope of finding a chance of sailing again. Fr. Caballero was to be allowed, as he had himself decided, to return secretly to Fujian to minister to the Christians there as best he could. The reader will easily understand that the Jesuits were not asked to vote on such a matter, nor was there any obligation to ask them. But the Lord Patriarch was consulted and gave his consent, and, they say, letters in which he appointed either Fr. Magino[^p294-29] or Fr. Caballero as Pro- Vicar Apostolic in Fujian. Armed with these documents, he donned ordinary clothes, shaved his beard, changed his Chinese name from Shan to Liu, and headed for Fujian. He reached Nan’an around Pentecost, and, having spent a week there, resumed his journey. It was not just to the one person that this decision of the Father seemed a good one; rather, it was commonly said by people in Guangzhou, and was put in writing elsewhere, that this way of getting around in China was practicable, as was borne out by a good deal of experience. To support those who took this view, there is the fact that Fr. Le Blanc in Fujian, Fr. Guigue in Shaozhou, and Fr. De Maij in Guangzhou had all escaped detection.

### 11 A European cannot long stay hidden in China

Nonetheless, this kind of plan was full of danger. For although some European might escape detection in China for a while if there was no search being made, it has always been judged impossible for any length of time if the usual search is mounted in China. The officials will easily turn a blind eye when there is no hope of a bribe, or when a search has not been ordered by a superior magistrate; but when the higher ranking one has issued an order, the one of lower rank has got to start looking in such a way as to be sure of finding the fugitive, under pain of losing all his goods. | The example of Fathers Le Blanc, Guigue, and de Maij is beside the point, because they had not been expelled by order of the Emperor, and on whatever day they were rounded up by the officials they could always say that they would be going to Beijing to apply, and that no time limit for doing so had been set in advance.

### 12 Dominicans on the run, and their hiding places discovered

In the case of Fr. Caballero, however, a special decree had been issued and he had been expelled by name by the Emperor in Hangzhou on May 3rd, and in addition he <!-- PDF page 295 | book page 286 --> had changed his name, which is a crime in China. He had therefore laid himself open to danger on two counts—his changed name, and that in contravention of an express decree of His Majesty he had freely, deliberately, and willingly re-entered with the intention of remaining in China in hiding. Not long after he had come back, he was betrayed to the Guangzhou mandarins. Since, as I related, the Macau cutter was stuck at Tien Pe, with the Ethiopians banqueting on everything there was, the magistrates in Macau had to appeal to the Chinese military commander to assist them in getting back both the Ethiopians and what was left of the goods. The Chinese commander[^p295-30] managed this with ease. He set some traps at the tables which had been laid and invited the kaffirs, saying that he was keen to get to know their dances and music. The better to gain their confidence, for two days he engaged in trade with them, exchanging wine, meat, and other such things in return for the more unusual things which the cutter contained. He told them that when the day of the festivities arrived, they should attend unarmed, since by Chinese law one could only attend a banquet unarmed, and that he did not wish to sit down in his festal dress with them in the midst of weapons. The Ethiopians complied, but when the wine was making their feet itch to get dancing, the traps sprung and caught them all except for just four who were stronger runners, and escaped into the tangled undergrowth. The Chinese soldiers managed this comic opera with ease, having first been induced by some money; then, having done some profitable trading with the Ethiopians; and thirdly, because they were looking forward to the reward which they hoped was yet to come from the people in Macau. When it came to light that those Religious had still been lying low in Macau up until the end of April, despite the fact that when the Guangzhou magistrate had put the perfectly legitimate question whether they had sailed in accordance with the Emperor’s command, the Procurator of Macau had assured him that they had, they accused him in a public proclamation from the Governor of lying to them, and considered that they had good grounds for imposing a fine. It is true that this did not happen without inspiring fear in the people of Macau, who wrote to Beijing requesting that, if the matter was referred to the Emperor by the magistrates, the Jesuits would please use what patronage they could on behalf of the city, especially since another Dominican, Fr. Astudillo, was still staying with the Lord Patriarch. But we have not heard of the mandarins trying to take the matter further than to reprimand the procurator of the city, and <!-- PDF page 296 | book page 287 | manuscript page 961 --> to send a naval guard to keep watch on the seaward entrance to the Lord Patriarch’s house. Through all the churches of Guangzhou they searched for Fr. Caballero and finally discovered that he had been staying in the Italian house and asked them to explain what he was doing. The clerics there replied that he had left for Fujian, to look for an opportunity of taking ship. Hearing this, the mandarins wrote to Fujian. For what happened next, see our account under September.

### 13 From what happened to the Dominican there is no basis for accusing or blaming the Jesuits

That is sufficient to establish that the Beijing Jesuits neither produced nor accelerated their memorial because of his re-entry, as I explained at some length under the end of June this year. For firstly, the decision to make the request was taken on March 6th, when nobody, including Fr. Caballero himself, had any doubt about his departure for Luzon. Secondly, the application was made on April 27th, at the very time he was on board the cutter. Thirdly, the decree which followed the request was sent to the provinces on June 17th, on which day nobody in Beijing could have heard anything about Fr. Caballero’s attempted entry or his wish to go into hiding in China.

On July 8th a ten-foot-high window was made for Fr. Appiani. He had asked for it three days earlier, and there was no problem in making it except that the Father had not paid enough attention to the nature of the site, at one time wanting it to face east and at another to face west. Fr. Kilian, whose help he had sought because he was responsible for the glass-making workshop, had asked him to decide where the window was to go. He decided on the western end of his quarters, in such a way that in the same place | a door could be cut through, something which had previously been impossible because it had been tiled over. Fr. Kilian did not dare to agree to a door being opened into the place in which Fr. Appiani was being detained; in such cases, the decision rests with the magistrate. But he did suggest to the carpenters that they should remove the tiles, but leave the bottom ones up to a height of one foot or a little more from the ground, in such a way that it could not properly be called a door. In the end, it was decided that the southern window, which was small, should be enlarged to ten feet, which was almost the height of the room itself. Fr. Kilian did not look after this because he was in charge, but out of kindness, arranging matters with the assistants of the official so that while the responsible officials were staying with the Emperor in Tartary, as a favour to him they might make a concession to the poverty of Fr. Appiani at Father Kilian’s expense.

July 10th. Reverend Father Vice-Provincial wrote to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing about the publication of the last decree through the Ministry <!-- PDF page 297 | book page 288 --> of Rites, as, in the appendix to June, we saw had been alleged in a letter of the Illustrious Lord Bishop of July 31st, which we reproduced there in anticipation.

### 14 Two of the Beijing Jesuits are summoned by the Emperor to Tartary

At this time, Fathers Bouvet, Régis, and Jartoux extended the map which had started from the sea and followed along the Great Wall as far as a place near the Emperor’s summer quarters. There they put the part completed so far on display, which pleased His Majesty. But since Father Jartoux, because of his usual haemorrhoids, had become ill on the way so that for some days he needed to be carried in a chair or taken by cart, it was decided that he should be left there in the service of His Majesty, and that someone else could stand in for him in the geographical work.

And so, on July 12th, about eight in the evening Henkama came with an order from the Emperor that people should be sent as quickly as possible to take his place. Fr. Ehrenbert Fridelli was asked for by name. But since it was requested that he should be accompanied by someone else who was not named, Fr. Luigi Gonzaga went with him, having packed his clothes in a hurry.

On July 16th, they both left, guided by Zhao (Purghama)[^p297-31] who had hitherto been the companion of the other three, and had been sent to Beijing to summon Fr. Fridelli.

On July 18th they reached the Emperor in Karahoton. The Prince asked them about their experience in geography, and they both replied that they had already learned the laws of science, but they were not as yet trained in drawing maps. When the Emperor was told this, Father Jartoux was ordered to continue the journey with the others, now that he had spent nine days in restful recuperation; Fr. Fridelli was told to stay so that he could assist Fr. Castner in his work; and Fr. Gonzaga, on the advice of the Emperor’s doctors, was allowed to come back home to look after his health, for he had not been well during the journey and had got worse with the stress of riding a horse at high speed.

### 15 Who Fr. Giambattista Iliceto was, and why he set out for Mexico etc

July 20th. From Macau news was brought to the Jesuits in Beijing of the discovery of a letter from Fr. Giambattista da Iliceto.[^p297-32] He is a Religious of the Observant

<!-- PDF page 298 | book page 289 | manuscript page 962 -->

Franciscans and a Neapolitan national, well known in Rome and to the Pope. He had previously been in China with some other members of his order: Castorano, Gabriele, Antonio Ripabottoni, etc.[^p298-33] He had left China to raise funds and went to the province of Mexico via Luzon in order to collect alms, although he and his companions are dependent on the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith, from whose funds they are supported, so there was no reason for them to be in need and beg for their keep. But he was turning over a larger scheme along with his companions, for which—since the usual and reasonable annual amount which was sent to him would not be sufficient—he sought more funding through himself and his Brothers in Mexico. He received all the more because he moved the pious feelings of many people by saying that he would build houses in China where abandoned children and their nurses could be supported, with baptism provided for the little children and the nurses educated in the mysteries of the faith.[^p298-34] The proposal was music to the ears, but absolutely impossible to carry out in China, as is obvious to anyone with any knowledge of the country. But it was useful as a way of appealing to the sympathy of the tender-hearted, and was so effective that more than ten thousand ounces of silver were raised, which the Father transferred | to Manila to be used for the benefit of his mission. The Lord Patriarch raised questions about this fund, saying that it was not within the powers of the Franciscans, who could not own any funds and were rather missionaries of the Sacred Congregation, to whom control of the fund should be handed over. To avoid this, Fr. Iliceto applied the sum to the convent of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. But this has nothing to do with the point I wish to make. Fr. Iliceto had come from Mexico to Manila and then from Manila to Macau at the time when the Lord Patriarch had first left Beijing, and things were in such turmoil that he never re-entered the mission, despite the fact that the people in Macau <!-- PDF page 299 | book page 290 --> wanted him and tried to persuade him to do so, so that he could wait there until the disputes were settled.

### 16 A disgraceful letter written by him

However, they did not treat Father Iliceto badly. He enjoyed the favour of many people, and was on good terms with our Fathers who lived in the college there. In the end, either because it was his own idea or because others had advised him, he wrote the following infamous letter to Mexico which was given to us by the Captain General in Macau. I do not know how the Captain got it but it may be that it was found in the cutter of the Ethiopians which has been discussed above, since it was supposed to be bound for Manila and when the cutter had been brought back to Macau in such a wretched condition, everything in it was topsy turvy. That the Lord Captain should have given that recovered letter to us was not due to friendship, but to laugh at us because our people had accepted Iliceto as a friend, and to reproach us because they had recommended him to the very same Captain General. Fr. João Mouraõ wrote about this on 20 July 1708: “This Religious, as soon as he arrived here last year, at once introduced himself as a great friend of ours. He told us that in Batavia he had had news from Rome that the decree concerning the Rites had still not come out. He also told us other things which were so full of nonsense that our Fathers took them to be a complete invention on his part … When he arrived last May, our superiors ordered me to give him an introduction to the Capital General, which I now regret not least because I trusted him, etc.”

What kind of retribution might have been inflicted on Fr. da Iliceto for his deceitful behaviour and for the defamatory letter against the people of Macau and Portugal in general I have so far never been able to discover, other than what he wrote to Fr. Carlo Castorano at the Lord Bishop of Beijing’s residence in Linqing, saying that in March 1708 and later he had suffered a lot at the hands of his brethren (that is to say, the Franciscans) in Macau with whom he had lived until then, of the citizens, and in particular of the Captain General, to the extent that he was forced to lie low in the mountains and hide away in some hermitage or other with four French missionary priests. This itself is not entirely true, and we shall throw more light upon it under the month of November. The letter, and the report of Fr. Iliceto, we shall give next month in August, in which a very energetic reply to that letter was made by the Most Illustrious and Reverend Lord Bishop of Ascalon and Vicar Apostolic in the province of Jiangxi. But His Most Illustrious Lordship wrote, as he himself says, of his own volition, out of a zeal for justice and following the example of His Most Illustrious Lordship Ildefonse de S. Thomas, Bishop of Malaga, who wrote against the Calvinist preacher

<!-- PDF page 300 | book page 291 -->

Jurieu, etc. suggesting that, unintentionally, Fr. Iliceto might have made himself into one of that man’s servants.[^p300-35]
