In this month too several things happened concerning the Reverend Father Ludovico Appiani; I will start by dealing with them, the better to clear the ground for talking about various other matters.

### 1 Some not very important matters concerning Fr. Appiani

On the 4th and 5th of October, Fr. Appiani’s bed and hypocaust was rebuilt with tiles one foot square, under the supervision and at the expense of Fr. Kilian, in accordance with the Father’s request made last month.

On the 7th, he asked Fr. Louis Porquet for some paper, but Porquet put off getting some, I am not sure why. One could suppose that, being a newcomer to our house who had not yet much experience of how things are done in Beijing, he preferred that this service to the Father should be performed by someone else better placed to know what the dangers were and what the guards might be disposed to allow. Fr. Appiani was somewhat put out by what he interpreted as the Father’s stinginess, and wrote a prickly little letter to that effect. He promptly proceeded to ask Fr. Kilian for the paper on which, as he protested, he wanted to note down all the lights and spiritual insights gained from his reading and meditation on pious books.

On the 14th, Fr. Appiani sent a leather waistcoat to Brother Rhod, from the sale of which he wished to get enough money to look after his needs by buying some utensils. The waistcoat was brought to the Brother by a soldier, one of the Father’s guards. The Brother replied that it was not at all proper that he should act as Fr. Appiani’s go-between, selling things as if they were his own and at whatever price he decided; the Fathers would not allow Fr. Appiani to go short of anything he needed, and the generosity they had shown towards him hitherto would never fail. If some things sometimes were not sent, he should not assume that they were being purloined, but that they were delayed until the guards gave permission to send them. But …

On the 16th of this month suspicions were aroused concerning this waistcoat. Verbal complaints were conveyed to Fr. Kilian in which no mention was made of Fr. Appiani by name, but we were told that one of the soldiers had asked him for money to help with the expenses of his mother’s funeral. When Fr. Appiani said that he had neither silver nor gold, the soldier asked him to lend him the roll of silk which he had, which he could then pawn for the amount of money needed to purchase a tomb in which his mother’s body could be laid. It was said that the soldier had then made off with the silk and would never be seen again, since he was going to have himself transferred to a different guard <!-- PDF page 446 | book page 437 | manuscript page 1042 --> division. One person was spreading this version absolutely dispassionately; but another was saying that there had been an arrangement between that soldier and Fr. Appiani: the soldier would convey the Father’s letters to the postmaster, who would send them on to Linqing or Guangzhou, and the soldier would be given the bolt of silk as payment for this service. The first informant wanted to enlist Fr. Kilian’s assistance to get the stolen bolt of silk returned to Fr. Appiani. But what was Fr. [Kilian] to do? If he set up an inquiry and the story got out, then both Father [Appiani] and the thief of the silk would be found guilty. But if he refused to help, Fr. [Kilian] could appear to be lacking in sympathy and taking delight in someone else’s loss. Approaching the affair with some caution, Fr. Kilian inquired whether the person who denounced the thief had been expressly charged by Fr. Appiani with reporting the matter to Fr. Kilian. When he said this was not the case, Fr. Kilian thought it better not to express any concern until Fr. Appiani himself made it clear that he wanted assistance.

On the 23rd or thereabouts, Fr. Appiani asked Fr. Kilian for Volume Three of Bucelin and some paper, by means of the following note:

Volume Two is returned, and I shall love it if Volume Three should come, to bring solace to my mind and to stimulate me to acquire virtue. My eye does not skim headlong over the pages as I turn them but I touch them eager for knowledge, aware that a perfect opportunity for reading and re-reading them is passing me by. I know it is so, and I most willingly embrace this time which God, the great giver of all gifts, has granted me to use and not to spoil by wasting time. God grant that, as I spend my days firm in this belief, so my life will forever correspond to what I believe. Your Reverence, I trust, will not be deprived of the reward which the kindness you have shown me deserves. Just as I thank the High God for the opportunity to read this work, whose title I had never heard of, so I pray that He might generously endow with the fullness of His Spirit both all of the members of the Society who have chosen the path of virtue, and also that, after an abundance of blessings | in this vale of tears, He might crown Your Reverence with a distinguished throne of glory. My constant prayer is that He might grant this to you, who have (almost before you had heard of my need) so generously and kindly arranged for my bed to be rebuilt. The charity of Christ compels Your Reverence, and may this love fill your Christian soul to overflowing. These are the prayers of him who, without any pretence, is Your Reverences most obedient servant, etc.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

Fr. Kilian sent the book and the paper, to which he added the following note: I send you as requested Volume Three: I wish that it may bring you comfort <!-- PDF page 447 | book page 438 --> of spirit, and be the means of an increase of grace. We have no other news except that the ship has arrived from Goa with neither masts nor sails. Letters from on board were sent to us from Guangzhou on September 3rd but they have not appeared as yet. An English ship (as it was first mistakenly thought to be, but later has definitely been identified as Dutch), similarly dismasted, ran aground in among the islands near Ningbo. So far, we have had no letters from her. The Emperor will be coming back about next Sunday; he is angry as a lion with the [Eldest] Prince, who recently killed some of his retinue. We commend ourselves to God that, at this bad time, there may not be bad news from the provinces.

That this agrees with the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

### 3 An altercation between some Franciscans and associates of the Lord Patriarch about the appeal lodged by the Franciscans

On [October] 1st, the actions of the reverend clerics, mentioned above under September 10th, were recorded in Spanish by the Most Reverend Fr. Fray Nicolás.[^p447-1] Yesterday evening, 30 September 1708, at this church of our Franciscan Fathers outside the walls of Guangzhou, there arrived the Abad Giampè and the Abad Cordero, along with Señor Bénard.[^p447-2] When we were all seated, Abad Giampè said that he had a letter from the Lord Patriarch of Antioch to pass on to us. He opened it and it was received by Brother Preacher Fray Manuel de S. Juan

<!-- PDF page 448 | book page 439 | manuscript page 1043 -->

Bautista (who is the President of this church), who in turn gave it to the Brother Preacher Fray Miguel Roca to read out. This he did while all listened in silence. When he had finished reading, the Abad Cordero said they had a complaint against us for having said in our appeal that those who were complying with the Apostolic Decrees were troublemakers. To this, some replied that this was not the case, and that our appeal did not contain anything like that. He said more on this topic, which I do not recall in detail. Brother Preacher Fray Manuel de S Juan Bautista addressed the aforementioned reverend gentlemen, who said that they had come to communicate the Patriarch’s letter, and they had done so; we had heard them, he said, and would fulfil our obligations. The Abad Giampè and Abad Cordero said that our appeal was null, since much time had passed since the promulgation of the decree before our appeal was lodged. To this I replied that when it was a question of commands relating to the future, an appeal can be made at any time prior to the end of the period fixed in the decree; this can be seen from the case of a decree of excommunication, requiring one to appear in court before three months have elapsed, and until the three months have elapsed one can lodge an appeal at any earlier time, and the appeal will be valid. Upon hearing this, Abbot Giampè said that what I said was true if the matter was one in which an appeal was valid in law, but the decree of the Lord Patriarch was not a matter against which an appeal was valid. I answered him, “Why is this not a matter against which appeal can be made?” And he said to me, “It is a matter of faith and religion, and in such matter an appeal is not valid.” I replied: “Why is it a matter of faith and religion, and why can there be no appeal in such a matter?” The exchange was interrupted by one of the three gentlemen—I do not recall which one—saying that it was inappropriate for us to harm others by describing them as “troublemakers.” I replied that if the appeal in question was harmful to anybody, let them point it out to me, and I was quite prepared to withdraw it in so far as that lay in my power—if there was anything harmful such as these gentlemen alleged. I repeated this once or twice. Then Abad Giampè said to me, “The appeal from you Fathers has no legal force, because the Lord Patriarch did not admit it.” In reply I said to him: “Whether the Lord Patriarch upholds it or rejects it does not deprive the appeal of its validity, if it is legitimate on other grounds.” He came back, “It is correct that the validity of the appeal does not depend upon whether it is upheld or rejected | by the Lord Patriarch, but if he does not uphold it, it is not valid and you are not allowed to avoid the censures.” I replied that no matter how many times the Lord Patriarch dismisses them, legitimate appeals cannot lose their validity for that reason.

<!-- PDF page 449 | book page 440 -->

### 4 The Patriarch’s associates maintain that whatever he decrees is

The Abbate Giampè went back to his earlier claim, that no appeal against the Patriarch’s decree could be valid since it dealt with a matter of faith and religion. I replied de fide that if what is commanded in the decree of the Lord Patriarch is a matter of faith let him produce any pontifical decree in which all that stands defined; if there is no pontifical definition, to seek to have us hold it as a matter of faith is to treat us as if we simply do not know our subject. The Abbot Giampè replied, “Whatever the Patriarch defines as a matter of faith or religion is to be held as a matter of faith.” I gave up, saying, “To say that whatever the Lord Patriarch determines is a matter of faith has to be held to be a matter of faith because he has said that it is, is a heretical proposition.”[^p449-3] As I said this, some Chinese were announced, and the three men left.

Note: In the decree of the Lord Patriarch which was the topic of this discussion, although in the dispositive part there are indeed things which are agreed not to be matters of faith, to say that whatever the Lord Patriarch determines in such a decree is a matter of faith has the result that everything which the Patriarch determines even in the dispositive part of the decree is also of faith; but since it is agreed that some of these have never been defined as such by the Catholic Church, then if they were to be of faith for some other reason it could only be because the Lord Patriarch had so determined, and that is why I understood the Abbot Giampè’s final remark in an unacceptable sense. That was why I interrupted, and expressed the statement in the form in which I understood it. But since I had raised my voice a bit and had become somewhat heated because of what had been said, perhaps I had not well understood this or that word. Indeed, I do not believe that such a Catholic gentleman would have intended it in any sense contrary to the faith which the Catholic Church teaches. I have put here the case as it stands, so that the truth of the matter may be known as well as possible, as it should be. I make this affirmation, and I sign it with my name on the first of October of the said year, which is a Monday, having been present during the events under discussion on the Sunday already mentioned, etc.

That this agrees with the authentic transcript, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

### 5 The Guangzhou Franciscans appeal against the excommunication pronounced against them by their own commissary

October 5th. Those Most Reverend Franciscan Fathers of the Spanish nation who are still living in Guangzhou replied to their Most Reverend Father Commissary, or, as they say, their ex-commissary, Fray Bernardino de las Llagas, appealing against the excommunication and curse which he imposed upon them as was said under September 28th above. The letter of appeal runs as follows:[^p450-4]

To our Brother Commissary Fray Bernardino de las Llagas.

Brothers Martin Alemán and Antonio de la Concepción presented to us a letter from Your Charity,[^p450-5] dated 28 September 1708, in Macau. A note was added after the date, signed on behalf of[^p450-6] Your Charity, which goes as follows: “They have today informed me that Your Charities have all appealed against the decree of His Excellency concerning the practices; if this is the case, and given also that I am your prelate, henceforth I place you under my curse, and that of our Father Saint Francis and of the whole heavenly court. Since you are in contravention of so many precepts of the rule and of my will and also since I am your prelate, I order both Your Charities to notify all the others of this point, and I order you under Holy Obedience, under pain of incurring major excommunication ipso facto, to retract the aforesaid appeal.

September 7th, as above. Fray Bernardino de las Llagas.”

Such are Your Charity’s words, and although you will not suggest that you needed only a few hours to decide to do something so contrary to justice, reason, and ecclesiastical law, our contention would be that, if Your Charity had taken some days to study and reach a decision in such a serious matter, we are certain that Your Charity would not have committed so many sins as are involved in your order and in the censures mentioned in that short sentence. We could say a great deal to Your Charity on the subject, but we will content ourselves with what is precisely necessary to assist your Charity to put such great sins behind you.

In the first place, that order and those censures have been made without any jurisdiction to back them up, and so | they are totally null and void. Because our

<!-- PDF page 451 | book page 442 -->

Brother Provincial acting within his area of responsibility in our Province of St. Gregory has ordered that in serious cases, and especially those which involve the survival or the destruction of this mission, Your Charity should do nothing until the advisors and the other Religious have met, reached a view about what is to be done for the best, and acted upon it. It follows that Your Charity cannot simply on your own authority do anything you fancy, without the vote of all the others. Your Charity has acknowledged this on several different occasions, calling various meetings, and you have seen that the opinion of everyone else was always that we should appeal against the decree and the censures of the Lord Patriarch; you were the only one who said at those meetings that he was unwilling to appeal. In forbidding us to appeal you have therefore exceeded your jurisdiction, and we are appealing with the approval of our province, as I have said, and Your Charity cannot prohibit us to do so—to say nothing of the acts of violence, of which we shall say more below.

### 6 The second reason why they judge this excommunication to be null

Even if we grant, without admitting that it is true, that you acted within your jurisdiction, even so your excommunication is void, for the three admonitions, at the required intervals, required by the Councils and by the Sacred Canons have not been given. Although sometimes a judge may dispense with some of the admonitions and intervals in case of necessity, nevertheless he may never dispense with all the admonitions and intervals, in the way that Your Charity has behaved, contrary to the law both natural and ecclesiastical. Hence this excommunication is clearly invalid, and since Your Charity has committed a grave sin against the rights mentioned above, and by the same token has sinned gravely in sending this censure like a thunderbolt without either explaining or indicating the reason for the thunderbolt, for this reason we insist on referring the matter to the judge for his determination.

### 7 The third and fourth reasons

The third reason. Even granting without admitting that Your Charity is acting within your jurisdiction and that you have not been guilty of nullity for want of admonition—even granting all these, this order of yours and these censures are manifestly unjust and as such cannot bind us, for it is public knowledge and notorious that Your Charity has always accepted the reasons we give for our appeal, considering the burdens and inconveniences which are laid upon ourselves and on these missions as a result of the enforcement of the Lord Patriarch’s edict. This is clear to all of us who live or previously lived in this house during the present year and the previous year, not to mention Religious of other orders who have dwelt elsewhere. We protest that we shall prove this to our Brother Provincial. It is not simply that

<!-- PDF page 452 | book page 443 | manuscript page 1045 -->

Your Charity has said that you are unwilling to appeal because you do not wish to give offence to the Lord Patriarch, and because Your Charity does not wish to remain in this China mission nor to be sent back to Manila. That is why you have constantly maintained that you do not wish to make a fuss, and since this was your constant position whereby you publicly held as justified the very arguments on which our appeal was based, it is clear that the prohibition against which we are appealing is formally unjust even according to Your Charity’s conscience. And since the prohibition is manifestly unjust, the excommunication is therefore equally manifestly unjust, and has been imposed like a thunderbolt for some special reasons of which Your Charity will doubtless have a better understanding.

The fourth reason. Even granting without conceding that the arguments we have just given above do not hold up, though they evidently do, nevertheless the command and the excommunication you have issued are manifestly sacrilegious, unjust, and null because they clearly contravene Canon 13 of the Bull De la Cena,[^p452-7] in which those persons are condemned and excommunicated who forbid recourse to the Holy and Apostolic See, or decree or command anything inconsistent with such recourse, such as Your Charity, with little fear of God and the Church, does in the clause of your letter cited above. So, it is Your Charity who is excommunicated by the bull De la Cena. Neither can you nor could you plead ignorance, for you are a confessor, and whenever you set yourself up as a judge and issue a command under pain of excommunication, you must know full well what you are doing, and not act on a “more or less” basis. Those of us who believe that in the Catholic Church there is but one Pope also believe that it is only from the orders of the Pope that there is no appeal to any other judge. Consequently, we believe that from no matter what | judge, and from whatever penalties such a judge might impose, it is possible to appeal to the Pope. Those who hold such a belief are blessed by God. Since the reasons for our appeal are sufficient even if the Lord Patriarch describes them as frivolous, the appeal reverts to His Holiness, who will pronounce whatever will be just. Such recourse neither you nor the Lord Patriarch himself can deny to us. Appeals to the Holy See by those who have a grievance against other judges is not an act of disobedience, but rather a recognition of the superiority of, and a submission <!-- PDF page 453 | book page 444 --> to, the Apostolic See. The opposite cannot be maintained without schismatic implications. Let Your Charity see who will turn out to be cursed by God, and by our Holy Father St. Francis, and by all those who submit to the Apostolic See; on the contrary it is Your Charity who does not worry about going against the natural law and the law of the Church, nor pays any attention to what is laid down in the sacred canons. By riding roughshod over such important rights and forbidding us to have recourse to the Apostolic See, you yourself are rebelling against it. Your Charity is prohibiting us from following the dictates of our own conscience, and is not even following your own conscientious convictions, so often and so seriously explained by Your Charity yourself.

Despite all that has been said, and because we hope that Your Charity will recognize the mistakes you have made and will set out to remedy them, in order to avoid rumours and scandals, and without conceding to Your Charity any greater authority than that which you have by law, in virtue of the greater abundance of the authority we have, we appeal against Your Charity’s command and excommunication as cited above, to our Brother Provincial of this Province of St. Gregory; we ask, as indeed we must, the Apostles to witness the good faith of this our letter and appeal, and send it to our Brother Your Charity entrusting it to our Brother Guardian of this house of Macau, where he may be found. Dated in this our Church of St. Francis outside the walls of Guangzhou on the 5th of October, 1708. From Your Charity’s servant, Fray Jaime Tarin, Fray Jose Navarro, Fray Manuel de San Juan Bautista, Fray Miguel Roca, Fray Nicolás de San José.

That this agrees with the authentic transcript, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Notary Apostolic

### 8 The instruction sent by the Guangzhou Franciscans to Rome in furtherance of their appeal against the Lord Patriarch

October 7th. A copy of the collection of material sent by the above-mentioned Most Reverend Franciscan Fathers to the Most Reverend Father Procurator of the Missions, in Rome, to provide him with material with which to reply to the objections of the Procurator Fiscal of the Lord Patriarch’s Office:[^p454-9]

The Most Reverend Father Procurator of the Missions can give a satisfactory reply in Rome to the allegations made by the Promotor Fiscal of His Excellency the Lord Patriarch, with the authentic copy of the official documents which we are submitting in this brief, in this form. Under the Letter Patent A there is a reply to the question raised by the Promotor, that the appeal had been made without the authorisation of any prelate. He correctly maintains that this is lacking; this was the very reason why Fr. Tarin held a consultation with everyone, and with their votes settle the appeal. To the Promoter’s claim that Fr. Fray Bernardino our commissary did not consent to the appeal, this is true, but under letters B and C it is shown that the Fr. Provincial of the Philippines had arranged things in such a way that in present circumstances he was divested of power and authority which was invested in the seniors;[^p454-10] several votes were involved, of which Fr. Bernardino had only one. Indeed, he did not have even a single vote, because when the appeal was lodged the Father in question had abandoned the mission and left China, as can be seen under letter A. The decree of the Lord Patriarch of July 3rd, which the Promoter accuses us of contravening, lays it down that nobody | new should be appointed as a prelate in all these missions without first having obtained leave from His Excellency. We said that although this is dated July 3rd, it was neither published nor promulgated before September 7th, when Fr. Ignazio Giampè handed it over to Fr. Tarin with seal intact, as is clear from his letter D, while the appeal was lodged during the month of August and so is not prejudiced by it.

### 9 The Franciscans continue to support their appeal

In so far as the Promoter maintains that Fr. Roca swore to observe the decree of 25 January 1707, the reply is that the aforementioned Father did not swear not to appeal if not doing so would involve doing harm to the mission. Nor did he swear not to seek to be released from an oath which, had it continued to be observed, would have been “a bond of iniquity.”[^p454-11]

<!-- PDF page 455 | book page 446 -->

As for the general remarks made by the Promotor, that the reasons for our appeal are frivolous, he neither gives nor can give adequate grounds to substantiate this, when it is a well-known and notorious fact that there are innumerable such reasons. If the fact that the Religious of St. Francis are losing so many houses and churches founded by their own labours and with funds given by the Catholic King and by the faithful of the Spanish nation is frivolous, will it likewise be frivolous when so many souls are lost now and in the future through the absence of the Religious to persuade them and administer baptism to them? And if this is frivolous, even more when we see the missionaries expelled, people apostatising from the Christian faith, and the temples of God taken out of our hands and robbed by gentiles, as has already happened, and given over to profane uses? If these reasons are frivolous, there are others which we leave to the consideration of His Holiness, since they touch upon the substance of that same decree against which our appeal alleges that it is unclear, arbitrary, and suspect, since it presumes to rest upon a decree of His Holiness which defines the matter ex cathedra, and does not refuse to allow anyone to see His decree. This is all the more necessary at a time when it is so essential to know exactly what it says in order to give a response to the Emperor in fairness and in truth. The Most Rev. Fr. Procurator of the Missions will find at the Sacred Congregation of Propaganda a great deal more with which he can oppose the Promotor Fiscal, for the Franciscans are neither the first nor the only ones to appeal; the first appeals were lodged by their Lordships the Bishops of Ascalon and of Macau and by the Vicar Apostolic of Nanjing, and they were also lodged by more than 30 members of the Society, theologians and professed members of their Order.

### 10 They reply to objections which could be made

As for the Promoter’s allegation that the Commissary accepted the decree by his letter of 19 April 1707, in this clause: “All of us gave it our obedience with the greatest joy, seeing that the decision had been taken which we had been awaiting for so many years”; the reply is that this acceptance was extracted from us by deception. The Lord Patriarch’s decree by using equivocal expressions gave us to understand that His Holiness ex cathedra had decided about the controversies in China. Moreover, at that time nobody had experienced the drawbacks of the decree. These, together with the expulsion of those who accepted it, did not happen until the month of April in the province of Nanjing, more than 200 leagues away from Guangzhou, from which the Commissary wrote on the 19th of that month. As a result of this deception some swore to observe the decree without having seen it nor experienced the great evils which follow from it.

<!-- PDF page 456 | book page 447 | manuscript page 1047 -->

We claim that it is for that reason that we are appealing, after almost two years, or to be more precise 17 months after we received notice of the decree. We declare that this appeal is a last resort and at this point unless measures are speedily taken neither the churches nor those who minister in them can be saved, even if everything is not already lost, and on every side the door is not already closed against the propagation of the faith. That is why the appeal was made, when the burden became intolerable.

Dated in this church of our Father St. Francis outside the walls of Guangzhou, on 7 October 1708. Fr. Jaime Tarin, Vice-Commissary

### 12 What should be gleaned from the Acts of the Franciscan appeal

I have reported the acts of these very Reverend Franciscan Fathers at some length, since, apart from their intrinsic interest, they are also extremely useful for refuting the lie which was put about in 1707 and continues to be repeated to this day, that eight Very Reverend Franciscan Fathers in the province of Shandong obtained the imperial permit allowing them to remain in China despite the fact that they had insisted to His Majesty in person that they intended to observe the decree of the Lord Patriarch against the Chinese Rites. For how, if things were as has been stated, could this truth be known to everyone except the Franciscans in Guangzhou? Did not they themselves take advice in person from Fr. Fray Antonio Frusononi (or Frusoloni) before he was expelled from China and left for Macau, going thence to Europe? Did those Fathers not long ago have with them Fathers Martin Alemán and Miguel Fernández, both of them Franciscan missionaries in the province of Shandong, | who took part in the ceremony in which they accepted the permit along with the other Brothers in Shandong? But if from these men and from the letters of others who are still in Shandong, they had learned that it was true that the permit could be given and the missionaries could be questioned with a view to accepting it no matter how vehemently they protested in favour of the Lord Patriarch’s regulation and against the Rites and the Emperor, to what purpose were all these efforts and appeals and pleadings and so on? They would indeed have deserved the most grave censures if, having had it in their power to obtain the permit at such a small price, they still preferred to avoid obeying at such great inconvenience to themselves.

### 16 Points to be noted in this letter

Fr. Bayard says that, in this letter of the Reverend Father and in his conduct, anyone who understands the situation will find things which defy common sense, and so will refrain from making any comments. In so doing he shows his simple sincerity of soul and the Society’s customary charity, which is indeed long-suffering. However, wellordered charity does not prevent us from protecting the interests of truth; one will be careful not to cause personal injury to those who stretch the truth, but nevertheless one will not shrink from restoring to its proper place the truth which has been violated. So, the reader will be willing to put up with a few comments from me, which I think will prove useful for posterity.

1. The reader should put these matters together with what I said about the Most Reverend Father under December 1707, February 1708, and March 1708, and excuse me if at that time I did not write sufficiently clearly. I did not then have this letter to hand, which would have helped me to explain things more distinctly. That I did not then have the letter was because, as Fr. Bayard says here, Fr. Müllener’s letter was suppressed, or at least not handed over, for thirteen months.

2. That letter of Fr. Müllener was signed on 29 June 1707, and on that date was left in Jingzhou fu for Fr. Bayard. It happened however that Fr. Müllener was sent back by the prefects of Guangzhou to Sichuan, and eight months after the letter was written he went back to the province of Huguang and met Fr. Bayard. He stayed with him as his guest for a day or two, but said nothing explicitly to him about the patriarchal decree, which he himself had promulgated in the Father’s mission eight months previously, nor about the obedience promised by the Christians which the letter hints at, nor about the letter he had left at Jingzhou fu; this despite the fact that Fr. Bayard, who still knew nothing about what had been done in Jingzhou fu, had introduced the Patriarch’s decree into a face-to-face conversation, as was made clear in March. What can explain | the reason for such a silence? Fr. Müllener was under no obligation in the circumstances to speak about the promulgation he had made; indeed, it might have seemed to him sensible to say nothing about it to Fr. Bayard, in case he got annoyed by being reminded of the infringement of his jurisdiction, whereas he wanted him to be willing to assist in, or to cover up his flight, which he was planning at that precise time. But I do not like to think that Fr. Müllener would consider it more important to conceal his flight than to speak of the promulgation of the decree. But if what he actually said to Fr. Bayard both at the end of February 1708 and face to face on September 8th of that same year are carefully weighed, they do not sound as if he had a very high regard for the Patriarch’s decree. For in February, he said that he would observe it according to his own understanding of it, and on September 8th he asserted that the decree <!-- PDF page 463 | book page 454 --> had been drawn up in such a way that each person could interpret it as he saw fit in accordance with his conscience.

### 17 The departure, or rather the flight, of Fr. Müllener from his Church

3. Fr. Müllener says at the beginning of his letter that a persecution had already begun in the province of Sichuan against the Most Reverend Fathers Basset and Baluère, but that he had left before it could land a single blow on his own skin. Who would have believed it if any Jesuit had written this about Fr. Müllener? But it is clear now from his own admission, with nobody forcing him or even asking him, that he deserted his sheep when there was no other shepherd for them, since Fr. Appiani was being held in Beijing, and Fathers Basset and Baluère were to be expelled any day soon. Whether it was fear of persecution or weariness at the rather unfruitful work that drove Fr. Müllener to desert his church is not clear; what is clear is that the explanation he gives to justify his departure is not at all satisfactory. This is what he says: “In the interests of Christianity [the Christians] and of my church, I left before the blow fell.” If that is to act in the interests of the church and the Christians, perhaps the hireling in the Gospel also cared for his sheepfold and his flock when he fled at the sight of the approaching wolf![^p463-23] What Fr. Müllener should have done, we might say, was to leave before the blow fell so that before any scandal arose among the Christians he could go to Beijing to equip himself with the imperial permit; that would have protected his own interests and those of his church against the malice of the officials. He seems to half admit that himself when he writes: “If they [the mandarins] were to ask any questions, the guardians of the church were to reply that I had been summoned to the Court to greet the Emperor, which is the unvarnished truth.” There is an objection to this, however, which remains, and is not lightly to be easily dismissed. For if it is “the unvarnished truth” that he had been summoned to greet the Emperor— as indeed in a sense he was—why did he not explain this to the officials so that with their full knowledge, congratulations and assistance he could go to the capital? Now that would indeed have been “in the interests of his church and the Christians.” And why did he not go to Beijing, but to Guangzhou instead? And when the Guangzhou officials tried to send him back to Sichuan, why did he resist them so fiercely that his fellow priests had to beg him by everything that is holy not to embark upon a course that would bring ruin upon himself and all their churches? Why did he not seek permission from the Guangzhou <!-- PDF page 464 | book page 455 | manuscript page 1051 --> officials to go to the court to greet the Emperor, which would have been the unvarnished truth? I myself have no doubt that Fr. Müllener acted as he did without malice, but for lack of good advice, and so it came about that, caught in a tight corner, he dreamed that he had to consider the other Christians: European cleverness could deceive Chinese stolidity. That was why he eluded the Sichuan officials before the blow fell, why he escaped from the confused Cantonese agents, and why he opened the formidable letter from one Viceroy to another. Since he admitted he had opened and read this letter, it is probably there that he will have discovered the reason for his expulsion, which no other missionary has so far been able to understand.

4. Had Fr. Müllener given the reason why proceedings had begun against Fathers Basset and Baluère, and why a persecution was in process of being stirred up against himself, it might have been possible to figure out the reason why he promulgated the Lord Patriarch’s regulation in a mission belonging to someone else. It is public knowledge in China that Fathers Basset and Baluère, Müllener, and Appiani in Sichuan together with their Christians followed the opinion and practice which was against the Chinese Rites, and which their vicar apostolic, the Most Reverend Bishop de Lionne, Bishop of Rosalia, was at that very time arguing in Rome should be introduced everywhere. On this point, I have the unchallengeable authority of the Lord Patriarch. When in my protest against Fr. Appiani’s actions which I presented in Beijing in January 1706 | I named him as a well-known opponent working against us, he took exception and replied that that Father together with his companion Fr. Müllener in Sichuan had followed a practice contrary to the decree of Alexander vii, on the grounds that it was only proper for him to do so since his vicar apostolic was defending the same contrary policy.

### 18 The reason for the persecution in the city of Sichuan[^p464-24]

It is therefore not a groundless conjecture that the reason for the persecution which was stirred up in Sichuan was the practice of these Fathers against the Chinese Rites. If it was because of this contrary practice which he had so far pursued that he had to leave Sichuan “before the blow fell,” why would he want to publish and introduce that same practice in a different province, when nobody ordered him, or urged him, or forced him to do so, and when the missionary who belonged there did not agree to it, did not even know about it, and had not been consulted? I confess, however, that my way of bringing all this together is <!-- PDF page 465 | book page 456 --> too summary to exhibit it as a series of actions befitting a prudent man. A rough sense of propriety in me keeps suggesting that I would never forgive myself if I had done what Fr. Müllener did in this kind of situation, and indeed, the shared judgement of mankind would come down against me. For if I had seen that my mission in Sichuan had been lost because of the contrary practice I then decided that that practice should destroy Huguang in the same way; that just as I had foolishly thought I could by some stratagem escape the clutches of the mandarins in Chongqing fu, I then hoped I could escape them in Jingzhou fu; or even that Fr. Bayard could be forced by me to go there—a man who had appealed against the regulation or decree of the Lord Patriarch—and that my promulgation of the Patriarch’s decree in advance would compel him to defend it against the local magistrates, though this was something I was unable to manage even with my three associates in Sichuan. And so, with my church, my Christians, and my mission abandoned, I would leave China for Europe, there to be rewarded by a fat benefice.

### 19 Inconsistent sections of Fr. Müllener’s letter

5. I do not wish to pursue all the thoughts which come to mind about the writings and behaviour of Fr. Müllener which I have just described. I ask the reader to consider just one more point. It concerns the sentence in this Father’s letter which says: “I have stayed here for about a month, during which time I have not wished to introduce any innovations or make any decisions … so I tried not to detract in any way from Your Paternity’s jurisdiction.” A little earlier in the same letter he admits that he had administered the sacraments without obtaining delegation to do so, that he had promulgated indulgences, had arranged what had to be done and need not be done in order to obtain the indulgences, had promulgated the Lord Patriarch’s decree against the Chinese Rites, and had accepted a promise from the Christians to observe it. Yet here he is protesting that as a guest he did not wish to introduce any innovations, nor take any decisions in someone else’s church, in case he infringed upon their jurisdiction! We may indeed admire such a cautious and scrupulous theology. I will become a bore if I decide to carry on about all this as it deserves; it will be better to follow Fr. Bayard’s example, and ignore it.

### 21 A Christian cemetery forcibly occupied by the local inhabitants

Also on October 11th, Fr. Girolamo Franchi for the second time wrote giving details of his complaints and those of his Christians | against the person who unjustly occupied the blessed cemetery for the Christian dead. Both to give us better information and to urge the Beijing Jesuits more strongly to come to his assistance, he sent an accusation written against the occupier in Chinese together with a plan of the cemetery itself and a description of the tombs and of their inscriptions. Such an outrage perpetrated against a public cemetery which for so many decades had been peacefully occupied was quite extraordinary. It is incredible that people in Shandong Province, which borders upon the capital, should have dared to give such offence to the Holy Law without the terrible effects consequent upon the Lord Patriarch’s rule followed by so many <!-- PDF page 467 | book page 458 --> repeated imperial orders aimed at those who are against the rites, together with the expulsions of so many missionaries, in particular Fr. ### 22 The mandarin Henkama dies a Christian

Also on October 11th. In Beijing, Henkama died of his illness, described above, like someone falling gently asleep. He was also known as Wang Laoye, and was a mandarin of the third rank. He has frequently appeared in these Acta Pekinensia. God had mercy on him, for he died a Christian; a grace he amply deserved for his many services to our Holy Religion in his honest dealings with the Emperor over very many years. His baptism took place in 1707 on the day before the Feast of Saints Peter and Paul; the saving waters and the name of Peter were conferred by Fr. José Soares. He will live on through his translations into Manchu of the captions for each of the illustrations in the Biblical atlas; an outstanding work, and one all the more distinguished because it was undertaken at the request of the Emperor, and carried out with holy intention. He did not live to complete it, and the finishing touches were provided by his brother, who is younger in age but greater in knowledge and learning, and who also confesses his adherence to the Christian faith.

### 23 The Emperor’s grief at the death of his son

October 15th. Fr. Parrenin wrote a sad letter from Tartary, which he finished on the following day, the 16th. ### 27 Father da Silva appeals from this citation to the Supreme Pontiff

From this citation and warning and these censures etc., Fr. da Silva appealed to the Holy See through our Procurator Fr. Giovanni Baptista, sj, as will be related in the month of December, below.

But you, Reader: what private thoughts, what picture of the Chinese mission are you forming when you hear the thunder of censures rumbling all around you, the lightning of citations flashes through the air, and missionaries having to make long journeys to contest accusations in tribunals? But I am not concerned to go on about this at greater length, nor to go into the reasons, nor to calculate the total expenditure of pontifical funds on sending messengers with similar citations to travel two, three, or four hundred leagues and the same again on return, as though they were being sent from the Minerva to Monte Cavallo, or to the Cascades, or to the Ponte Sisto, at the expense of one giulio.[^p471-35]

October 20th. The first news reached us of the arrival of the ship from Goa which was towed, heavily damaged, into Macau. It was pointed out that a second ship would have to be got ready so as to leave for Goa early in the New Year, since the damage to this one could not be repaired in time.

### 28 A Jubilee is proclaimed in Macau despite the efforts of the Lord Patriarch to prevent it

On this same 20th October, one of our friends wrote that a Pontifical Jubilee had been sent in the Goa ship by the Cardinal and Apostolic Nuncio to Lisbon, to the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Macau, who published it to the faithful.[^p471-36] However, when the Lord Patriarch heard that it had been published, he was intent upon revoking the publication, or, as widespread rumour had it, revoking the Jubilee itself. With this in mind, <!-- PDF page 472 | book page 463 | manuscript page 1055 --> he ordered the Most Reverend Father Commissary of the Spanish Franciscans, Fray Bernardino, who has often appeared earlier in these pages, to think up some kind of notice of annulment and publish it. The Reverend Father did think something up, but he preceded it by a summary of the Jubilee itself, to which he then appended all the titles of the Lord Patriarch and stated that it was null on the grounds that the person who had previously published it was notoriously excommunicate. Then, so as not to deprive the Christian people of the such great indulgences, the Jubilee was finally and legitimately published by the Patriarch himself; 15 days were assigned for it, and, as for churches, it was determined that they should be the church of the Augustinian Fathers (previously placed under interdict by the Lord Bishop of Macau) and a chapel (of the same Augustinians) commonly known as the Hermitage of Our Lady of Penha, where three or four clerics were staying who had been expelled from China. However, before Fr. Bernardino went down to publish this amended notice as he had been asked to do, he had shown it to one of the Religious of his order to get his advice. The advice was that if he loved his freedom and did not want to be held as a prisoner in the riverside fort, he should not publish it but leave it up to the Patriarch to decide. That is what Fr. Bernardino did, and nothing more came of the new publication.

At the same time, we had news that in Macau they were still considering the instruction from Goa with a view to giving full | freedom to the Lord Patriarch. Most people seemed to be very favourably disposed towards it, but thought that it should be postponed for the moment on the grounds that the Chinese magistrates had sent thirty guards in small boats to keep watch on the Lord Patriarch’s palace. ### 32 The mandarin Henkama’s funeral

October 25th. The mandarin Henkama was buried, but His Majesty did not offer any posthumous tribute to him. He had known of his death around October 15th, and did indeed send his son home ordering that he should be told that he had gone to his rest. The Court was surprised that there was not even the slightest tribute to the virtues of this man to whom, when he was ill the previous year, he had shown signs of tender concern and whom he had restored to health. Indeed, he had openly praised his dedicated service, going out of his way to favour him above all the other courtiers, and placing a garland on a portrait of him which he had had painted as a perpetual memorial. His coldness about Henkama’s death is perhaps to be ascribed to the fact that the Emperor, the Crown Prince, and the whole imperial family were utterly distraught at this time.

### 33 Damaging rumours about the crime and punishment of the Crown Prince

October 27th. The court in Beijing was echoing with rumours both about the punishment of the Crown Prince and about his crime.[^p475-41] Opinions vary, depending on an individual’s personal interests, or on whether they are sorry for the misfortunes of someone who is in other respects a benevolent prince, or whether they had hoped that the firstborn royal son would be chosen instead of this prince, with consequent advantage for themselves. There were a few who looked at it from a higher standpoint and regretted that the Palace should have its peace disturbed, and were apprehensive that the unrest might spread to the people. The main charge against the Prince was a complete fiction, which was nevertheless widely believed simply because it was often repeated by so many people. The Firstborn Son’s faction painted a circumstantially convincing picture so that it was very difficult to be prudent and not jump <!-- PDF page 476 | book page 467 | manuscript page 1057 --> to conclusions. It was said that he had gone into the Emperor’s tent during the night, armed with a dagger, to assassinate his father; this rumour was so cleverly put about the camp that even our Fathers who were there had trouble not believing it.

### 34 Mistaken conjectures and dreams of Dr. Borghese about Fr. Pereira’s death etc

October 28th. When the Emperor was approaching the city, a messenger arrived with an order that none of the important people nor anyone from the Palace should go out of Beijing to meet His Majesty on the way, as was otherwise the custom. The Jesuits accompanying him in the imperial party had sent a letter on ahead giving exactly the same advice, although their private warning arrived only after the public one. I must not here omit to mention | Dr. Borghese’s mistake. He is the Lord Patriarch’s doctor, who has been staying with us since 1705. He saw that we were not going out to meet the Emperor as was customary, and inquired why. In good faith he was told that the Emperor had ordered word to be sent that nobody should go to meet him. The doctor took it that this prohibition applied only to the Jesuits, so at once he deployed all his mental forces to discover the reason for this. It did not occur to him to find out the truth by asking someone; he took the reason he thought up to be true, and noted it in his diary. Later, when at the end of December 1708 Fr. Tomás Pereira died, the Doctor put two and two together and dreamed up a cause of his death which he communicated in writing to the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing, more or less as follows: While in Tartary, Brother Paramino had incurred the indignation of the Emperor and at the Emperor’s order had to endure the punches of a eunuch for saying that he was unable to cure the Emperor’s little son, and indeed he did not. But the Emperor had extended his anger to Fr. Pereira because he had often recommended Brother Paramino as particularly expert in the art of surgery, and had got him to come from Macau to enter the Emperor’s service. In his anger the Emperor had forbidden Fr. Pereira to go to meet him. Fr. Pereira was deeply hurt by this turn of events, and went into a decline through melancholy until an attack of apoplexy finished him off! This whole story is nothing but a string of fantasies with no basis in fact, and no more needs refutation than the infinite number of other fictions strung together by the same mind. I thought it worth mentioning, however, because I knew that the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Beijing had already expressed his confidence in the doctor and had written so to others. Alas for us if we had to cope with the tongues and pens of others! We will be calumniated, but someone will expunge the calumnies and ensure that innocence triumphs in the end.

### 38 The Emperor slips back into Beijing with the Crown Prince laden with chains, etc

### 39 About Brother Domenico Paramino

I think I should add a supplement here which for good reason I did not mention under September nor in this month of October, concerning the fortunes—or misfortunes—of Brother Domenico Paramino. He was serving his fourth year as the imperial surgeon, at home but principally during journeys, because of the Emperor’s chronic chest problems which had flared up in 1707, when he had fallen seriously ill while engaged in hunting, and in this year, 1708, when he had a recurrence of the same illness during the same pursuit. On this occasion, however, it was much aggravated by the death of his son, who was hardly nine years old and was the Emperor’s favourite.[^p480-48] The Brother was not at fault, since the illness was outside his speciality. Different people give different versions of what happened. Since I was far away, I have collected the accounts of the three priests who at the time were in attendance with the Emperor. Their versions agree precisely on the key facts, though some had more and others less to say, depending on whether they had more or less about which they could be sure. To keep this account short, I will present only the one letter from Fr. Parrenin to me; not because the accounts of the other two priests, Fathers Castner and Liebstain, are not reliable, but because Fr. Parrenin was Brother Paramino’s interpreter from beginning to end, saw and heard everything in person, and himself played a large part in what happened. It runs as follows:

<!-- PDF page 481 | book page 472 | manuscript page 1060 -->

Reverend Father Kilian Stumpf,

I have got your letter asking me to give a written account of what happened to our dearest Brother Domenico Paramino in Tartary on the occasion of the death of the Emperor’s 18th son. To do as Your Reverence has requested, I shall relate in Latin the principal events, which I noted down at the time in French.

### 40 He is summoned by the Emperor to cure his little son who was ill

The little prince, nine years old, was his father’s favourite, and when he had been ill for several days and the Chinese doctors had tried every remedy they had with no success, they still did not dare to tell the Emperor that there was a danger that the child might die. On the contrary, when a consultation was set up with the head eunuchs, they constantly repeated that there was no danger of death. Eventually, however, the boy’s right knee gradually became red and swollen. This gave the doctors their chance to dissociate themselves from the case. They told the King (Emperor)[^p481-49] that the child was free from any internal illness; he had an inflamed and swollen knee, but that was the surgeons’ responsibility, not theirs. The eunuchs who were looking after the boy said the same thing. On this advice, the Emperor ordered the European surgeon to be sent for. So, on 27 September 1708, that is, the 15th day of the 8th month, at 7 pm, our dear Brother was sent for along with myself, so that if there was anything he did not quite understand I could interpret for him. The patient was sitting in a tent, supported in the arms of the eunuchs, who at once said to the Brother: “The young prince here has been ill for a long time. At length, however, thanks to the efforts of the doctors and the royal medicines, he has recovered. Since the King (Emperor) found this difficult to believe, he himself took the boy’s pulse and agreed with the doctors. The boy is cured; his knee is still swollen, though, which is why you have been sent for so that you can cure the swelling.” The Brother wanted to see and to examine the boy, and tried to put his finger into his mouth. But the boy himself turned his head away, and the eunuchs would not let the Brother carry on, saying “What is the point of annoying him? There is nothing else | wrong with him. Just tell us if there is any danger, and what is to be done.” “If there is nothing else wrong with him,” the Brother said, “other than the swelling and otherwise he is well, as you say, there is no danger. Just apply such and such an ointment exteriorly, and wash out his mouth with such and such a potion.” (I did not make a note of, nor do I recall, the names of the remedies.) This was reported to the Emperor, who said “Then do so.”

<!-- PDF page 482 | book page 473 -->

### 41 He pronounces the illness incurable

On the morning of the 16th day of the same moon, the Brother went in again. Examining the illness carefully in broad daylight, he came to the conclusion that there was nothing could be done for him. When he told me this in Portuguese, for me to inform the eunuchs, I impressed upon him that he should not say anything hastily, but should conduct a more careful examination. “There is no need for any further examination,” the Brother said, “He is terminally ill, and there is nothing anyone can do to save him. The boy is extremely weak, and has a fever; his tongue is dry, decay has begun to eat away at his jaw and inside his knee, and he is too weak to withstand any further treatment. The Chinese doctors are saying there is no internal illness, so that it should be in my hands that he dies.” I promptly translated this; I informed the pages and the Firstborn Son himself, who was in charge of the boy. But all that day there was nobody who was willing or had the courage to tell the King (Emperor). The Brother was not summoned in the evening, so we could not find out what was going on.

On the 17th in the very early morning, thinking his son was out of all danger, the King divided the camp. He left behind the queens, the doctors, the surgeons, and many other men with the child, at a place called Yonga pai anga. He took the huntsmen with him along with half of his army, and went about five leagues away intending to hunt on the other side of the mountains and then come back to the original camp a few days later. The eunuchs said goodbye to the King (Emperor) very hopeful for the health of his son, then they summoned the Brother once more. When he had visited the child, he said that his condition had worsened still further; whereupon the eunuchs began to fly into a violent rage. They heaped unfair accusations on Brother and threw him out of the camp compound, but very soon called him back to argue with him and question his judgement. “How do you know,” the head eunuch Liang[^p482-50] said, “that he has a fever? You are not a doctor! Are we to believe you, or rather the specialist doctors and indeed the King himself, who say the opposite? Whether he has a fever or not is not your concern. You’ve only got to cure the swelling.” “But that swelling cannot be cured while he still has a fever,” the Brother replied, “and when his whole body is so completely exhausted by fever.”

### 42 The argument with the Chinese doctors

Then the eunuch turned to the doctors. “You’ve got to refute this brute,” he said. “Just look at you!” they all cried at once. “Tell us then, you who presume to take his pulse, in which part of his body is the boy ill?” “I ask you,” Brother replied, “in which part of his body is he <!-- PDF page 483 | book page 474 | manuscript page 1061 --> not ill? I see no hope of him recovering, and I think you have recognized that perfectly well yourselves. I don’t know who is driving you to speak as you do, but I will not deceive the King.” In the end, after more words had been exchanged, the eunuch asked the Brother if he dared to put what he had said in writing and sign it, when the doctor had said there was no danger. “I will write at length,” the Brother replied, “and put my name to it. The other day you deceived me, saying that the boy was not suffering from internal illness, and not permitting me to examine him. That is why I said that the boy can be cured if there is nothing more wrong with him than the swelling.” And so, a report was written in which the sickness was explained, and the Brother added that he did not hold out any hope that he could be cured. At the same time the Chinese (doctors) wrote a second report in which they said that the boy was in no danger, and was free of all internal illness. These two reports were sent at about the tenth hour in the morning post-haste on horseback to the Emperor, who was at that moment engaged in hunting on the top of the mountain. We were both ordered to follow, so that someone would be available to answer the King’s questions. But the Brother, who had been unwell for a long time, was unable to go. |

The King read the Brother’s report first, and flushed with anger; at once, a mandarin, Zhang Charki,[^p483-51] proffered the doctors’ report, saying “Here is the opinion of our own people!” The Emperor read it, but it did not make him less angry; he was still worried, moving restlessly on his horse, looking in this and that direction; then suddenly he told the eunuch Wei Zhu and Zhang Charki to go back to the camp and closely question the surgeon and report back to him. (They did not tell the King that I was there because the Brother had not come.) We mounted our horses at once, and took the road past the camp going round the mountains. The Brother did not meet us, because he had wandered off the route we took, so we rode five leagues to the camp for nothing. Since the Brother was not there, we changed horses and once again set out back along the route we had already taken. After three leagues we did find Brother, on his way back and exhausted from the journey, half fainting from his illness; another eunuch, Zhang Qilin, followed closely after him, sent by the King with further orders which he would not disclose. But he did launch an unending stream of abuse upon the Brother, whose horse he ordered to be encouraged by a secretary with a whip to get a move on; it was in vain, however, for the wretched horse simply could not increase its pace. The eunuch was not prepared to put up with the delay, and went on ahead of us. It was completely dark when we reached <!-- PDF page 484 | book page 475 --> the eastern gate of the camp, where a eunuch and the important people in the middle of a large crowd were waiting for the Brother and sending horsemen every minute to hurry him along. Finally he arrived, about the first watch of the night. Both of us stood before the eunuch, encircled by the men as though we were in a court of law. When he started to speak, I asked whether he was speaking on his own account, or by command of the Emperor. “By command,” he replied. At once we both dropped to our knees. He, however, turned to the Brother and said, “On the 15th day of this moon you said this and that, on the 16th you said nothing, and on the 17th after the King had left you wrote these things, etc.?” The Brother repeated again the remarks I have written above on the days in question, along with what the head eunuch had said. He added that on the 16th in the morning and for the whole of the rest of that day nobody had been willing to convey anything at all to the King. “To whom did you give your advice?” asked the eunuch. “To this and that person,” the Brother replied, “those who had a special responsibility to advise the King.” So, when the Brother had given appropriate answers to all the questions, the Eunuch turned to someone else to carry out the planned punishment.

### 43 Paramino resents being insulted and beaten, since he simply persisted in telling the unwelcome truth

He added: “When, the day before yesterday, I myself called you at the command of the King, you did not at once get out of bed; you came reluctantly.” Hardly had he finished the last word that the Eunuch began to beat the Brother with his fists and to kick him, while telling me to stand back: “What’s it got to do with you?” he said: “It’s not your fault; all you can do is pass on the message that this criminal gives.” At that point I could not restrain my tears. “How can I put a good face on it if you treat my companion in this way when his only offence seems to have been to give you his honest opinion, as indeed is his duty; for we are bound to die rather than tell a lie!” He did not answer me; he just said to the Brother, “I am not beating you at the King’s command; I wouldn’t care even if the King were to beat me for doing so. But I certainly will beat the hide off you when that boy gets better.” Having said which, he went into the tent of the queens. The Brother, who had borne all this with the greatest patience, waited there with me until the fourth watch of the night, at which time the eunuch mounted his horse to leave and give an account of his journey to the King. What kind of account he gave, I have no idea.

### 44 The Emperor questions Paramino about the boy’s illness and the cure for it

On the 18th at nine o’clock in the morning, the King’s Eldest Son, who had been sent by his father, arrived, intending to convey more accurate news. We were not summoned. He went directly to the boy’s tent. The <!-- PDF page 485 | book page 476 | manuscript page 1062 --> eunuchs were afraid on their own account and tried to prevent him going in, giving as an excuse that the boy | was in the Queens’ enclosure. After a bit of an argument, the Prince went in. He knew of the danger, yet still did not dare to write the whole truth to his father, as he admitted to me. All he did was to arouse his suspicions and make his father more worried. As a result, on the 19th the King arrived with his entire army, and as soon as he saw his son, he began to be afraid. Acting as if the Brother was completely unharmed, he ordered us to be summoned into the first hall along with all the Chinese doctors and surgeons, and had the Prince inquire of Brother whether that blackness which was already evident on the surface of the boy’s knee could be cut out with a knife, or pierced with a needle? “That could be done,” Brother replied, “but nothing will come out but blood mixed with water.” “But have you no ointments which would hasten the healing?” “I do,” the Brother answered, “but they would cause pain in the process, which the prince would not be able to bear.” (The Brother wanted to avoid having to apply any medicine to a subject for whom there was no hope, in case he gave the Chinese any handle for saying that it was the European ointments which had caused the boy’s death.) When the King heard this answer, as the Brother was outlining it slowly, he said: “He has answered well and wisely.” It was about ten o’clock in the morning. We were taken back to our tents.

And there you are! After noon, we were once again summoned by the Crown Prince and the Firstborn Son, at the King’s order. Neither wished to allow us to reply on bended knees, as is customary. “We are here to consult you on a serious matter,” said the Crown Prince. “The King asks what can be done. Would it not help to make an incision with a knife?” The Brother replied that he had not seen the patient on the previous day, the 17th, so he could not say for sure whether it would have helped to make an incision or not. He was at once taken inside, and when he was brought back out again, he said: “If an incision is made, no matter at all will come out, and a poultice will be applied in vain.” “But the King asked what we should do. Today he is sick; will he be any better tomorrow if we do nothing?” The Brother replied, “If His Majesty wishes to try it, I shall willingly do so; but I could not do so without first saying what I really think.”

“The Chinese doctors say,” the King said, “that it would be good to make an incision, and I take a similar view: do not be afraid. It is on my authority that an incision is made on his knee with a knife. Let all the doctors and surgeons be brought in: but let the European put his hand to the task, since he has the greater dexterity.” It was done as the King commanded. The whole interior of the knee was obviously gangrenous, and his jaw similarly. Almost everyone found the stench unbearable. The dead pieces of flesh were removed and an

<!-- PDF page 486 | book page 477 | manuscript page 1063 -->

Egyptian ointment[^p486-52] was applied without causing the patient any pain. Then the King wished to go in to the boy’s tent. He was prevented by the Crown Prince, but the King insisted vehemently, asking whether there was any hope that he would live? The Chinese replied in the Chinese fashion, that there were five parts hope out of ten; but the Brother, trembling, said there was just a single such hope, etc, because the sick boy’s forces were spent.

On the morning of the 20th, the wound was opened so that it might be cleaned, but despite the Egyptian ointment the decay had spread further. When the Crown Prince and the Eldest Son saw that he was in no better condition, they said to the King, “There is no change since yesterday. But the boy is very weak.” Then the King ordered the Brother to be told that he did realise that it was all over with his son, but that “if you can do any good for him, I shall be very grateful indeed, etc.”

### 45 The courtiers act as if there is no danger

On the 21st in the presence of the same people, the wound was once more inspected by the Brother, who had lost any hope of recovery and in whose judgement there was nothing more to be done. On being asked, he did not dare to say more, but when the King, through many pages, vehemently pressed for an answer, which nobody was giving him, the Eldest Son, bursting into tears, said that it was all over. The King | was so angry at this that he had him subjected to many indignities and expelled from the imperial camp, saying that he was totally heartless, a man who had no love for his brothers. The result was that everyone was even more afraid to say anything to him about the danger of death. From that day on, Brother Paramino was not brought in again to try to do anything more for the boy.

So, to draw this briefly to a close, I shall leave out many things which I have often spoken about and which add little that is relevant. For what is the point of saying how many surgeons were summoned from the capital as fast as horses could carry them who, as soon as they saw him, as the Crown Prince forced them to do, said that there was more room for hope than for fear? They were rewarded by the King, who not merely ordered them to tell the important people so, but also sent people to stand by the roadside on the way to Beijing to warn the passers-by not to bring any bad news to the court.

On the 24th, the rumour spread that the beloved son was restored to health; the King was overjoyed, and, to make the Brother ashamed of himself, he <!-- PDF page 487 | book page 478 | manuscript page 1064 --> ordered him secretly to look from a corner and see the child sitting and eating, surrounded by the Chinese doctors and the eunuchs singing in triumph. They insulted the Brother, asking him if he was still despairing, and demanding a reply. “Be careful,” said the Crown Prince, “not to say anything bad.” The Brother, very much afraid that he would be killed, a threat which he thought was imminent, said that he was better, and coming outside afterwards said to me: “The child is sitting and eating, but he will soon die, he’ll go out like a candle. But please do not say so: they want to kill me!” Nevertheless, I replied (on Brother’s behalf) that he was better, since the inflammation is reduced; I said nothing about the other symptoms, and I was evasive and changed the subject. That same day, the King moved camp about 10 stadia away;[^p487-53] he was the first to leave, together with the queens, and a little later the child appeared, carried in a chair. The Crown Prince went ahead of the chair on foot, and among so many thousands of men who followed them there was not one, no matter what his status, who rode on a horse. It was forbidden, because the lamas (the Tartar popes) had said that the urine and dung of the horses would harm the child.

### 46 The sick boy dies

During all that day the Chinese doctors were in constant attendance on the child, while we were sent away from the royal enclosures in which we had previously stayed day and night. A little woman was brought in, said to be an extremely expert surgeon, to see if she could effect a cure for the patient. There was nothing she did not promise, and she accepted many rewards from the queens. But many believed that child died on that very day, or the day after. It is still disputed when he did actually die. However, that may be, the funeral preparations were made and the bier prepared, and eventually on the 4th day of the 9th moon, October 17th, the death of the child was announced, and on that very day in the afternoon, the Crown Prince was deposed, taken captive, and bound with iron fetters. Many of his pages were killed, as Your Reverence knows, and in all this commotion the Chinese doctors, who otherwise would have paid the just penalty for their temerity, made their escape. A few days later, the First Minister of the Empire met me: “You have won,” he said “and it is alright for you to be seen in public. But why are the Chinese doctors still being fed?”

I have nothing more to say. This agrees with the original. I so witness. |
