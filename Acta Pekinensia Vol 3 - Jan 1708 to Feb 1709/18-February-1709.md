February 1st. The Rev. Fr. Appiani wrote a very short note to Fr. Kilian asking for some meditations on the Passion of the Lord. But Fr. Kilian happened to know that the aforenamed Father had asked for something similar from another priest, and since, on the same day, Fr. Pierre Jartoux had sent to Fr. Kilian some poems of Father ….,[^p601-1] among which were selections on the suffering of Christ the Lord, Father Kilian thought that these had been sent so that they might be given to Father Appiani, and so he handed them over; this was done through error and was of little importance. Once he knew his mistake, he sent along other meditations with a note, which I will refer to under March 27th, the same day on which will be found the response of Fr. Appiani.

### 1 Father Visdelou comes to Macau and is consecrated Bishop of Claudiopolis

On the same day (the first of the month), Rev. Fr. Visdelou disembarked from an English ship, which moored within sight of the city of Macau one whole night and almost one whole day. He entered the city and on February 2nd was consecrated Bishop of Claudiopolis by the Lord Patriarch in the residence of the said Lord Patriarch of Antioch, and on the same day reboarded the ship, and no person opposed or interfered with him in any way. On the same day, Fr. Agostino Capelli went aboard the same ship, since he was to go to the Madura mission, as was said above in the month of January. But Father Cordero who had come from Canton remained in Macau, as he was to go to Manila to direct the new seminary, which it is said has been established there. These matters are covered in the letters of others which will be made known throughout the course of this month.

February 3rd. Father Kilian wrote to the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon asking that he should send back the letter which Father Kilian had sent him at the beginning of January. This letter, however, was not sent until the end of February, when after the seals have been opened the public couriers begin to operate.

### 2 The Crown Prince comes to Beijing; likewise, the Emperor

February 5th. The Crown Prince came from the Villa to the city and went now for the first time since his recent deposition to his own palace where he will henceforth <!-- PDF page 602 | book page 593 --> dwell, just as previously, as Crown Prince. Concerning that point, if any European with little experience should need to take counsel on Chinese affairs, a lot would have to be written, and maybe even illustrated, but that is not appropriate under our religious statutes.[^p602-2] For the reader let it suffice to know that within the walls, or, as I may more correctly say, within the fortifications of the Palace of Beijing, there are many houses which together are sufficient to constitute a good-sized city. Within these is the palace which is called the Yuqing gong,[^p602-3] said to be a place for the residence and use of the princes who are declared to be successors to the throne, on the eastern side touching where the Emperor normally dwells with his women.

February 6th. The Emperor entered the city to make preparations as a preamble to the coming solemnity of the New Year. His Majesty was sick in body still, and so from the gate of the orchard of his villa all the way to the northern gate of the Palace, a way had been made strewn with soft earth so that the wagon would not shake at all. The preparation of such a road was once common among Chinese Emperors, though more rare among the Tartars, and rarest for the present emperor Kangxi, whose bodily strength was equal to the labours of Hercules and who outdid him in travels, and who does not care at all about conveniences of this sort when travelling by road. Going into the city the Emperor made a diversion to the new palace which he had built for his fourteenth son;[^p602-4] it seemed to him to be rather confined, and so he ordered the neighbouring houses on both sides to be bought, so that more space was opened up.

February 7th. In the Palace we inquired after the health of the Emperor and at evening we were sent a New Year’s gift, which according to custom consisted of fish, venison, pheasants, etc.

February 8th. We gave thanks for the New Year’s gift. In these days the atmosphere in the Palace was cooler than usual, and no one dared speak either about any business or about the person of the Emperor or the Prince.

### 3 Chinese New Year

February 10th. The Chinese New Year began. The mandarins were gathered together according to custom to hold the ceremony of <!-- PDF page 603 | book page 594 | manuscript page 1126 --> adoration[^p603-5] of His Majesty. But the ceremony was dispensed with because the Emperor did not appear | in public. The cause was believed to be that the Crown Prince had not yet been publicly restored.

12 February. The Reverend Fathers Diego de Santa Rosa and Juan Fernández [Serrano] wrote to Father Visitor from Ji’nan zhou, not only thanking him graciously but also informing him about the persecution, about which Father Serrano had given his own testimony in his letter of January; they informed him that they had sent to Father Kilian a narrative of what had occurred when both received the permit in Beijing, and that the narrative was quite accurate.

Here follows the letter of Father Diego:

Most Reverend Father Visitor, the Peace of Christ. I have read the letter that Your Reverence has been good enough to write to Fr. In this brief account of ours I can do no more than inform you of the situation and what I think about it as it affects me alone, and in regard to this I would be very grateful if Your Reverence could use your services to pass on this news a quickly as possible to Guangzhou so that it can be quickly sent on to Manila. Note, in the first place, that the thanks which Father Diego speaks of have already been recorded by me under the month of December, when they were written, although they actually arrived very late: this year was unusual in that many letters from the neighbouring province of Shandong were brought very slowly to us at court. Secondly, note that Father Diego, when he wrote this, had already accepted the office of Provincial Commissary, of which office the patent letters came into his hands not long after he had departed from Beijing.

### 7 The journey of the Emperor from the city to his villa

On the same 13th day of February, the Emperor returned to his villa. It rained that day in the city, and it snowed in the nearby mountains; His Majesty was unwilling to order the road to be prepared, and so he proceeded on horseback, not by wagon, holding in contempt the mud and the light rain. Another unusual thing was that the Emperor forbade the streets of the city to be closed, which otherwise was always done to hold back the people, and he wanted no one to leave the streets; to that end, a great part of the guard had been dismissed, and he had only six eunuchs to clear the way. Mildly | and without shouting the eunuchs warned whoever was in the way that the Emperor was near, more that they might see him on horseback than that they might get out of the road because of fear. It should be known here that it is a perpetual custom of the Chinese that as often as and whenever the Emperor goes through the city, from the Palace to another place, or all the way up to the gate of the city by which he is going to leave, all of the side streets are closed off by setting up double screens at intervals far enough from each other that neither foot nor eye of men may even enter the cross-streets the Emperor passes. The great row of houses remains so <!-- PDF page 607 | book page 598 --> empty that not even a dog can be found in them. The soldiery alone on foot keeps watch at the many gates of the streets, and the officers stand ready at intervals on the main road. The reason why the Emperor on this occasion did not want to observe this custom and instead showed himself to those he met so they could see him seemed to be this: a rather grave report about the Emperor’s sickness had circulated because he did not appear in public on New Year’s Day. Hence, some assumed that the Emperor wished to show both that he was well by riding on horseback under a rainy sky and through the mud and that he was of a cheerful mind by allowing the people he met with to see him.

### 8 The letters of the Captain General of Macau were not accepted by the Bishop of Beijing

February 15th.[^p607-16] Fr. Girolamo Franchi from the mission of Jiaoxian[^p607-17] indicated that the Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing had not accepted the letter of the Captain General of Macau. These are the words of the Father: “The Most Illustrious Bishop of Beijing sent back to me the letter of the Captain General of Macau completely untouched.” On this point, it ought to be noted that this letter of the Captain General was different from that about which mention was made in January. This one, however, had been sent to Father Franchi, that he might hand them over personally or send them on since he was said to be about to go into Linqing on mission business. But both letters had been sent before the first response of the Lord Bishop written on January the …18 had arrived for Father Soares.

On the same day, February 15th, the Most Illustrious Lord Bishop of Ascalon[^p607-19] wrote to Fr. Kilian about the consecration of Fr. Visdelou, as also about the annoyances which His Person suffered, even from his own religious order, but it is helpful to lay these things out according to the original. It goes like this:

### 9 The Bishop of Ascalon is sent to Macau

Last month, I wrote to Your Paternity, and to those letters which I had then received I gave a response: now I have received another dated December 13th … I wrote how I was then relegated to Macau according to the final decree through the Libu, and here I remain, received very well by the Portuguese but held in contempt and forsaken by <!-- PDF page 608 | book page 599 | manuscript page 1129 --> those missionaries who follow the Lord Patriarch, even those of my own order; but if God be for us, etc.[^p608-20] I await the opportunity to go to Manila in April, and from there, perhaps, to Europe, if my health and circumstances are favourable. Reverend Father Pro-Vicar Fernández has not written to me up until now, so any news about him will be very pleasing to me.[^p608-21]

### 10 On the appeal of the Franciscans

(Then he discusses his appeal and that of the Society, to which this response was given:) “It is not allowed: they are to know that they are perjured and excommunicated.” This response was not news to me, but even very much hoped for. Father Fernández did not understand why I felt this way. I am not amazed that the Reverend Fathers did not appeal, because I think that they, aggravated with difficulties over their own defence, have abandoned everything, since neither the investigations they sought for were allowed nor even their procurators admitted. What I am amazed at is that, without first appealing, they wished to pursue the case further here or in Rome, which is a thing foreign to the law. The Fathers should have pressed hard to good effect in their own defence before the sentence was given, and after it they ought to have appealed; then, would the way have been open for excusing themselves.[^p608-22] Now the way is not open, except after the Acts of the Visitation have gone to Rome, and then complete restitution ought to be sought, because the money is the Church’s, and in no other way will the case be brought forward anew.[^p608-23] In addition, in approving an appeal, or not, it is certain that the Lord Patriarch was not going to give attention to these matters, for he has admitted nothing as yet, nor has he attempted to refute the charges against him, nor has he published Acts, nor testimony, | but he does everything de facto, nothing de iure …

### 11 On the consecration of Father Visdelou: whether it is valid

The Most Illustrious Visdelou was consecrated on the second of this month by the Lord Patriarch. I, however, am doubtful whether he received the sacred character for the reasons I gave to Your Paternity and to the Father Visitor, since consecration by only one bishop is invalid, unless there is a dispensation from the Supreme Pontiff, just as is consecration by a simple priest. I commend myself greatly to Your Paternity and to the prayers of the Order.

At Macau, 15 February 1709. Your Paternity’s most devoted servant, Fray Alvaro, Bishop of Ascalon.

That this letter agrees with the original,[^p609-24] I so witness.

### 12 The alkermes mixture is sent to the Emperor

February 15th. Father José Monteiro sent letters from Guangzhou to Fathers Soares and Kilian to notify them that he had sent a little of the mixture called alkermes for delivery by way of the Supreme Prefect of Arms[^p609-25] according to a mandate of the Emperor given on January 9th which arrived in Guangzhou on February 7th.

Fr. Miguel Fernández when he was questioned by the Lord Patriarch about whether Fr. Franchi in Ji’nan fu had called all the Christians to the church and read to them the Declaration of the Emperor to lead them away from the observance of the decree of the Patriarch. On this point, he has given honourable testimony on behalf of

Father Franchi, and that testimony has been sent to Rome as the official version. Indeed, in this case, explanation is necessary, for rumour earlier had it that testimony was got from [the friar] not without skill, and that among the Acts of the Legation the testimony was recorded against Father Franchi in the same case. ### 14 On Fr. Antonio da Silva: whether he ought to lay aside the apostolic vicariate

Also on February 16th, Fr. Manuel Mendes[^p610-27] wrote to the Fr. Visitor discussing the difficulties pressing hard on Fr. Antonio da Silva: on the one hand, the Lord Patriarch threatens excommunication if he should give up the apostolic vicariate; on the other hand, the Lord Archbishop of Goa, the Primate of the Indies, threatens the same punishment unless he lay aside the vicariate in a diocese belonging to the Portuguese padroado. His own inclination is to the view that the Vice-Provincial should not at the moment relieve the said Fr. Antonio da Silva of the burden lest he expose himself and the Society to the most serious troubles, since it is certain that the Lord Patriarch is not going to acquiesce for any reason but is going to proceed by way of deeds.[^p610-28]

### 15 A letter of Fr. Giovanni Battista di Serravalle to Father Visitor

February 17th. Fr. Giovanni Battista di Serravalle, an Italian Franciscan, wrote letters of condolence to Father Visitor on the death of Fr. Pereira, in words few but distinguished commending the departed and at the end lamenting the loss of a support that was necessary.

This is his letter, as follows:

For a long time, I have wanted to write to Your Reverence to congratulate you on being elected Visitor, and so as a consequence the common father of all the missionaries of China. But whether my natural laziness in writing to my <!-- PDF page 611 | book page 602 | manuscript page 1130 --> friends or a certain kind of fear arising from the drastic changes that we see in this poor mission | have held my pen back, just the other day my sense of obligation returned to me on seeing Your Reverence’s name on the sad news of the death of the late Very Reverend Father Pereira, whose soul we have every reason to expect is already enjoying the reward of his apostolic endeavours. I have celebrated the prescribed masses and this morning we had a solemn funeral mass in our crypt here in the Nantang asking for prayers for the soul of the deceased by the neophytes who were present.

### 16 He grieves over the death of Fr. Pereira

The China mission is losing its best workers and it seems as if the Lord God is angry, but I don’t know whether it is against the Europeans or the Chinese. May God assist you with his holy grace and grant you the peace you long for so that without discord and jealousy we may all be of “one heart and one soul,”[^p611-29] and work in this vineyard solely for the glory of the Creator and the salvation of souls redeemed by the most precious blood of Jesus Christ.

### 17 He seeks some monetary support

I would have begged Your Paternity to give me any news you have if only I could imagine it might be good, but since the little that arrives here is all sad, I will not go to any lengths to request it. But when you come across any good news, please share it with me. In particular, let me know if you have news of my salary, since up until now I have not received from Europe a single real. So far as I know the other missionaries of the Sacred Congregation have all received their annual payments on time.[^p611-30] I have frequently sent my just complaints to the Lord Patriarch who I presume is still in Macau. In case he persists in denying me my due subsidy, and our Pro- Vicar Fr. Antonio[^p611-31] cannot continue to pay my expenses, I will be obliged by <!-- PDF page 612 | book page 603 | manuscript page 1131 --> necessity either to turn to Your Reverence for assistance, or get permission to return to Milan, the capital[^p612-32] of Lombardy, where I would be better off than in China. I beg Your Reverence to pardon my negligence, and convey my humble respects and reverence to you, as does the Pro-Vicar, and recommend myself to your prayers and sacrifices, as also to all the Fathers of the Court whom I revere with all my heart.

Xi’an fu in Shaanxi, 17 February 1709. Your Reverence’s most humble and indebted servant and compatriot,[^p612-33] Fray G.B. di Serravalle of the Order of Minors.

That this letter agrees with the original, I so witness.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary

Note: I give the latter part of the letter, not to complain about the parsimony of the Lord Patriarch towards Fr. Serravalle, but purely because such is what the said Franciscan Father writes, and the writer is responsible for its truth. If, however, his words in this letter are closely examined, the Franciscan Serravalle is not complaining that the Lord Patriarch did not give him anything but that he received nothing from Europe. This, however, may have happened for many reasons, without anyone’s fault. And furthermore, Fr. Serravalle does not seem seriously to seek support from our Fr. Visitor; for, when it is said that thus far he has neglected to write, he is at the same time implying that there had been no need for him to seek support.

### 18 The reason for a letter of Fr. Kilian to Fr. Juan Fernández

February 18th. Father Kilian wrote to the Most Rev. Fr. Juan Fernández Serrano, who was then on a journey and staying in Ji’ning. The occasion of writing was a bundle of letters from Manila sent to Beijing because those in Guangzhou assumed that six Franciscan Fathers, who had departed thence in October, had arrived at court. At that time in Beijing, however, it was not known where those Reverend Fathers had been detained, and it seemed entirely necessary to find that out by every good means, so that their Manila chapters might know and they might agree with the determination of their superiors before receipt of the document, and so that, having received notice about the views of their superiors, they might choose what their superiors thought more suitable and useful to the mission, | and so free themselves from troubles and us from suspicions, <!-- PDF page 613 | book page 604 --> which some had been able to produce regarding these points, as if the Franciscan Fathers had been led into seeking the document at the persuasions of the Jesuits in Beijing. The letter of Father Kilian goes as follows:

### 19 The letter of Fr. Kilian to Fr. Juan Fernández

Most Reverend Father in Christ, The day before the Chinese post closed for the New Year, letters from Manila came to Guangzhou for certain Most Reverend Fathers of the Seraphic Order,[^p613-34] who a little while ago set out on a journey toward Beijing. Since we have nothing but unclear reports, some writing that they are in Nanjing, others that they are in Huai’an, others that they are in Yangzhou for the winter, it ought to be assumed, not without good reason, that what is contained in these letters is important for them to know. It seemed best to Rev. Fr. Visitor and to the Vice-Provincial, with the agreement of the bearer who brought them after we had paid him, to send all those letters to meet them so that, as soon as possible, they might learn everything they needed to know. Since, as I said, I did not know whether they were coming by boat or wagon, I decided to send letters to Ji’ning zhou because whether they came by river or by road, they ought to pass through it; in this way they would get the information they wanted from Manila. The bearer of these letters will depart tomorrow, 19 February, and will hardly arrive in Ji’ning zhou in less than nine days; if, however, when the bearer arrives, the Reverend Fathers from Guangzhou have not yet come, Your Most Reverend Paternity will be able to take care of the letters and either move them further along to meet them or keep them to await the arrival of the Reverend Fathers. The Manila ship brought very little news to us, which I share with you on the word of the authors who wrote from there.

### 20 Rumours about the cardinalate of the Lord Patriarch

First, they say the Lord Patriarch was made Cardinal of Santa Sabina.[^p613-35] There is no proof of this, except that someone from Mexico wrote to the Lord Patriarch with the title “Your Eminence.” Against this is that the Patriarch’s supporters in Guangzhou and Macau say that nothing authoritative on this point has come and so the Lord Patriarch is unwilling to take the title “Your Eminence.” In addition, the people in Guangzhou point out that the cardinal of Santa Sabina is Don Portocarrero: one ought to agree that this man is dead if someone else succeeded <!-- PDF page 614 | book page 605 --> in his title, but nothing is known as yet in Manila about the death of His Eminence.[^p614-36] Second, one man indicated that the Religious in the whole West have not received a pontifical brief on the subjection to be offered to ordinaries, but representations against it have been made to the Catholic King and to the Supreme Pontiff, and also to the bishops, to show how much damage, spiritual and temporal, to the Holy See and the Spanish crown such a subjection would cause if it were to be imposed.[^p614-37]

### 21 Other news from Manila

Third, they reported that the ship of Don Miguel de Castro, which he captained on its way from the port of Guangzhou to Peru, while still near the port had captured and despoiled a Chinese suma (a kind of ship) that was coming from Siam, and then set sail for Peru. But after being caught in a storm he had landed at a certain island subject to Japan, where he halted for seven days repairing the ship in whatever way possible, and afterwards he again renewed his course towards his goal of Peru.[^p614-38] But the crew protested and under duress he changed course for Manila, which he was not able to reach since the ship was leaking. He was forced to put into Cacajao[^p614-39] on 1 August. Everything was taken out of the ship, even the very riggings. The evacuation of the ship was finished on 28 August, and everything was stored away inside the house of the Dominican Fathers. On the following night the house and the church burned down together with all the goods from the ship. The result was, they write, that the Don de Castro and the supercargoes for the French merchants saved nothing from the fire but their skins.

Guigue in Nan’an, summoned to the side of a dying man, compelled that man to hand over his ancestor tablet under pain of being denied the sacraments, and that tablet was burned in the entrance of the church. I commend myself to Your Reverence’s most holy sacrifices. Beijing, 18 February 1709.

That this letter agrees with the original, I so witness.

### 22 The Bishop of Ascalon, abandoned by his own Augustinians, is harassed by the Lord Patriarch

February 20th. Father João Mourão, writing from Macau to Father Visitor, in a few words explained the difficulties with which the Most Illustrious Bishop of Ascalon found himself encumbered, and promised that, in every way he could, he would have taken care of what would be advantageous to the Most Illustrious Lord. These are the words of the Father: “As for the good treatment of the Bishop of Ascalon, Your Reverence can be at ease since I am taking special care of the matter, which he is very pleased to see, in case he does not embark.”[^p615-40] Whether it is a fact that this gentleman so deserving of praise has been abandoned in his old age and sickness by his Augustinian brothers I am unable to say for certain. I do not dare to rely on mere conjecture, but I can report some things that happened in the days that followed so that certain things can be deduced with a degree of certainty from the actions of one or other of the Augustinians in opposition to the Jesuits.

This is what Fr. Manuel da Souza wrote from Guangzhou on February 25th. The Lord Bishop of Ascalon leaves at the end of March for Manila. He has been well received by the Captain General and most of the people of Macau because he has behaved well and politely. ### 24 The relief provided by the Jesuits to the Bishop of Ascalon when he was harassed by his own people

From these words I want to draw the reader’s attention to the fact that the reason the Lord Bishop of Ascalon was abandoned seems to have been that no one now dared to associate with him unless they wanted to dissociate themselves from the Lord Patriarch. ### 25 Censure of the Franciscans intimated by the Lord Patriarch

### 27 On the declaration of the Emperor concerning the Chinese Rites made in the year 1700

On 26 February again. Throughout the course of this month, it was confirmed by the testimony of several people that Father Carlo Amiani, who was gone now more than two years from the mission, wearied himself <!-- PDF page 619 | book page 610 | manuscript page 1134 --> and others with his anxieties regarding what happened at the time of the visitation of the Lord Patriarch or the force of his decrees, and Father Kilian wished | to comfort him with a friendly letter and to inform him correctly about the matter. The letter was returned from Macau to Beijing because of an error, and sent a second time from there; it came without doubt later than either of the Fathers wanted, as will be seen from Father Amiani’s response in the month of …51

In addition, the original itself has not yet been sent back by Father Amiani, as Father Kilian asked; the cause of delay is not known. Nevertheless, it seems that the postscript of that letter should by no means be omitted here, in which the heart of the information is contained, and the matter of the Lord Patriarch is set forth very clearly and in detail. Therefore, let the reader have here in its proper place the postscript, until the letter happens to return, and with its response can be entered under the appropriate month.

Postscript. When I read over my letter, I became aware that I had been too brief in explaining the business of the Lord Patriarch, since I supposed it was known equally well to Your Reverence and to me. If however Your Reverence remains in some doubt over any aspect, let it be known to you that the whole case consists in this: that when the Lord Patriarch was asked by the Emperor what kind of reception the declaration on the Chinese Rites given by His Majesty in the year 1700 had received in Europe, he replied that the case of the rites has not been fully laid out by us, and Rome was amazed at the audacity of the Jesuits, because it did not pertain to them to determine or take care of such matters. The Emperor in his wisdom deduced from this that his own account of the Rites was held by Europeans to be insufficient, yet its purpose had been to fully inform people about the facts of the case. Since this insufficiency was thought to be based on this, namely that the problems about the Rites were not fully presented, nor were they expounded by those who ought to have done so. This is what the Lord Patriarch said. The Emperor prudently decided to remedy this, so he ordered that the Lord Patriarch himself should present the case, and make it fully, as he thought it should have been presented. In addition, we petitioned the Lord Patriarch that, if he saw that we somewhere for whatever reason had not adequately presented the doubtful points before the Emperor, he should deign to put down in writing what and how matters regarding the Chinese Rites ought to be presented before the Emperor. But the Lord Patriarch did not listen to us; he put the Emperor off to another time, when, as he said, after recovering from his illness he would be feeling better.

<!-- PDF page 620 | book page 611 | manuscript page 1135 -->

In the meantime, he wished the legation to depart for the Supreme Pontiff, without the Emperor committing to writing anything concerning the Rites for announcement to the Supreme Pontiff, thus suggesting that the Emperor had accepted something on the authority of the Lord Patriarch.[^p620-52]

### 28 How the Emperor dealt with the Lord Patriarch and he in turn with the Emperor

This went down badly with the Emperor. Still, he concealed it, as kings usually do. Then, after five months in which the Patriarch was quite often warned by the Emperor that he should do what he was sent by the Supreme Pontiff to do, and that concerning the Chinese Rites he should not pay attention to the remarks of the half-literate, still less the opinions of the mob, but should drink in the understanding of these things from His Majesty as from a spring, the Patriarch made the excuse that he could neither speak nor understand Chinese but had waited so many months until His Lordship Charles Maigrot should arrive, summoned by himself, in rank a bishop, blameless in lifestyle, exceptional in European and Chinese learning, who in his place would satisfy the Emperor, and although very humble and reverent, would nevertheless freely disclose his opinions concerning the things which are disputed by the missionaries. The Emperor joyfully accepted this, but he was very much offended when after three days he read the statement of the Bishop of Conon, in which the Bishop presented many propositions that had nothing to do with the matters in dispute, and he became even more filled with anger when the Bishop of Conon, frequently ordered to expound his doubts concerning the Rites controversies, continually refused to expound them and with the Patriarch’s full knowledge and consent chose rather to confess in writing that he was too ignorant to expound those points, whose exposition he knew pertained by an indisputable right and incontrovertibly to His Majesty. The Emperor, however, lest he seem to judge more in the heat of rage than by the weight of justice and truth, | summoned the Bishop of Conon into Tartary, together with his advisors and ours, so that in his presence he might hear the Bishop discussing the Chinese Rites and he might instruct him even against his will.

### 29 How the Bishop of Conon conducted himself with the Emperor

The Lord Patriarch, intent on undermining in every way possible this proposal of the Emperor, in word and in writing now forbade anyone from disputing matters pertaining to our holy religion in the presence of the

<!-- PDF page 621 | book page 612 -->

Emperor, although no one, thank goodness, was being asked to dispute about such things. The Emperor simply intended that those who rejected his earlier explanation of the Chinese rites and terms, should now be given an explanation so full and adequate that neither party should remain in doubt about anything regarding the views of the Chinese people and the Empire, whether it agreed with the Christian religion or not. Therefore, Your Reverence can see now at last the third offense given to His Majesty by the Lord Patriarch. But ones ever more serious followed, when the Emperor in person saw the Lord Bishop of Conon and he himself experienced with his own five senses that this gentleman was so ignorant of Chinese matters that he was neither able to read Chinese books without European notes made by others, nor to understand without an interpreter what he heard read. The Emperor considered that he ought to be otherwise, and he thought that he might teach the ignorant Bishop of Conon and the Lord Patriarch, who was going astray because of the ignorance of the Bishop of Conon. And so he took up his brush and wrote out an explanation with his own hand, and sent it to the Lord Patriarch so that he might send it off to the Holy See. The Lord Patriarch with an obdurate mind objected to this, saying he would rather die than permit that God be called by the unworthy name of Tian (Heaven). He closed his eyes and bowed himself to the ground before the imperial intermediaries and said that he was not willing to hear anything else or respond further.

### 30 The Emperor’s marvelous moderation and prudence

Whence, the Emperor seeing that his authority was being challenged, and that he was being denied the right to explain the rites and ancestral terms of his own Chinese Empire, not without stupendous moderation of spirit in the most absolute monarchy of the gentile world, sent back the whole matter to the Supreme Pontiff and sent legates to appeal against the Pontifical Legate. He claimed no more than that according to the law of nations he should be heard on the interpretation of these matters within his own empire to ensure the peace of his subjects, and in addition he left completely to the judgment of the Supreme Pontiff whether the interpretation within the Chinese Empire is compatible with the Christian religion or not. The Emperor seemed to be going to keep quiet about these matters until he received the response from Rome. It happened, however, that Fr. Guéty with incredible foolishness babbled about many things and stirred up feelings against the Jesuits, and from these things new suspicions arose.

### 31 His just indignation

Hence, Fr. Guéty himself, the Lord Bishop of Conon, Fr. Mezzafalce, the Fujian literati, etc. were interrogated, and when the Emperor <!-- PDF page 622 | book page 613 | manuscript page 1136 --> learned from their confessions that there were many missionaries who even after the explanation of His Majesty and neglecting the appeal he had made to the Supreme Pontiff still stuck to the erroneous teaching of the Bishop of Conon on the Chinese Rites, which the Lord Patriarch wanted to sustain with his authority, only then the Emperor went on to more severe measures. He threw out the three aforementioned gentlemen and ordered everyone exiled who did this injury to his authority by preferring the ignorance of the Bishop of Conon about the Chinese rites and terms to his own certain knowledge of them, and refused to defer to him while awaiting a decision of the Supreme Pontiff as to whether these things are compatible with the Christian religion or not. He proclaimed that all missionaries must submit to be examined on this point, and that unless they received a permit, they would be expelled. So then, my Father, you see how scene followed upon scene. Now Your Reverence will see the final act of this tragedy.

From the decree of the Emperor and the exile of their allies, our adversaries understood that if Rome acted on their information and that of their allies when the explanation of the Emperor was already public knowledge, and was beyond any possible doubt, likewise the danger of the mission which hitherto they had denied was equally public; so, in order to bind the Supreme Pontiff, they relied on the authority of the Legate, and brought it about that he held in contempt | the appeal of the Emperor, and on his own authority decided directly against the explanation of the Emperor and the correct understanding of the Chinese rites and terms even going beyond those which hitherto were controversial, and he assigned the palm to the Lord Bishop of Conon.

### 32 The behaviour of the Lord Patriarch deservedly reprehended for many reasons

Many things in this decision of the Patriarch were offensive to the Emperor: firstly, the contempt for His Majesty explaining the rites and terminology of his own empire. Secondly, the rejection of a legitimate appeal, not the kind that the law allows to those of lower rank but the kind that kings with authority are accustomed to make. Thirdly, the abuse of the power of a commissary, when the Commissary wanted to decide the point in his own words, not those of the one who commissioned him. Fourthly, the suspect claim of a pontifical decree; if this was to hand, why did the Commissary not produce it? Did he prefer to oppose the supreme lord in this empire on his own authority rather than on that of his own supreme lord? Fifthly, the gratuitous amplification of power not only in prohibiting but even in condemning not only the rites in contention up to this point but also those which had never come into question, which were irrelevant to the mission but useful only for gaining the palm of victory for the Lord Bishop of Conon and angering the Emperor. Sixthly, the <!-- PDF page 623 | book page 614 --> contradicting of the Emperor to his face, which was required of all missionaries, which, seventhly, incurred penalties greater than mortal death by adding a clause that the missionaries were bound to believe the Lord Patriarch against the Emperor even in the understanding of Chinese rites and terms.

The Emperor went through these points finally, not to inflict a penalty or prevent further discussion, but rather simply to coerce those abusing the power of the Lord Patriarch, and he ordered him to be held at Macau until a response came from the Supreme Pontiff. From all this, Your Reverence sees clearly that the detention of the Lord Patriarch came about by not deferring to the appeal of the Emperor. Against this, if Your Reverence tells me that the Most Excellent Lord Patriarch thinks that he did everything lawfully and we ought to uphold his rights and liberty, then I ask what can we do against an Emperor who has appealed lawfully and considers that by the custom of kings he himself has legitimate power to pursue his appeal against those who illegitimately impede it?

I thought it would take just a few closing words to conclude my letter and just see how it has run away with my pen!

So went the postscript of the letter of Father Kilian to Father Carlo Amiani.

That this postscript agrees with the original, I so testify.

Fr. Kilian Stumpf, sj, Apostolic Notary
